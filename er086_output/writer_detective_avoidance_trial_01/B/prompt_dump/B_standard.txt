# “We Won’t Attack”—Does That Explanation Include Refueling?

“We won’t attack.” If so, their role must be that of a “shield.” But the support for Saudi Arabia announced by the UK has more to it than that one phrase can explain. It involves more than fighter jets. It also includes support for refueling in the air, passing fuel from one aircraft to another.

First, what happened in Saudi skies? A spokesperson for the Saudi-led coalition in Yemen announced that it had intercepted and destroyed a ballistic missile launched by the Houthis toward Riyadh.

In response to these threats, the UK Ministry of Defense said that Royal Air Force Typhoons would carry out air defense missions. They would protect Saudi cities and key facilities, and their missions would be limited to defense. They would not attack the Houthis, it said.

So far, the explanation is easy to understand: “The Typhoons are a shield.” It fits neatly into the explanation box. But if we try to fit all of the UK’s support into that box, the lid will not close.

That is because there is another kind of support: refueling in the air.

The UK says it will extend its refueling support for Saudi air defense operations. This means supplying fuel to aircraft while they are flying. But there is one point we cannot overlook: what the aircraft receiving the fuel will do.

According to the announcement, this support also covers operations to weaken Houthi military targets inside Yemen. The UK says these are military targets that threaten people in Saudi Arabia and Yemen.

“Wait. Wasn’t the claim that they wouldn’t attack?”

Let’s go back to the first sentence. The claim that “there will be no attacks” applies to the Typhoon’s mission. It does not mean that all Saudi operations supported by the refueling are limited to defense. Even within the same UK support, the missions are different. If we change the subject from “the Typhoon” to “all UK support,” the meaning changes completely.

One mission protects cities and facilities. The other passes fuel to other aircraft to support their operations. If we sum it all up in the line “The UK won’t attack,” this division of roles disappears.

Of course, knowing the difference does not settle whether the support is right or wrong. Before treating it as a choice between “defend” and “attack,” first ask: “Who is not doing what?” And what is being supported at the end of the refueling hose? The shorter the explanation, the more important it is not to miss what comes next.
