# “We Won’t Attack”—Did You Forget the Subject?

Fighter jets are coming, but they will not attack. “What? Even though they are fighter jets?” If this surprise makes us react too quickly, the news may take an unexpected turn. This time, a simple but important “subject” is the key to understanding it.

The Houthis fired one ballistic missile toward Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. A spokesperson for the Saudi-led coalition forces in Yemen said they had stopped and destroyed it.

The UK Ministry of Defence announced an air defense mission by Typhoon fighter jets. The jets would protect Saudi cities and important sites from air attacks. Their job would be only to defend. The UK says they will not attack the Houthis.

“Oh, so all of Britain’s support is only for defense!” It is too soon to decide that. The announcement names one more task: air refueling.

The UK also says it will extend air-refueling support for Saudi Arabia’s air defense operations. This support also covers operations to weaken Houthi military targets in Yemen. According to the UK, these operations are against military targets that threaten people in Saudi Arabia and Yemen.

“Wait a minute. Didn’t you say you wouldn’t attack?”

Yes. But those words belong to the “Typhoon mission” seat. The seat next door, “air-refueling support,” has not been booked under the same rules. Even in one announcement, the limits are different for each task.

So do not swing too far the other way. It would also be wrong to read this as, “Then will the Typhoons attack the Houthis too?” What the fighter jets do and which operations the refueling support helps are two separate things. We need to read them separately.

“Not attacking” and “not helping operations that attack” sound alike, but they do not mean the same thing. If we treat them as one, we may reach conclusions that are not in the announcement.

Also, this is what the UK announced about its missions and support. It does not say that Britain has already done all these things. We need to keep a clear line between “said it would do” and “actually did it.”

When a news story says “support to protect,” take a breath. “Whose job, and which job?” Before you go with a headline’s strong message, check the subject. Check this before asking what the fighter jets can do. It can help us avoid getting the story wrong.

## In one line
Britain says Typhoons will defend Saudi Arabia, not attack the Houthis; refueling will aid operations against Houthi military targets. These jobs differ; Britain announced plans, not actions already done.
