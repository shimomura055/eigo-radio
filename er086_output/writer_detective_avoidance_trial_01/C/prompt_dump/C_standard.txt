# “Fighter Jets That Won’t Attack” Set to Deploy—Britain’s Support Has Another Side

Britain is sending fighter jets, but not to attack the other side. The mission it announced for its Typhoon jets seemed surprising at first. But is all of Britain’s support limited to defense? Another announcement shows that the story is not so simple.

A spokesperson for the Saudi-led coalition said it had intercepted and destroyed one ballistic missile fired by the Houthis toward Riyadh. The first thing to note is that this was the coalition’s account. It was not a report that British Typhoons had intercepted the missile.

So what will the Typhoons do? According to Britain’s Ministry of Defence, their role is to protect Saudi cities and key sites from air attacks. They will stand on the defensive, not attack the other side’s bases.

Britain also says it will limit the mission to defense and will not attack the Houthis. The powerful sound of the words “fighter jets are being sent” is different from the job they have actually been given.

If that were all, the story might end with “the jets are being sent to defend.” But there is another strand to Britain’s support: an extension of its air refueling support.

Britain says it will also extend air refueling support for Saudi air defense operations. That support also covers operations to weaken Houthi military targets inside Yemen. The targets are military sites that threaten people in Saudi Arabia and Yemen.

This changes how the announcement looks. The Typhoons themselves have a clear limit: they will not attack the Houthis. But the operations supported by refueling do not follow that same limit. The mission to protect cities and the support for operations to weaken military targets need to be read separately.

The fighter jets are what draw the eye. But it was the refueling that showed how far Britain’s support reaches. It is not enough to look only at what the aircraft do directly. We also need to ask which operations they support by providing fuel. Seen this way, the picture cannot be summed up with the word “defense.”

So if we sum it up by saying only “Britain will not attack,” we leave out the targets covered by its refueling support. But if we take it to mean “British fighter jets will attack the Houthis,” we misunderstand the limits placed on the Typhoons.

Even within Britain’s support, there are jobs it is taking on and jobs it is not. The focus this time is not only on the fighter jets’ arrival. It is also on the limits placed on the jets and the wider reach of the operations linked by refueling support.
