Full Story Part 1 (Aoede)
voice=Aoede		Space security news often uses one phrase for very different things: weapons in space.

That phrase came into focus on September 14, 2026. U.S. Air Force Secretary Troy Meink said the United States was deploying orbital “space control weapons” to protect U.S. forces from hostile actions. An official U.S. Space Force article called this the first public acknowledgment that the Space Force had deployed weapons in space.

But the statement did not name the systems or explain how they could attack another satellite. No target was identified.

The wider term is “counterspace.” A 2025 U.S. Space Force framework uses that term for actions in orbit, through communication links, and on the ground. It can include physical attacks, electronic interference, and cyber operations.
Comment 2 (Charon)
voice=Charon		So far, the United States has openly said that it put a kind of weapon in orbit, but it has not said exactly what that weapon is. What other activity has been reported, and what is the important change now?
Full Story Part 2 (Aoede)
voice=Aoede		That means the current picture is mixed. In 2024, the Pentagon said a Russian satellite had been placed in a similar orbit to a U.S. government satellite and was likely able to attack other satellites. But no attack was confirmed. In December 2025, one report also described an alleged Russian plan to use small satellites and pellets against Starlink. The information could not be independently confirmed, and it was unclear whether the system had been tested or deployed.

So the important change is not that every counterspace activity is new. It is that the United States has now openly acknowledged one category of weapon being placed in orbit.

Point Two (Aoede)
voice=Aoede		The legal picture is narrower than the technology picture. The Outer Space Treaty bans nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction in orbit, and prohibits installing such weapons on the Moon and other celestial bodies. It also requires space activity to follow international law. But it does not completely ban every conventional anti-satellite missile, jammer, or cyber operation. United Nations votes in 2024 and 2025 supported existing principles and further arms-control work, but they did not create a new comprehensive treaty. The U.S. pledge against destructive direct-ascent tests remains a national policy commitment.
