# Why Can Silence Feel Uncomfortable?

A conversation can feel uncomfortable before anyone says anything rude. Sometimes, all it takes is a short silence.

In a 2011 study, 102 university students read a group conversation. After a mildly controversial comment, one group saw the conversation continue. The other group saw a brief silent moment.

The silence changed how people felt. They reported more rejection and more negative feelings. They also felt less accepted, less confident, and less sure that the group agreed with them.

A different study used a six-minute video of a conversation. Some viewers saw one four-second silence in the video. Most did not notice the exact pause. Even so, they reported a similar feeling of rejection and less connection than viewers who saw the conversation without the pause.

But silence does not mean the same thing in every conversation.

In natural ten-minute conversations, long gaps of more than two seconds were more common between friends than between strangers. For strangers, these gaps were linked with less momentary connection. For friends, they were linked with more connection. Other people who watched the conversations also rated the gaps between strangers as more awkward.

Silence can also feel difficult when we are alone.

Across 11 studies, people spent between six and 15 minutes with nothing to do but think. In general, they enjoyed simple outside activities more than sitting alone with their thoughts. In one study, 55 university students sat alone for 15 minutes. They could choose to give themselves a mild electric shock that they had already experienced. Many chose the shock at least once, rather than remain without outside stimulation.

Still, silence is not always unpleasant. In one study, people who chose to be alone reported feeling more relaxed and less stressed. A review of 37 studies also found that body responses to silence can change with the setting, a person’s past experience, and the sounds around them. In one study that measured participants’ heart activity, silence was not the most calming of the sounds tested.

The research points to a simple but important idea: silence is not one single experience. A sudden pause in a conversation can feel like rejection. Quiet time alone can feel difficult when there is no clear activity. But chosen silence, with the right setting, can feel very different.

### Silence Depends on the Social Setting

Japan–U.S. survey findings suggest that Japanese respondents viewed silence more negatively with strangers than with close friends, unlike Americans, who reported using silence more strategically. Yet a staged Japan–UK tutorial found similar discomfort and tolerance: average tolerance differed by only about seven seconds, and duration was not linked to discomfort. Meaning depends on context—not nationality or a stopwatch.

### Why Quiet Feels Different to Different People

In a replication involving 2,557 college students at 12 sites in 11 countries, everyday activities were more enjoyable than thinking for pleasure in every country. Differences between countries were statistically explained by five factors: how much people tend to enjoy thinking, openness to new experiences, meditation experience, how positive they felt at the start, and reported phone use; greater reported phone use was associated with less enjoyment of thinking. Quiet, then, is not equally easy for everyone.

## In one line

Silence is neither a universal warning nor a universal refuge; its meaning emerges from the relationship, the setting, and the person.
