# The AI Race Meets the Rulebook The world of AI is now moving on two clocks. One clock is the rulebook. In the European Union, duties for AI models that can do many kinds of work, called general-purpose AI models, began on 2 August 2025. More rules began to be enforced on 2 August 2026. They cover prohibited AI practices, some transparency duties, and duties for these models. Not every EU rule started that day. Some systems placed on the market before then have until 2 December 2026 for certain marking and detection duties. The other clock is capability. In cyber tests, the best AI models completed apprentice-level tasks about half the time. In early 2024, the figure was just over one in ten. The length of cyber tasks they could complete without human direction was doubling about every eight months. The tests also found vulnerabilities in every system. But results varied by model and by type of misuse. The U.S. administration is also urging speed. An executive order from 2 June 2026 called for innovation, security, and rapid deployment of secure AI. It also set a goal of strengthening U.S. national security and global AI dominance, while rejecting regulation that is too heavy. At the global level, a UN scientific panel said in a July 2026 report that current safeguards cannot keep pace with AI capabilities. So the tension is clear. Safety rules are growing, but the push to build and deploy more powerful systems continues. ### Not one worldwide speed limit Here is the uneven part. The EU’s dates create concrete duties for providers. The UN panel is different. Its members can publish scientific assessments, including the warning that safeguards are falling behind, but its work does not create binding rules or stop a company. AI regulation is not one worldwide speed limit. It is stronger in some places and advisory in others. ### Safety pauses do not stop the race Inside the companies, restraint and speed can exist together. OpenAI said it held back a large training project while raising safety and security requirements, then restarted it on 28 August 2026. Smaller tests stayed on hold. OpenAI also proposed common tests and independent checks, but those proposals are not law. Anthropic said pacing means choosing safety over speed inside one company, while industry-wide pacing needs lawful coordination. Google released Gemini 3.7 Flash three weeks after 3.6. One company’s pause is not an industry slowdown. ## In one line… AI safety rules are becoming real in some places, but they do not form one global limit. The race continues, and one company’s restraint has not yet become a shared plan.

Other parts of the EU AI Act began enforcement on 2 August 2026. These include rules on prohibited AI practices, some transparency duties, and certain duties for general-purpose models. But the timetable is staged. Not every rule began on the same day. International action is also growing, but it has a different role. The United Nations created an independent scientific panel with 40 members in August 2025. # When Silence Feels Too Loud The strange thing about silence is that it can feel louder than speech. Imagine a conversation. Someone says something, and then nobody speaks for four seconds. The pause may seem very short. But it can still change the feeling in the room. In one experiment, students watched a six-minute video of a conversation. After one statement, the conversation stopped for four seconds. Most participants did not notice the exact pause. Still, compared with people who watched a smooth conversation, they reported more negative feelings. They also felt less accepted and less connected, and they were less sure that the group agreed with them. A second study with 102 students found a similar result after a short silent moment in an imagined group conversation. These studies do not show that every silence feels bad. They show that an unexpected break can change how people experience a social moment. Everyday conversations tell a more surprising story. In ten-minute conversations, gaps longer than two seconds happened more often between friends than between strangers. For strangers, these longer gaps were linked with less momentary connection. For friends, they were linked with more connection. People watching video clips also rated long gaps between strangers as more awkward. As the gap became longer, awkwardness rose more strongly for strangers. Silence can also feel difficult when nobody else is there. Across 11 studies, people generally did not enjoy sitting alone for six to 15 minutes with nothing to do but think. Simple outside activities were usually rated as more enjoyable. In one study, 55 students sat alone for 15 minutes. They had a button that could give a mild electric shock they had already experienced. Many chose to use the button rather than sit without outside stimulation. But silence is not always unpleasant. Other studies found that chosen time alone could lower strong feelings and was linked with relaxation. In one study, about 46 students spent six minutes and 30 seconds in silence indoors and outdoors. Both settings increased relaxation. The outdoor setting also brought less boredom and more attention to the present moment. Research across countries also shows that there is no single rule. In a study of 2,557 college students at 12 sites in 11 countries, an everyday activity was more enjoyable than thinking for pleasure in every country tested. But people still differed. A review of 37 studies found that the body’s response to silence can change with the situation, the type of silence, and how familiar a person is with it. So, the uncomfortable feeling may not come from silence alone. It may depend on what the silence seems to mean, what a person does with the quiet, and whether the moment feels chosen or unexpected. ### Quiet Does Not Always Mean Calmest A quiet room is not automatically the body’s lowest-arousal state. In an experiment that balanced the order of the sounds, heart measurements showed that the body’s rest-and-recovery system was more active during noise from an MRI scanner than during silence, while the body’s alerting system was more active during white noise than during the MRI noise. Feeling calm and having the lowest level of bodily activation are therefore different claims. ### The Person and the Relationship Matter Comfort with silence may reflect both personal orientation and the relationship involved. Enjoyment of thinking for pleasure was linked with a preference for thinking deeply, being open to new experiences, meditation experience, feeling more positive at the start, and using a phone less—but these were related factors, not proven causes. Japanese respondents viewed silence more negatively with strangers than with close friends; Americans did not show that pattern. Yet a staged Japan–UK tutorial found similar tolerance and discomfort. ## In one line The quiet itself may be simple; the experience of it is not.

# Why Silence Can Feel Uncomfortable A conversation can be going well. Then someone finishes a sentence, and no one answers. In one 2011 experiment, 102 undergraduate students imagined a group conversation. After a mildly controversial comment, some students read that the conversation continued normally. Others read that a brief silence followed. The silence group reported more feelings of rejection and negative emotion. They also reported less belonging, lower self-esteem, and less agreement with the group. A second experiment showed a similar pattern in a more realistic setting. In this experiment, students watched a six-minute video of a conversation. In one version, a single silence lasted four seconds. Afterward, those students reported more rejection and negative emotion, and less positive emotion and belonging. Most were not aware of the exact pause. But a long pause does not always feel bad. A 2023 study looked at natural ten-minute conversations between strangers and friends. Gaps of more than two seconds were more common among friends. For strangers, these longer gaps reduced the feeling of connection. For friends, they increased it. People who watched the conversations also judged long pauses between strangers as more awkward. The same question appears when people are alone. Across 11 studies, participants generally did not enjoy spending six to fifteen minutes with nothing to do but think. They often rated simple activities as more enjoyable. In one study, 55 students sat alone for 15 minutes with the option of giving themselves a mild electric shock they had already experienced. Many chose the shock instead of remaining without outside stimulation. That was a result from this experiment, not a claim about everyone. At the same time, silence can also bring calm. In one study of roughly 46 students, six minutes and 30 seconds of silence increased relaxation in both a university room and a city garden. The outdoor setting also brought less boredom and a stronger feeling of being in the present moment. Research on the body shows the same mixed picture. A review of 37 studies found that responses to silence depend on the kind of silence, the setting, familiarity, surrounding noise, and other personal factors. In a study that measured heart activity, one noise condition showed more activity linked with the body’s calming system than silence, while white noise showed more activity linked with its alerting system than that noise. People also differ across studies and cultures. In a study of 2,557 college students at 12 sites in 11 countries, an everyday activity was enjoyed more than thinking for pleasure in every country tested. Still, differences between countries were linked with personal factors such as openness, meditation experience, starting mood, and phone use. In one Japan–United States survey, Japanese respondents viewed silence more negatively with strangers than with close friends, while Americans did not show that difference. ### When Quiet Has Structure The key distinction is not silence itself, but whether it is chosen and mentally structured. In one study, actively choosing solitude was associated with relaxation and lower stress. In another study, students told to occupy their thoughts for a brief period reported better mood, greater relaxation, and lower arousal afterward. Quiet can therefore function differently when people have agency and a mental path to follow. ### Context Over Country A staged one-to-one tutorial complicates any simple cultural story. Japanese and UK undergraduates differed by only about seven seconds in their mean tolerance of an instructor’s silence. Both groups reported similar discomfort, around 4 on a six-point scale, and longer silence was not linked to greater discomfort. Nationality alone, then, is a weak guide when roles and setting change. ## In one line Silence is not a universal signal; before judging the pause, listen for the situation that gives it meaning.

# When the News Feed Knows You ## The Question Before work, a phone fills with headlines. The list is not the same for everyone. One reader finds stories that fit a limited morning. Another notices that similar stories and opinions keep returning. Both are looking at a feed shaped by past clicks, follows, and interests. The question sounds simple: does this make news better? Behind it are two experiences—useful focus, and a world that may be getting smaller. ### One Voice: The reader who counts on her feed On the morning train, she opens a news app’s “For you” page. Stories close to her interests are already there, so she can catch up before work without digging through an endless list. She follows topics and sources, hides others, and asks for more or fewer stories. That gives her a sense of control, even though she cannot see every signal behind the ranking. She still worries that an important story may be left out. But on a busy day, relevance is the reason she can keep up. ### Another Voice: The reader who feels it narrowing Familiar opinions return, and she begins to wonder what is missing. She cannot see the signals behind the ranking. She knows they can include past clicks and search history, while the system can be tuned for engagement or ad revenue. The choice may not feel like hers. In the United States, about 60 percent of people in one survey worried about missing different views. Her unease has a concrete basis: under one news-feed ranking, people saw less news from different viewpoints than in friends’ shared content, while users’ clicks limited that exposure even more. ## Why They See It Differently Both care about what they see because it shapes how they understand their lives. But one risks losing time needed to stay informed; the other risks entering a narrow view without noticing. Follows and hides make personalization feel chosen, while the same ranking feels imposed. In a short window, measured political attitudes may not change, while years of exposure remain outside the picture. Two valid needs still leave no simple answer. ## What This Is Really About The deeper question is who sets the boundaries of attention, and whether they feel chosen or quietly assigned. A private route may fit one morning perfectly, while making another reader wonder which roads have disappeared. The issue is not only what people read, but how much of the map they can see.
