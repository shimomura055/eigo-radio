# Before the Last Light

Melos’s voice rang through the hall. “You kill men because you fear them. You won’t even let them speak.”

Silence followed. Then the guards seized the young shepherd, and the ruler sentenced him to death.

“Give me three days,” Melos said. “My sister is getting married. Let me see her wedding. I will return.”

The ruler smiled. “Your friend will stay here. If you are late, he dies instead.”

Melos turned to his friend, but before he could speak, his friend held out his hands for the chains.

“Go. I know you’ll come back.”

At the wedding, his sister’s laughter rose above the other voices. Melos listened closely. He wanted to remember exactly how it sounded.

Before dawn on the third day, he left for the city.

At the river, he stopped. The bridge was gone. Rain had turned the water brown and violent. He stepped back, ran, and threw himself into the flood.

The current pulled him under. He fought toward the light, caught a branch, and dragged himself onto the far bank.

Farther along, three men blocked the road. A knife flashed. Melos struck the man holding it with his walking stick and ran uphill, their shouts following him.

By afternoon, every step hurt. Blood stained his feet. He fell beside a rock and tried to rise, but his legs would not obey.

He could wait here. Someone might find him. He could go home.

His friend would die.

For one terrible moment, that seemed easier than getting up.

Then he remembered the hands held out for the chains. His friend had trusted him without asking for another promise.

Water dripped from a crack in the rock. Melos drank, pushed himself onto his knees, and stood.

One step. Another.

At the city gates, the execution bell rang.

He ran toward the square. Above his friend, the sword was already raised.

“Stop! I am here!”

His friend turned. Melos fell against him.

“I nearly abandoned you,” he whispered.

“I nearly stopped believing,” his friend answered.

They held each other as the last sunlight touched the walls.

The ruler stared at them.

“Lower the sword,” he said at last. “Free them both.”

Then, more quietly, he asked, “How does a man earn such trust?”

For the first time, he waited for an answer instead of ordering one.
