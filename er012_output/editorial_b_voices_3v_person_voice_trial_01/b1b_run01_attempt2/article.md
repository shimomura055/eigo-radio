# When AI Screens a Job Application, Three People See Three Different Risks

## The Question

A résumé is sent. A program reads it, scores a test, or watches a video interview. The result may help decide who moves forward. By September 2026, this is part of hiring in many workplaces. The same screen looks different to the person judged, the worker running it, and the owner carrying the decision.

### The Applicant’s Voice: Speaking to a Machine

I send my résumé and sit before a camera. Software may score my tone, face, and gestures. I can do well on a skills test, yet a low video score can close the door. One woman described this and later faced long-term unemployment. I want a reason, a fair chance, and a way to challenge a hidden bias.

### Another Voice, the Recruiter: Running the Process

I open the résumé tool and work through a large pile. It saves time, but my job does not end when scores appear. I may need to prepare an audit summary for someone asking whether the process was fair. I also judge AI-written applications: useful skill, or too little effort? I use the system, but I do not choose the company’s policy.

### A Third Voice, the Business Owner: Carrying the Risk

Hiring time and costs matter to me. When the team fills jobs on time, I can breathe; the business has to keep moving. I see the attraction in reports of much faster hiring at one company and lower costs at another. But if the process appears unfair, public damage, legal costs, and lost trust can come back to me. I decide whether we keep using it.

## Why They See It Differently

All three want the right person in the right job. Then the stakes split. The applicant may lose a chance without being fairly seen. The recruiter must keep the process moving and answer for it, but cannot set policy. The owner must keep the business running and carry the result. Their power is unequal: judged, operator, decision-maker. Outside rules narrow the choices too. In New York City, Local Law 144 requires a bias audit and notice to applicants; in the EU, hiring AI systems that fall within the AI Act’s high-risk rules require human oversight. No single voice, or simple total, answers the question.

## What This Really Means

The deeper issue is not only whether a machine can sort applications. It is who defines a fair chance, who may question the judgment, and who carries the consequences when a quick decision closes a door. AI hiring is also a question about power, proof, and responsibility.
