# When AI Enters the Hiring Room

## The Question

A job application arrives, and software reads it before a person does. A short video is scored for voice, facial expression, and movement. Behind that screen are three different people: the applicant being judged, the recruiter using the tool, and the owner deciding whether the company can keep hiring this way. The same AI system does not mean the same thing to all three.

### Voice One: The Applicant Facing the Screen

I sit before a camera and try to speak naturally while software scores my tone, face, and movements. I want my skills and character to be seen, not turned into a secret score. One woman said in a report that she did well on a skills test, but the video system marked down her gestures and facial expressions. She was not hired and later faced long-term unemployment. I cannot choose the tool or challenge its judgment. I may never know why I was rejected.

### Another Voice: The Recruiter Using the Tool

I move from screening resumes to arranging interviews, with AI helping me handle a large pile of applications and match people with roles. But after the screen goes quiet, I may need to prepare a plain explanation of the tool’s outside check for unfair patterns. About 90 percent of HR leaders reported actively using AI somewhere in hiring. I did not make the final decision to buy it. I still have to use it every day and answer worried applicants.

### A Third Voice: The Business Owner

Each week, I open the dashboard showing hiring time and cost. If vacancies stay open, the business feels it, and I must decide whether to keep using the tool. I have seen a reported hotel case where hiring time fell by about 85 percent after AI was introduced. That is hard to ignore. But if the system treats people unfairly, legal costs, public blame, and lost trust can come back to me. I am protecting the company’s future and my own judgment.

## Why They See It Differently

All three want the same basic result: the right person in the right job. But the stakes split. The applicant may lose a chance without a fair look. The recruiter must keep work moving while explaining a system they did not choose. The owner must keep the company alive and answer for it. Their power is unequal: the applicant is judged, the recruiter operates the tool, and the owner approves it. In New York City, employers must arrange a yearly check for unfair patterns and notify applicants. In Europe, worker-selection AI is treated as high-risk. Three reasonable views still do not add up to one answer.

## What This Tells Us

The question now looks less like a simple choice between modern technology and human judgment. It is about who defines a good candidate, who can question a score, and who carries the cost when that score affects a livelihood and a company. AI has not removed responsibility from hiring. It has spread it across people who do not hold equal power.
