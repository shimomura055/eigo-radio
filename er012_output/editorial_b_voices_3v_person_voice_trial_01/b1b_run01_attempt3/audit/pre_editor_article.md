# When AI Enters the Hiring Room, Three People See Three Different Risks

## The Question

A resume enters a hiring system. A program may scan its words, score a test, or watch a video interview. At the same time, three people face the same tool from different sides: the person hoping to be hired, the worker running the process, and the owner who must live with the result. The question is already in the hiring room.

### The applicant's voice: Under the camera

I face a camera and try to sound natural. Software may score my tone, face, and gestures. I want my skills and character—not a guess about how I move—to be judged. I can do well on a skills test and still lose the job after a low video score. If I cannot opt out or challenge it, I am left with a result but no clear reason. Nearly half of working US job seekers see these tools as more biased than human recruiters.

### Another voice: The recruiter at the screen

New applications arrive, and I open the resume-screening system. It helps me sort resumes, arrange interviews, and match people with jobs. I am not unusual: 91% of HR leaders say they actively use AI somewhere in hiring. But the work does not end with a shortlist. I may need to prepare a clear bias-audit summary for applicants who distrust the tool. I also face a hard judgment: is an AI-written application a useful skill, or weak effort? I run the system, but I do not make the final company decision.

### A third voice: The business owner's dashboard

Each week, I study the dashboard: hiring time, cost, and open jobs. I decide whether an AI tool stays. I have seen cases where hiring moved from weeks to days, and a reported 30% cut in IBM’s hiring cost. That pressure is personal. If bias becomes public, the damage can reach the company’s name, legal costs, and my own credibility. I am trying to keep the business running, but the final responsibility comes back to me.

## Why They See It Differently

All three want the same result: the right person in the right job. But the applicant risks losing a chance without being fairly seen. The recruiter must keep work moving while answering questions about the tool. The owner must keep the company running and carry the decision. The power is uneven: the applicant is judged, the recruiter runs the system without choosing the policy, and the owner chooses whether it stays. Their reasonable views still do not make one answer. A New York recruiter may need a bias audit and notice before using such a tool, while European rules treat hiring AI as high risk. The outside rules limit all three.

## What This Changes

The deeper issue is not machine versus human. It is whether a hiring decision can be trusted when the person judged, the person operating the tool, and the person accepting the risk control different parts of the process. Their views alone cannot settle it. The rules outside the room are part of the decision.
