# When AI Enters the Hiring Room, Three People See Three Different Risks

## The Question

A resume enters a hiring system. A program may scan its words, score a test, or watch a video interview. At the same time, three people face the same tool from different sides: the person hoping to be hired, the worker running the process, and the owner who must live with the result. The question is already in the hiring room.

### The applicant's voice: Under the camera

I face a camera and try to sound natural. Software may score my tone, face, and gestures. I want my skills and character—not a guess about how I move—to be judged. I can do well on a skills test and still lose the job after a low video score. If I cannot opt out or challenge it, I am left with a result but no clear reason. Nearly half of working US job seekers see these tools as more biased than human recruiters.

### Another voice: The recruiter at the screen

New applications arrive, and I open the resume-screening system. It helps me sort resumes, arrange interviews, and match people with jobs. I am not unusual: 91% of HR leaders say they actively use AI somewhere in hiring. But the work does not end with a shortlist. I may need to prepare a clear bias-audit summary for applicants who distrust the tool. I also face a hard judgment: is an AI-written application a useful skill, or weak effort? I run the system, but my assessment may be only one part of the company’s final decision.

### A third voice: The business owner's dashboard

Each week, I study the dashboard: hiring time, cost, and open jobs. I help assess whether an AI tool is worth keeping for the business. I have seen cases where hiring moved from weeks to days, and one report found a 30% cut in a company's hiring cost. That pressure is personal. The cited analysis suggests that if an AI hiring system produces discriminatory outcomes, the company may face legal and reputational risks. I am trying to keep the business running, but the decision still comes back to weighing efficiency gains against legal and reputational risks.

## Why They See It Differently

All three want the same result: the right person in the right job. But the applicant risks losing a chance without being fairly seen. The recruiter must keep work moving while answering questions about the tool. The owner must keep the company running and carry the decision. The power is uneven: the applicant is judged, while the recruiter or hiring manager may use the system or help decide whether it is used, and the business side weighs potential efficiency gains against legal, reputational, and other risks. Their reasonable views still do not make one answer. A New York City recruiter may need a bias audit and notice before using such a tool, while European rules can treat AI used in hiring as high risk when it may significantly affect a person’s career, livelihood, or workers’ rights. The outside rules limit all three.

## What This Changes

The deeper issue is not machine versus human. It is whether a hiring decision can be trusted when the person judged, the person operating the tool, and the person accepting the risk control different parts of the process. Their views alone cannot settle it. The rules outside the room are part of the decision.
