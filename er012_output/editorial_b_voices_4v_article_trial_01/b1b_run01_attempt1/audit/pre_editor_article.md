# When AI Reads the Resume, Four Different Stories Begin

## The Question

An applicant speaks into a laptop camera while software checks voice, face, and gestures. Another system sorts resumes. A recruiter explains the process, a manager watches time and cost, and a legal reviewer checks the record. By 2026, these can be parts of one hiring process. Yet the same system looks different from each seat around the table.

### The Applicant's Voice: In Front of the Camera

The camera stays on while the applicant speaks. A score may come from tone, expression, or gestures. One woman told BBC Worklife that her skills test went well, but an AI video interview rated her movements poorly. She was not hired and later faced a long period without work. In another case, an applicant sued after CVS Health used AI to score “reliability and honesty,” with no way to opt out or challenge the result. They are not choosing the system; they are trying to be understood fairly.

### The Recruiter's Voice: Behind the Screen

After resumes are sorted, the recruiter still asks: who fits this job, and who might fit another? The tool may save time, but it creates work too. NBCUniversal published a notice after an independent review of its automated hiring tools under New York City law. The recruiter may finish screening, then prepare audit information for a worried applicant. AI-written resumes also divide opinion: useful skill to some, too little effort to others.

### The Business Voice: Watching the Dashboard

On another screen, the hiring dashboard shows time and cost. Reported company cases describe hiring moving from weeks to days, with lower spending after AI was introduced. That is the attraction: handling more hiring without the same growth in workload. But unfair treatment can bring legal trouble and damage the company’s name. The business leader weighs a visible gain today against a risk that may appear later, in a lawsuit or a public headline.

### The Fairness Voice: Reading the Case File

An auditor opens an old case file. Amazon stopped an internal hiring tool after past resumes taught it to rate women lower in some cases. New York City requires a bias audit, a public summary, and notice to candidates before certain automated tools are used. The EU classifies AI for recruitment as high-risk, with rules on transparency and human oversight. But the legal picture is uneven: Japan did not have an equivalent audit law at the time examined. The reviewer asks whether a decision can be checked, not simply whether software can produce a score.

## Why They See It Differently

These four people are not on opposite sides of one line. The applicant carries the result directly: a score can close a door. The recruiter uses the tool for volume but must explain it. The business leader sees time, cost, and scale, while fearing the price of a fairness failure. The legal reviewer comes later, trying to make the process answerable. Their views differ because they stand at different distances from the result, and the cost becomes visible at different times.

## What This Tells Us

That changes the question. “Should companies use AI?” sounds like a vote on a machine. The deeper issue is whose opportunity is being measured, who can question the score, and who must answer when it is wrong. Speed, fairness, and accountability meet in one hiring decision.
