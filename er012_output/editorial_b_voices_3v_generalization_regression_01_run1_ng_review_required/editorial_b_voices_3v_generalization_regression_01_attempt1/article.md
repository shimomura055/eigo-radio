# When AI Enters the Hiring Room

## The Question

An application is sent. A video interview begins. Behind the screen, software may read a résumé, score a personality test, or judge a voice, face, and gesture. The same process looks different from three seats: an applicant waiting, a recruiter facing a crowded desk, and an owner deciding what the company can carry.

### The Applicant’s Voice: Under the Camera

I speak into the camera while software watches my tone, face, and gestures. I may never know why I did not move forward. In one reported case, a woman said her skills test went well, but an AI video score marked down her gestures and expressions; she later faced long-term unemployment. Another applicant said he was scored for “reliability and honesty” without a chance to opt out or challenge the result. I want a fair chance to be understood.

### Another Voice: At the Recruiter’s Desk

I open the screening tool after a morning of applications. It helps me sort résumés and match people with jobs. Then an applicant asks how the system was checked. I cannot answer, “The computer said so.” I may prepare a bias-check summary and explain the notice. One company has publicly shown this process under New York City law. I also judge AI-written applications: useful skill, or less effort? I may help decide whether to use the tool; if we do, I must answer for it.

### A Third Voice: Watching the Dashboard

I look at the weekly dashboard: how long hiring takes and what it costs. I need enough people, quickly enough, to keep the business moving. Other companies have reported hiring faster and spending less after using AI, so the choice can feel tied to survival. But when I see a tool facing a privacy lawsuit over consent and disclosure, I think about my own name on the decision. I cannot hand that risk to a machine. I am protecting the company’s future and trust in my judgment.

## Why They See Different Stakes

All three want the same thing: the right person in the right job. But the wager differs. The applicant risks losing a chance unseen; the recruiter risks speed without explanation; the owner risks the company’s future and responsibility. In the evidence gathered here, their power appears uneven: the applicant is judged, while the recruiter may be involved in using the tool or in decisions about its use. New York City’s yearly bias check and notice rule adds work for the recruiter, limits the owner’s choice, and requires notice to the applicant. One company stopped an internal tool after it rated women’s résumés lower. Outside rules and past failures mean these reasons cannot simply be added.

## What the Question Really Is

The deeper question is not only whether a machine can sort applications. It is who gets to define a fair chance when the machine becomes part of the gate. Speed, trust, and responsibility belong to different people, while rules and past failures surround them. One hiring decision is also a question of access, explanation, and control.
