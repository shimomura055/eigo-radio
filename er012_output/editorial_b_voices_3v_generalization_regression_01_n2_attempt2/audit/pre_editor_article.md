# When AI Becomes the First Hiring Gate

## The Question

An application is sent. A video interview is recorded. Behind the screen, software may sort a résumé, score a test, or read a face and voice. The moment looks different to the person being judged, the worker running the process, and the owner keeping the company moving. Their views do not make one easy answer.

### The applicant: facing the screen

I send my résumé and sit before a camera, knowing software may score my voice, face, and gestures. I want my skills and character seen, not just a pattern on a screen. I have heard of a case where a good skills result was followed by low AI scores for gestures and long-term unemployment. I also know that one applicant said there was no chance to refuse an honesty score or challenge it. If the decision is wrong, I may not know where to begin.

### The recruiter: keeping the process moving

I open the screening dashboard and work through a pile of résumés. AI helps me screen them and arrange interviews. It saves time, but it does not remove hard questions. When I am asked how the tool was checked, I prepare a summary of its bias audit and the notice we gave. I also judge AI-written applications: are they a useful skill, or a sign of less effort? I did not choose the final policy, but I must run it and answer for it.

### The business owner: watching the clock

I look at the weekly dashboard: how long hiring takes and what it costs. I need enough people to keep the business running. AI is tempting; in reported cases, hiring time fell from weeks to days and costs fell. But if an AI decision is called discriminatory, I feel my name will be attached to it. I do not write the rules or answer every applicant, but I decide whether we keep using the tool. That makes the choice personal.

## Why Their Choices Pull Apart

All three want the same result: the right person in the right job. But their risks differ. The applicant may lose a chance without being fairly seen. The recruiter must keep work moving and explain the tool. The owner must keep the company running and answer for the choice. Power is uneven: the applicant is judged, the recruiter runs the tool without choosing it, and the owner decides whether it stays. Outside limits matter. New York City requires yearly bias audits and notice for covered tools, so the owner cannot judge speed alone and the recruiter must document the tool. Amazon stopped an internal tool after it rated resumes with women’s terms lower. Those rules and failures narrow the choices; the views cannot simply be added.

## What the Question Really Is

This is about more than machine accuracy. It is about who gets a fair chance, who must explain the process, and who carries the decision when speed and risk meet. AI may change the hiring gate, but it does not make responsibility disappear. The deeper question is who can answer for the choice.
