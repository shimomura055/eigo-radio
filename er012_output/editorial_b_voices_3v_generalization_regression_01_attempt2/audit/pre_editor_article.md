# Three People, One AI Hiring Decision

## The Question

A résumé enters a company system. A video interview ends, but the first judge may not be a person. Software can sort applications, score personality tests, and examine a candidate’s voice, face, or gestures. By September 2026, this is no longer a distant idea. It is one moment, seen from three places: the applicant, the recruiter, and the owner keeping the business alive.

### The applicant’s voice: speaking into the camera

I send my résumé and sit in front of a camera. I know software may score my tone, expression, and gestures, even as I try to show my real skills. I want a fair chance, and I want to understand why I move forward or stop. That feels frightening when I cannot refuse the score or question it. I may do well on a skills test and still be marked down for how I move or look. Nearly half of employed U.S. job seekers share the fear that these tools are more biased than human recruiters.

### The recruiter’s voice: making the list

By morning, I am moving through résumés, arranging interviews, and writing job posts. The software helps me handle a large pile and connect people with possible roles. But I may be the person who must answer when an applicant asks, “How was I judged?” I prepare plain explanations and audit summaries, even though I did not make the final decision to adopt the tool. I also face a new judgment call: is an AI-written résumé a sign of useful skill, or too little effort? In one survey, 87% of recruiters said they use AI in at least one hiring step.

### The business owner’s voice: watching the clock

Every week, I check how long jobs stay open and what hiring costs. If key roles remain empty, I worry about keeping the business moving. That makes AI hard to dismiss. I have seen hiring move from weeks to days, so speed can feel like a lifeline. But the decision comes back to me. If a tool is accused of unfair treatment, I picture my name attached to that choice and feel the legal and public pressure. I am protecting the company’s ability to keep hiring—and my trust in my judgment.

## Why They See It Differently

All three want the same thing: the right person in the right job. But the applicant may lose a chance without being fairly seen; the recruiter must keep hiring moving and answer for the process; the owner must keep the company running and carry the decision. Their power is unequal: the applicant cannot choose the system, the recruiter runs it without final authority, and the owner approves its use. Outside rules narrow these choices: New York City’s annual bias checks and notice rules limit what the recruiter and owner can approve, while Amazon stopped an internal tool after it rated women’s résumés lower. So the three views cannot simply be added together.

## What This Changes

The deeper issue is not simply whether a machine can sort people well. It is who controls access to work, who can question a score, and who must answer when a fast decision feels unfair. AI brings opportunity, daily work, and business survival into one process. The question is about power and responsibility, not technology alone.
