# The Desk Question: A Place to Belong or Space to Manage?

## The Question

Each morning, an office worker checks which desks are empty. A bag goes under one. Papers stay in a case. The keyboard may have been used by someone else yesterday. On another floor, someone responsible for the workplace sees many unused desks. These are two scenes inside one question: should people have a desk of their own, or choose one each day?

### One Voice: The worker looking for a place

The search is part of the morning. No familiar drawer, screen, or corner is waiting. The worker starts in a space shaped by someone else. A free seat can feel less like freedom and more like a daily test.

The worry is practical: where do papers and personal things go? Is the desk and keyboard clean? Workers in one Japanese workplace said, “I cannot settle because I have no desk,” and, “I do not know where to put my papers and personal things.” For more than a third of people working this way, the flexible choice tends to become the same seat again and again. The need is a stable start, with enough continuity to focus and feel included.

### Another Voice: The person responsible for the whole floor

An empty desk still has to be explained. Linda Foggie, a global head of real estate and corporate services at a major bank, must weigh unused space against people who need to feel they belong. She also sees the risk that too much flex seating may weaken a team’s sense of being together.

Foggie described a “slow, quiet shift” toward more assigned seats and fewer flex seats, or desks shared on different days. That is not a personal vote. Her decisions cover employees with different schedules and must be explained to senior leaders asking about space costs. She is protecting company resources and people’s sense of place.

## Why They See It Differently

The worker measures the office in minutes: Can I sit down, settle, and concentrate? The workplace leader measures it across a whole floor: How much space is being paid for, and who needs it on which days? In one benchmark, the share of companies using assigned seats fell from 83 percent to 55 percent by 2024. Yet one major company returned its U.S. headquarters offices to assigned seats while keeping shared desks in some European offices. Local attendance, team needs, and actual use can shape the choice. The question is not “Which system wins?” but “What does this place need its seats to do?”

## What This Tells Us

Before these voices, a desk can look like a simple policy. After them, it becomes two measures of a good office: the worker’s daily feeling of focus and belonging, and the leader’s duty to make limited space work for everyone. Neither measure cancels the other. A seat is small, but what it means depends on who must live with it—or answer for it.
