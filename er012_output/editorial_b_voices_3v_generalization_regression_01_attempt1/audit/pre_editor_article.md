# One Hiring Tool, Three Different Stakes

## The Question

A job applicant speaks to a laptop camera while software watches the voice, face, and small movements on the screen. Elsewhere, a recruiter moves through a long line of applications. A business owner checks the hiring clock. One AI tool becomes three different experiences. Should companies use it?

### The applicant at the camera

I send my application and sit in front of a camera. Software may score my tone, face, and gestures. I want a fair chance and a reason for rejection. I have seen a reported case in which a woman did well on a skills test but received a low video score for her gestures and expression, then faced long-term unemployment. Another applicant sued after having no way to refuse or challenge an AI score about trust and honesty. I cannot change the rules; I can only wait.

### The recruiter between speed and questions

I open the screening system after a flood of applications. It helps me move people toward the right jobs, but it does not remove my responsibility. I may need to prepare an audit summary when a candidate asks whether the process was fair. I also face a hard question: when an applicant uses AI to write a résumé, is that a useful skill or too little effort? I use these tools daily, but I do not set policy. I keep the process moving while answering questions.

### The business owner watching the clock

Each week, I check a dashboard for hiring time and cost. If we cannot fill roles fast enough, I feel that pressure as my decision. AI looks attractive: a hotel chain reported cutting hiring time from weeks to days, while IBM reported lower hiring costs. But I also see the other side. If an AI tool is accused of discrimination, my name will be tied to the choice. I am carrying the risk of keeping the company running.

## Why They See the Same Tool Differently

All three want the right person in the right job. But the stakes split: the applicant may lose a chance unseen; the recruiter must keep speed and explain the process; the owner must keep the company running and answer for the choice. Power is unequal: the applicant is judged, the recruiter runs a tool without choosing it, and the owner chooses it and carries the result. In New York City, yearly bias checks and public notice make speed not just a private choice: the recruiter must document the tool, and applicants must be told. Amazon stopped a tool after it rated women’s résumés lower. So these reasonable views cannot simply become one answer.

## What This Question Is Really About

The question is no longer only whether a machine can help a company hire. It is also about who gets a fair chance to be seen, who must explain a score, and who carries the final risk. AI changes the hiring room, but outside rules, past failures, and unequal power decide how large that room really is.
