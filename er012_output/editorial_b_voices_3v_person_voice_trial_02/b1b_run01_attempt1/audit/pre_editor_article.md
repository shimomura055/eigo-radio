# When a Machine Reads the Résumé First

## The Question

A résumé is sent. A recorded interview follows. The first reader may be software, not a person, scoring words, tone, facial movement, or answers. In hiring today, this is a real moment shared by three people: the applicant, the recruiter, and the owner whose decision keeps the business running.

### Voice One: The Applicant

I send my résumé, then sit in front of a camera. Software may judge my tone, face, and gestures. I can do well on a skills test and still be rejected after my movements receive a low score. I can then face long-term unemployment. I can also receive a “reliability” score without a chance to opt out or challenge it. Nearly half of working U.S. job seekers see AI hiring tools as more biased than people. I cannot choose the system. I want a fair chance and a reason.

### Another Voice: The Recruiter

I open the hiring dashboard and let a screening tool sort a large stack of résumés. It helps me move faster and match people with jobs. Then I hear a candidate ask how the system works. I may need to explain an audit and public notice, as NBCUniversal has done. Around nine in ten HR leaders say they already use AI in hiring. I also judge whether an AI-written résumé shows useful skill or too little effort. I run the tool, but I do not decide whether the company adopts it.

### A Third Voice: The Business Owner

I check this week’s dashboard: hiring days, money spent, and whether teams have enough people. I have seen reported cases where Hilton cut hiring time from weeks to days, and IBM cut hiring costs by about 30%. That can feel tied to whether my company keeps up. But if a tool appears unfair, I imagine my name attached to the decision. I cannot hand responsibility to software. I must live with the choice to keep using it, even when speed and risk arrive together.

## Why They See It Differently

All three want the right person in the right job. But the applicant may lose a chance unseen; the recruiter must keep work moving and explain the tool; the owner must keep the company running and own the result. Power is unequal: the applicant cannot choose the tool, the recruiter runs it but does not choose its use, and the owner decides. Outside limits make a simple sum impossible. New York City requires a recent bias audit and notice. That limits the owner, adds work for the recruiter, and gives the applicant notice, not control. Amazon stopped an internal tool after it rated résumés linked to women lower. Rules and failures constrain all three, so their reasonable views cannot answer the question alone.

## What This Tells Us

The deeper question is not simply whether a machine can sort people. It is who gets a fair chance to be understood, who must explain a judgment, and who carries it when the system fails. AI hiring is also a question about power: who can choose, who can object, and who must answer.
