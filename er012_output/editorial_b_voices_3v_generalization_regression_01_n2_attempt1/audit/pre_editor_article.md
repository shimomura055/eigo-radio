# When AI Reads the Resume, Who Carries the Risk?

## The Question

A resume is sent, a video interview is recorded, and a computer helps decide who moves on. The applicant may never see the score. The recruiter must work with the tool and answer questions about it. The business owner watches time and cost. One hiring process now carries three very different kinds of pressure.

### The applicant: speaking to the screen

I send my resume and speak to a camera while software scores my voice, face, and movements. I want my skills seen, not reduced to a hidden score. A woman told the BBC her skills test went well, but an AI video score on her gestures and facial expressions was low. She was rejected, then faced long-term unemployment. In another case, an applicant said they could not refuse or challenge an AI “reliability” score. I cannot choose the process, but I must live with its result.

### The recruiter: running the system

I sort a large batch of resumes with software, then prepare to answer questions about the process. Around nine in ten HR leaders say they actively use AI somewhere in hiring. It can free time and help match people to jobs. But I still face questions about fairness and about AI-written applications: do they show useful skill, or too little effort? NBCUniversal made an independent review of a hiring tool public. I run the system, but I do not decide whether the company buys it.

### The business owner: watching the dashboard

Every week, I look at the dashboard: how long hiring takes and what it costs. When I see reports of a hotel chain moving from weeks to days, I feel the pressure ease. I need the company to fill roles fast enough to keep running. But if a hiring tool is accused of unfairness, I imagine my name on the answer. I choose whether we continue, and the result comes back to me.

## Why They See It Differently

All three want the right person in the right job. But the stakes differ: the applicant risks losing a chance unseen; the recruiter must keep work moving and explain the tool; the owner must keep the company running and answer for the choice. Power is uneven: the applicant cannot choose, the recruiter operates but does not buy, and the owner decides. New York City requires a recent fairness check and notice, limiting what owners can buy, recruiters can run, and applicants can accept. Amazon stopped a tool after old resumes led it to rate women lower. Outside rules and past failures change each person’s choices, so their reasons cannot simply be added.

## What This Changes

The question is not simply humans versus machines. It is about who gets a fair chance, who can question a hidden judgment, and who must carry the decision when speed meets risk. AI moves through a chain of unequal power, while outside rules and past failures shape what each person can do.
