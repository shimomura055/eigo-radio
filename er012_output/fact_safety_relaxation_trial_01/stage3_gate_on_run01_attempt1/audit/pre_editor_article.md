# When AI Reads the Job Application, Three People See Three Different Risks

## The Question

A job application can now pass through a machine before it reaches a human. A program may sort a CV, score personality, or judge a recorded interview through a candidate’s voice, face, and gestures. The same screen can feel like a time-saving tool, a fairness test, or a door that closes without an explanation.

### The applicant: speaking to the camera

I send in my CV and speak to a camera, knowing software may score my tone, face, and gestures. I want my skills and character seen, not reduced to a score I cannot question. I could do well on a skills test, yet a low mark on my expression could end the process. I cannot ignore a woman’s account of that experience and a long period without work. I also cannot ignore an applicant given no chance to opt out or appeal after a “trustworthiness” score.

### Another voice: the recruiter’s busy screen

After I use screening software on a large stack of CVs, I still answer people who ask how they were judged. I need the tool to match skills to jobs, but I also need to explain its role. I may have to prepare a bias-audit summary or transparency notice. I saw a public notice from NBCUniversal showing that an independent review of an employment tool had been carried out. I did not choose the system, but I must operate it carefully. I also have to judge what an AI-written CV means.

### A third voice: the business owner’s dashboard

Each week I check the dashboard for hiring time and cost. I ask whether we can fill jobs fast enough. AI is tempting because reported cases show hiring moving from weeks to days, while others report lower costs. I feel relief when my team fills roles on time. But I also think about the legal and ethical risk if an AI decision harms people. If that becomes public, my name is attached to the choice. I am protecting the company and trust in my judgment.

## Why They See It Differently

All three want the right person in the right job, but their stakes differ. The applicant may lose a chance unseen; the recruiter must balance speed and explanation; the owner must keep the company running and answer for the result. Power is uneven: the applicant cannot set the process, the recruiter operates but does not choose the tool, and the owner chooses and carries responsibility.

Outside limits turn these differences into hard boundaries. In New York City, an employer must arrange a yearly bias audit and notify applicants. That adds work for the recruiter and limits a speed-only choice by the owner. Amazon’s team stopped an internal tool after it rated women’s resumes lower. Because of rules and past failure, these three views cannot simply be added together.

## What This Tells Us

The question is bigger than whether a machine is good or bad at hiring. It is about who gets seen, who must explain the judgment, and who carries the consequences when a fast decision is questioned. AI creates a shared process, but not a shared risk. The same screen can therefore mean three different things.
