# Three Lives Behind an AI Hiring Decision

## The Question

An application is sent. A recorded interview begins. Behind the screen, software may sort a résumé, score a test, or judge a voice, face, and gestures. The same process touches three very different lives: the person seeking work, the person running the hiring process, and the owner who must live with the decision.

### The Applicant: Speaking to the Camera

I speak into a camera while software watches my voice, face, and gestures. I can do well on a skills test and still be rejected after AI gives low scores to my gestures and facial expressions. I may face a long period without work. I may also be asked to accept a score for “reliability and honesty,” with no chance to refuse or challenge it. I do not control the tool. I want a fair chance—and a reason I can understand.

### Another Voice: The Recruiter at the Screen

After the applications arrive, I open the screening tool. It helps me sort résumés, arrange interviews, write job posts, and match people with roles. I need that help when the list is long. But I cannot stop at a score. I may need to prepare a summary of how the tool was checked and a clear notice for candidates. I also have to judge an application written with AI: does it show useful skill, or too little effort? I run the tool, but I do not decide whether the company adopts it.

### A Third Voice: The Business Owner’s Dashboard

In the documented business cases, relevant efficiency measures can include how long hiring takes and what it costs. When some case studies report that hiring can sometimes happen in days and costs may fall, I can feel pressure to keep using the tool. If hiring slows, I worry about keeping the business running. If an AI decision is called discriminatory, I know the question will return to my desk. I am protecting the company and the trust placed in my judgment.

## Why They See It Differently

All three want the right person in the right job, but each risks something different: the applicant may lose the chance to be fairly seen; the recruiter must keep work moving and explain the tool; the owner must keep the company running and answer for the decision. Their power is unequal: the applicant cannot choose the process, the recruiter runs it without choosing adoption, and the owner chooses and carries the result. In this discussion, outside checks and past failures add important context, but they may not by themselves settle the competing concerns into one answer. In New York City, covered tools need a yearly check for unfair patterns and a notice. This gives the recruiter more to explain, while the owner may need to weigh speed against compliance and other legal or reputational risks. One company stopped an internal system after learning that it rated some women’s résumés lower. The applicant still cannot change the process, while the other two face these limits before anyone has the last word.

## What This Question Really Means

This is not only a question about whether a machine can sort people quickly. It is a question about who gets to define a fair decision when power is uneven: who is judged, who must explain the process, and who must answer for using it. AI makes hiring a test of opportunity and responsibility at the same time—not just a test of speed.
