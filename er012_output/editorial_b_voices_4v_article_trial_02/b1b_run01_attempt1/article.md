# When AI Screens the CV, Who Carries the Result?

## The Question

A job seeker sends a CV and records a video interview. Software scans the application, scores answers, and may read a voice, face, or gesture as a signal. A recruiter sees a faster list of candidates. A business leader sees time and cost. Later, a lawyer asks whether the process was fair and could be checked. The tool is the same. The stakes are not.

### The applicant’s voice: a chance that cannot be explained

I send my CV and record a video. I know software may judge my voice, face, or gestures, as well as my skills. I want a fair chance and a reason I can understand if I am rejected. I cannot forget one reported case: a woman passed a skills test but received a low video score for her gestures and expression, followed by long-term unemployment. I also know of an applicant who sued after being scored for trustworthiness without a way to opt out or challenge the result. I cannot choose the system.

### Another voice: the recruiter in the middle

I open a screening tool after hundreds of applications arrive. It helps sort CVs and move people toward suitable jobs. But speed does not end the conversation. I may need to explain an independent check for bias and prepare documents for worried applicants. I can see this in a public notice about hiring tools. That duty is already part of real hiring work. I did not choose every company rule, but I must operate the tool and answer questions.

### A third voice: the business dashboard

I watch the hiring dashboard: days to fill a job, money spent, and how many applications the team can handle. I see reported cases where hiring time fell from weeks to days, with lower costs in some companies. That is hard to ignore when a business needs people quickly and at scale. But if the system treats a group unfairly, I also face legal trouble and damage to the company’s reputation. I am protecting speed, but also the company’s ability to keep operating.

### A fourth voice: the fairness check

I read audit summaries, public notices, and old case records. I have seen one company stop an internal hiring tool after learning that it used past résumés and rated résumés linked to women lower. I also see rules moving at different speeds. New York City requires a recent independent bias check for tools covered by its law. The EU treats certain AI systems used in hiring and employment, particularly those that can significantly affect decisions, as high risk. I see that As of September 2023, Japan did not yet have an equivalent law requiring audits. I am asking whether anyone can inspect the process when a person is harmed.

## Where the Pressure Meets

The disagreement is not only about whether AI is accurate. It is about where uncertainty is allowed to sit. An applicant carries it as a possible lost opportunity. A recruiter carries it as a question to answer after the score is produced. A business leader carries it beside a clock and a budget. A fairness reviewer carries it into audits and legal records, sometimes after the tool has already been used. The same uncertainty feels personal, operational, financial, or legal because each person meets it at a different point.

## What This Changes

The question is no longer only whether a machine can sort applications faster. It is also who carries the result when a score opens a door, closes it, or cannot be explained. AI in hiring is therefore a question about control: who is judged, who must explain the judgment, who counts the cost, and who checks it afterward.
