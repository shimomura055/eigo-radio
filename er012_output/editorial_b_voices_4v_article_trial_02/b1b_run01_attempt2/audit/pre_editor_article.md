# When a Hiring Decision Starts with a Machine

## The Question

A candidate sends a résumé, completes a video interview, and waits. Software may read the résumé, score a test, or judge a voice, face, or gesture. The candidate may not know what mattered most. Around that decision are four people: the person being judged, the recruiter using the tool, the business counting time and cost, and the people checking fairness and the law.

### The applicant’s voice: A score I cannot question

I speak to a camera while software watches my tone, face, and gestures. I want my skills seen, not reduced to a score I cannot question. I have seen a reported case where a skills test went well, yet a video system marked down the applicant’s expressions; long-term unemployment followed. I also know of an applicant who sued CVS Health after an AI “reliability and honesty” score came without an opt-out or appeal. Nearly half—49%—of working US job seekers see such tools as more biased than human recruiters.

### Another voice: The recruiter in the middle

I open the screening tool because a large stack of applications will not wait. AI helps me sort résumés, arrange interviews, and match people with jobs. But when a candidate questions a score, I must explain the tool and prepare fairness documents. I have seen NBCUniversal publish notice of an independent review of its employment tool. I also ask: is an AI-written résumé a useful skill, or a sign of less effort?

### A third voice: The business case

I look at the hiring plan and ask whether the company can bring in enough people quickly, without letting costs grow. I have seen reported cases at Hilton and a global cloud company where hiring moved from weeks toward days after automation. That speed is hard to ignore. But unfair treatment can bring legal trouble and damage the company’s reputation. I am protecting growth while carrying the risk of a public failure.

### A fourth voice: The fairness check

I open an audit file and ask: can anyone check how this decision was made? I see Amazon’s case in the record: an internal hiring tool learned from old résumés and rated signs of women’s experience lower. I also see HireVue’s settlement over alleged biometric privacy violations. Rules differ by place. New York City requires audits and notice for covered tools, while the EU treats hiring AI as high risk. I am trying to protect fair treatment when the record is hard to see.

## Why They See It Differently

The conflict is not simply pro-AI versus anti-AI. The applicant may lose the opportunity itself. The recruiter must keep work moving and answer for a tool they did not create. The business watches speed and scale, but also the cost of legal and public failure. The fairness reviewer may arrive after use has begun, asking for records that are hard to find. Each person faces a different risk in the same chain.

## What This Question Really Measures

Seen this way, the question is larger than whether AI can sort applications. It is about where judgment sits, who can question it, and who bears the loss when a score closes a door. The machine may enter the process, but the weight still falls on people.
