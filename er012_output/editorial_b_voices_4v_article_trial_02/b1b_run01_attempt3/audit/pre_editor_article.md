# When a Machine Helps Decide Who Gets Seen

## The Question

A job applicant sends a résumé, records a video answer, and waits. Somewhere else, software may scan the document, score a personality test, or read a face and voice. The company sees a faster path through a crowded process. The person on camera may see a silent judge. One system now carries four stories: being judged, using the tool, paying for results, and checking fairness.

### The applicant at the camera

I send my résumé, then sit before a camera and try to sound natural. My tone, face, and gestures may become scores. I want a fair chance to show what I can do, and a reason if I am rejected. A woman once did well on a skills test, but a video score marked down her gestures and expressions. She later faced long-term unemployment. I have no clear way to challenge such a score.

### The recruiter in the middle

I use software to sort résumés, arrange interviews, and help write job ads. When hundreds arrive, that help matters. HR leaders are already deep into this shift: 91% say they use AI somewhere in hiring. But the work does not end with a shortlist. I may have to prepare an audit summary for people who distrust the tool. I also judge whether an AI-written résumé shows skill or too little effort.

### The business leader watching the clock

I watch the hiring dashboard: time, cost, and the number of people one team can handle. The attraction is clear. In one hotel case, hiring reached five days instead of weeks. That speed can help a business grow. But unfair treatment can bring legal trouble and public damage. I am not only buying speed; I am accepting responsibility for what it produces.

### The fairness lawyer checking the record

I open audit records and ask what happened before a score reached a hiring decision. Old résumés can carry old prejudice into a new system. I cannot forget an internal tool that rated signs of women’s experience lower after learning from past résumés. It was stopped. Rules differ by place: New York City requires audits and notice, while the EU treats hiring AI as high-risk. Japan has no equivalent audit law yet.

## Why They See It Differently

These pressures do not line up. The applicant risks losing the chance itself. The recruiter stands between a useful shortcut and a difficult explanation. The business measures time, cost, and scale, while fearing public failure. The fairness and legal role looks for records and rules, sometimes after a tool is in use. The same score becomes personal loss, daily work, a business bet, or an accountability question.

## What This Really Changes

The deeper issue is not simply whether a machine can sort applications. It is who defines a fair chance, who carries the cost when a score is wrong, and whether the decision can be understood after it changes someone’s path. AI has entered hiring, but responsibility has not become automatic.
