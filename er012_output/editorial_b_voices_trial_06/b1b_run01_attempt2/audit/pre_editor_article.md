# One Office, Two Ideas of Home

## The Question

Every morning, one employee arrives and looks for an empty desk. Another walks in and chooses a quiet corner, or a place near other people. Both use the same office, but they are not looking for the same thing. Some companies are bringing back assigned desks, while others continue with shared seating. The question is not simply which system wins. It is why the same office can feel reassuring to one employee and restrictive to another.

### One Voice: The employee looking for a place to return to

Every morning, I scan the room for an empty desk. I may sit at a keyboard someone else used, then wonder where to put my papers and personal things. Yesterday’s view and today’s neighbor may both be different.

I used to open the same drawer and begin work without thinking. That fixed desk gave me a small sign that I belonged here. Now the office can feel borrowed.

Even in a free-choice system, 36.8 percent of hot-desk users said their seats tend to become fixed. I understand why. When moving is tiring, people quietly create a place of their own.

### Another Voice: The employee who chooses the day’s setting

Some mornings, I need quiet, so I choose a quiet corner. On another day, I want to talk with people from another team, and I sit where conversation is easier.

If a relationship feels uncomfortable, changing desks gives me distance without making a big event of it. Working from home showed me what my own work setting can feel like. I no longer need the office to give me one permanent place.

I value changing the setting around my task, my mood, or the people nearby. A different desk does not feel like losing my place. It feels like choice.

## Why They See It Differently

The disagreement is not really about furniture. It is about where comfort and control come from. For one employee, comfort grows from sameness: the same drawer, the same view, and a place that does not have to be found again. For the other, control comes from movement: choosing quiet or company, and creating distance when needed.

People with assigned seats reported belonging at 87 percent and concentration at 80 percent in one large workplace survey, higher than people without assigned seats. In a TOKYO MX street interview, 40 of 50 office workers accepted free address, pointing to choice and personal distance.

These results do not decide every office. They show why one policy can feel safe to one worker and freeing to another.

## What This Tells Us

After hearing both voices, “fixed or free” looks less like a contest between old and new ideas. It becomes a question about what helps a person begin work: the security of returning to one place, or the freedom to choose a different place. The same empty desk can feel like a loss of belonging—or a welcome way to move.
