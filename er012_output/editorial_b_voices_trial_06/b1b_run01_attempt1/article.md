# Fixed Seats or Free Seating? The Same Office, Two Ways to Feel at Home

## The Question

Each morning, an employee walks into the office, looks for an empty desk, and puts down a bag. On another day, a colleague chooses a quiet corner instead. Both work in the same office, but a desk means something different to each of them. Some companies are bringing back assigned seats, while others keep shared desks. The question is why the same workplace can feel settled to one employee and limiting to another.

### One Voice: A worker who needs a home base

Every morning, I look for a desk no one is using. Sometimes I sit where someone else worked yesterday, beside a keyboard that is not mine. I have nowhere to leave papers or personal things, so work begins with searching and packing away.

What I miss is not furniture. It is arriving and starting without making a daily decision. The same drawer, view, and nearby team make work predictable. When they change each day, the office can feel like a place I visit, not a place where I belong. I do not get to choose my seat. About 87 percent of workers with assigned seats in one large survey said they felt they belonged at work.

### Another Voice: A worker who needs room to move

I come in and choose my desk for the day. For deep focus, I look for a quiet corner. For conversation, I sit where people are moving and talking. If I need distance from a workplace relationship, changing seats gives me space without an explanation.

After working from home, I learned how to make a work area that suits me. I do not need one permanent spot at the office. I value changing pace, meeting people from other teams, and choosing the setting for today’s task. A return to fixed seats would take away that choice.

## Why They See It Differently

These are not simply two tastes in furniture. For one worker, a stable place supports a quick, familiar start. For the other, choice supports control over the day.

Even free seating can become almost fixed in daily life. About 37 percent of free-seating workers in one survey said their seats tended to become fixed. Papers, power, and nearby coworkers were among the reasons. So “free” does not tell us how the office is actually lived. For one person, belonging comes from a place that stays. For another, it comes from choosing where to work—and where not to sit.

## What This Tells Us

After hearing both voices, the desk question looks less like a fight between old and new rules. It is about what makes work feel workable: a stable place to return to, or the freedom to choose a different place today. The same office can feel secure to one employee and open to another. That difference is the heart of the issue.
