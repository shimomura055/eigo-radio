# When AI Sits Between a Job and a Person

## The Question

A job application may go through software before a person sees it. Software may check a résumé. It may score a personality test. It may judge a video by a person's face, voice, and movements. The applicant sees a chance for a job. The recruiter sees many applications waiting. The owner sees a choice that can affect the company. The same screen gives three people three different experiences.

### The Applicant's Voice: Under the Camera

I speak to a camera. Software scores my voice, face, and movements. I read about a woman who did well on a skills test. But AI gave her low scores for her movements and facial expressions. Then the company rejected her. She was without work for a long time.

I know about another applicant. This person said AI scored their reliability and honesty. They had no way to say no to the system. They also had no way to challenge the result. I want people to see my skills and character. I do not want them changed into one score.

### Another Voice: The Recruiter

I start with résumés and an AI tool. It sorts them. It sets interview times. It matches people with jobs. It helps when applications come faster than my team can read them.

But a score is not the end of my work. I may prepare a simple summary of a fairness check. I may answer candidates' questions. One company's public notice shows this duty clearly.

I also think about résumés written by AI. Is this a useful skill? Or did the person make too little effort? I run the tool. But I do not decide if the company uses it.

### A Third Voice: The Business Owner

I often look at the hiring dashboard. It shows time, cost, and the people we still need. If hiring slows, the work does not stop.

I have seen reports of faster hiring and lower costs after using AI. That is why I may keep using it. But I heard that one tool gave women lower scores. So I look again at my own choice. If people question this choice, my name is attached. I am protecting the company's future. But I also carry the risk of being wrong.

## Why They See It Differently

All three want the right person in the right job. But each person faces a different problem. The applicant may lose a fair chance. The recruiter must keep work moving and explain the system. The owner must keep the company running and answer for the choice.

The applicant cannot choose the system. The recruiter runs it and may help with the decision to use it. The owner may make the final choice. Rules outside the company limit all three roles.

In New York City, an employer must have a recent fairness check. It must also give the public a simple summary and give notice before using an automated hiring tool. The recruiter cannot use it in secret. The owner cannot rely on speed alone. The applicant still cannot control the score. One company stopped a tool after it gave women lower scores. These different views do not give one easy answer.

## What This Question Really Means

This is not only about a machine reading a résumé or a face. It is about who decides what a fair chance means. It is about who must explain the system. It is about who must deal with the results if the system fails.

Hiring is not only a choice about a tool. It is also about power, proof, and responsibility.
