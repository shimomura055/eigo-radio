Write a natural English news article for A2-level learners, based on the same story told in the source article below.

Your goal is NOT to reduce how much of the story is told — keep the same core facts as the source: what happened, who was involved, why it matters, and the key numbers or examples. Your goal is instead to make the article easy to understand by ear, on a single listen, while still reading as natural, real English (not stiff "Controlled English" or robotic textbook English).

FACTS AND INFORMATION (do not change from previous guidance):
Do not remove a fact just because it seems too advanced for A2 — express it more simply instead, using more sentences if needed. The Full Story (the title, introduction, and body text before "Today's ... Points") must include the primary facts on its own: what happened, who was involved, why it matters, and key numbers/examples. A listener who only hears the Full Story should already understand the core news. Point One and Point Two must not simply repeat the Full Story and must not be where the main facts appear for the first time — they should go deeper (background, meaning, significance, a notable detail).

SENTENCES:
Aim for an average sentence length of about 11 words or fewer. Do not write any single sentence longer than 18 words. Each sentence should express one clear idea. Prefer simple subject-verb-object sentences. Avoid relative clauses. Do not use participial phrases. Avoid complex passive voice; prefer active voice unless the agent is unknown or unimportant. Use the present perfect and past perfect only when truly necessary. Avoid the subjunctive mood and hypothetical conditionals.

VOCABULARY — NEW, STRICT RULE:
Across the ENTIRE article, use no more than 5 general-purpose words that are above A2 level (harder than everyday, high-frequency vocabulary). This 5-word limit does NOT include:
- Proper nouns (names of people, places, organizations, events).
- A small number of specialist/news-specific words that are truly essential to understanding this story and cannot be simplified without losing important meaning — treat each such word as a "Key Phrase candidate" and use it only when genuinely necessary.
Everywhere else, actively choose the simplest, most everyday word that keeps the same meaning. If you are unsure whether a word is simple enough, prefer the simpler alternative.

ABSTRACT VS. CONCRETE — NEW RULE:
Prefer telling the listener WHO DID WHAT over describing things with abstract nouns. For example, instead of "The policy changed greatly in only 24 hours," prefer something like "Trump changed the plan one day later." Instead of "The results show that default settings can guide people's choices," prefer something like "Many children kept the first setting they saw." Words like policy, measure, approach, behavior, result, investment, tension, risk, choice, strategy, concern, and impact are not banned, but check each time whether a concrete "who did what" sentence would communicate the same fact more clearly. Never sacrifice factual accuracy for concreteness.

NUMBERS — NEW RULE:
Keep every number that matters to the story; do not remove numbers and do not round them. But as a rule, put only ONE number in each sentence — do not stack several numbers (dates, percentages, prices, counts) into one sentence. Split them into separate sentences instead. Exception: a single tightly-bound number expression may stay together in one sentence — for example an age range ("16 and 17"), a score ("2–1"), or a time range ("9 p.m. to 7 a.m.", "midnight to 6 a.m."). Do not force an unnatural split of these tightly-bound expressions.

PROPER NOUNS — NEW RULE:
Proper nouns are not counted in the 5-word vocabulary limit, but they still add listening load, so reduce unnecessary density. If a specific person's name is not itself important to the story, consider a general description instead (for example, "an England player" instead of a minor player's surname). After a person is introduced once by full name, you may refer to them by a shorter, natural form afterward (for example, first name only) if that reads naturally. Do NOT do this for people whose identity itself matters to the story (for example, keep "Messi" and "Trump" as named individuals throughout). Never blur who did what just to cut a name.

SPOKEN-FIRST STYLE — NEW RULE:
Write sentences that are easy to follow by ear, not just easy to read. Prefer: the subject appears early; the verb appears early; avoid opening a sentence with a long prepositional phrase or a long noun phrase before the main subject and verb; avoid sentences where the listener must hold everything in mind until the very end to understand the meaning. This is a style preference, not a strict grammar rule — the article must still read as natural spoken-style English, not simplistic or choppy "textbook" English.

RULES EXPLICITLY NOT USED THIS TIME (do not follow these overly strict versions):
- Do not limit each sentence to exactly one new idea/concept (this would be stricter than "one message per sentence" and could make the English unnatural).
- Do not mechanically repeat every important noun instead of using a pronoun — natural pronouns and paraphrase are fine, as long as the reference stays clear.
- Do not restrict yourself to a narrow set of sentence patterns (like only "X is Y" or "X did Y") — keep natural English variety.

Use straight apostrophes (') and straight quotation marks throughout the article, including inside the required heading text — not typographic/curly quotes (’ “ ”). This applies especially to the "Today's ... Points" heading, which must use a straight apostrophe exactly as shown below.

Do not wrap the "In One Line" sentence in markdown bold (**) or any other markdown symbol, and do not end the article with a markdown symbol as the very last character. Write "In One Line" as a plain sentence ending in normal punctuation (a period).

Do not write childish or overly simplified English that loses the news value of the story. Do not do any web search; use only the source article below.
