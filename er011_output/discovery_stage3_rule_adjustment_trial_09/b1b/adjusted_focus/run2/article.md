# The Small Refrigerator Setting That Can Help Produce Stay Fresh

Two crisper drawers can look almost identical, but they do not create the same air. One is a low-humidity drawer, with a vent that lets in more outside air. The other is a high-humidity drawer, with a structure that is more nearly sealed. That small difference changes what happens to fresh produce.

As fruit matures, it releases ethylene gas. If other produce stays exposed to that gas, it can ripen too quickly and spoil sooner. In a ventilated, low-humidity drawer, foods that release a lot of ethylene can let the gas escape, so nearby produce may keep longer.

High humidity serves a different purpose. Some refrigerated produce loses water quickly. Its leaves droop, and the food begins to wilt. A more closed drawer helps hold moisture around that produce.

So the real story is not a simple “fruit here, vegetables there” rule. The setting depends on the problem the food is most likely to face: ethylene affecting nearby produce, or water loss causing wilting. The two drawers are small tools, but they have different jobs.

### The question is what the food is doing

For produce that belongs in the refrigerator, ask one practical question: Is it mainly releasing ethylene, or mainly losing water? Apples and pears are examples for the low-humidity drawer. Leafy greens, such as kale, and broccoli fit the high-humidity side because they can lose water and wilt quickly. This behavior-based rule is more useful than “fruit” or “vegetable” alone.

### First decide where it belongs

But the humidity choice comes second. First decide whether the food should be refrigerated at all. Tomatoes and bananas are recommended for counter storage, not the refrigerator. Potatoes, sweet potatoes, onions, and garlic need a cool, dry place. So do not put tomatoes or bananas in the low-humidity drawer just because they release ethylene.

## In one line…

Freshness starts with two choices: give the produce the right storage place, then choose the drawer that matches its main risk. The best guide is not simply what the food is called, but what it does after it is stored.
