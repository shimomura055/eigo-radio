# The Refrigerator Drawer Has Two Jobs

Open the crisper drawer in a refrigerator, and you may find two settings: low humidity and high humidity.

They look like small controls, but they create two different storage spaces. The low-humidity drawer has a vent that allows more air to move through it. The high-humidity drawer is more enclosed.

At first glance, this may seem like a simple fruit-and-vegetable system. But fresh food does not all lose quality in the same way. Some foods wilt as they lose water. Others release a gas as they mature, and that gas can make nearby produce ripen too quickly.

That is why official food-storage guidance focuses on a food’s main risk, not only on whether it is called a fruit or a vegetable.

Used correctly, the drawer can help fresh produce stay in good condition longer. The slider creates a better setting for different kinds of food.

### Match the setting to the food’s main risk

The practical question is not simply, “Is this a fruit or a vegetable?” Apples and pears release a lot of ethylene gas, so they fit the ventilated, low-humidity side. Leafy vegetables such as kale, and vegetables such as broccoli, can lose water quickly, so they fit the more enclosed, high-humidity side. This gives you a simple rule when the label alone is not enough.

### Sometimes the right answer is no drawer

But some foods should not be placed in either crisper setting. Bananas and tomatoes release ethylene, but official guidance recommends keeping them at room temperature, such as on the counter, instead of refrigerating them. Potatoes, sweet potatoes, onions, and garlic do not need refrigeration. They are better kept in a cool, dry place. First choose the storage place, then choose a drawer setting.

## In one line…

A crisper drawer works best when you ask two questions: what threatens the food’s quality, and does it belong in the refrigerator at all? That pause can turn a confusing slider into a more useful storage choice.
