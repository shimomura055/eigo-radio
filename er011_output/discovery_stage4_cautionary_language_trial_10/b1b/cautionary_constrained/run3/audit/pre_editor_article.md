# The Small Refrigerator Choice That Gives Fresh Produce More Time

A small slider inside the refrigerator can be easy to ignore. Yet it separates two different storage spaces: a low-humidity drawer and a high-humidity drawer.

In many refrigerators, the two drawers may look almost the same. But the air inside them moves differently. A low-humidity drawer lets more air in, while a high-humidity drawer is closer to closed.

That difference matters when fresh produce is stored. The drawer is not simply a place for anything labeled “fruit” or “vegetable.” It creates a different environment around the food.

Used with the right kind of produce, these settings can help food stay fresh longer. The useful question is not only, “Is this a fruit or a vegetable?” It is also, “What is happening to this food as it starts to lose quality?”

### Look at how the food changes

The better rule is to ask what is making the food deteriorate. Apples and pears release substantial amounts of ethylene, so the low-humidity drawer helps that gas escape. Leafy greens such as kale, and vegetables such as broccoli, lose water and wilt quickly, so high humidity is a better fit. This rule is more useful than sorting food by name alone.

### Sometimes the drawer is the wrong place

But sometimes humidity is not the decision to make. Tomatoes and bananas are recommended for room-temperature storage, so they should not be used as low-humidity drawer examples. Potatoes, sweet potatoes, onions, and garlic need a cool, dry place; they are not simply the same as counter-stored foods.

For strawberries and oranges, commercial storage figures of 90–95% humidity do not automatically mean a household high-humidity drawer. Official appliance guidance differs, so these foods should not be placed under one universal fruit rule.

## In one line…

The right drawer depends on how the food loses quality—but first, make sure that food belongs in the refrigerator at all.
