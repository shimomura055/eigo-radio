# Japan’s Young Travelers Want More Time to Choose — But Trips Are Still Short

As of September 2026, a clear preference is emerging among some young people in Japan, especially young men. Travel is not only about seeing as many places as possible. More young travelers are showing interest in trips that leave room for their own interests and their own pace.

A 2025 JTBF survey found that men aged 29 and under ranked solo travel and hobby-focused travel among their top choices. Each was chosen by about one in four people. In a separate survey by the Japan Tourism Agency, about 90 percent of Japanese Gen Z respondents wanted free time during an overseas tour. About four in five of them wanted half a day or more.

But the calendar tells a less relaxed story.

When people were asked what they would do if they could take a month off, the most common answer was a trip of about one week. It was chosen by 24.1 percent. Yet among people planning an autumn trip, the average stay was only 1.8 nights. The median was 2.0 nights.

The Tourism Agency’s 2025 white paper still lists longer stays as a policy challenge. So the wish for slower, more personal travel is visible, but it has not become a clear change in how long people stay.

And the shift is not the same for everyone. Among women aged 29 and under, nearly 45 percent were still interested in visiting famous tourist sites. The picture is mixed, not a simple move from sightseeing to long stays.

### A trip can be partly self-directed

The practical meaning of this preference is not “never join a tour” or “skip every famous place.” A young traveler may still choose both, while keeping one part of the schedule for personal use. That might be a half-day of solo exploring or time for a hobby. The emerging model is a flexible mix, not an all-or-nothing choice.

### Slow and long are different measures

The duration figures should not be treated as one test of whether travel is becoming slower. One measure asks what people would choose under a month-long break. The other records plans being made now. A short trip may still allow a personal pace, while a hoped-for week may never happen. The evidence points to two tasks, not one verdict: support longer stays and improve the short trips people already take.

## In one line…

The direction is clear: many young travelers, especially men, want more control over their time. But the evidence shows an emerging preference, not a completed shift to long stays.
