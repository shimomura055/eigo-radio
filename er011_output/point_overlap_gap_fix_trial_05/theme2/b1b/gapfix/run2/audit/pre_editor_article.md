# Japan’s Young Travelers Want More Time. Their Trips Are Still Short

At first glance, Japan’s young travelers may seem to be moving from famous sights to slow travel. But the evidence tells a more interesting story.

Many young people, especially young men, want more control over their travel time. They want to move at their own pace, follow their interests, and leave room in the schedule.

One sign comes from a Tourism Agency survey of 400 Gen Z respondents aged 19 to 25. About 90% said they wanted free time during an overseas tour. About 80% of that group wanted half a day or more.

This does not prove that they want to stay in one place for a long time. But it does show a strong wish for space and freedom inside a trip.

At the same time, surveys of planned travel still show short stays. The Tourism White Paper also says Japan needs to increase the length of stays. So the wish for slower travel is moving ahead of the actual travel schedule.

By September 2026, this is not a simple story of young people giving up sightseeing. It is a gap between what they want their trips to feel like and how long those trips are likely to be.

### A different map for young men and women

One important detail is how the pattern differs by gender. Among men under 30, solo travel and hobby-focused travel were top choices, at 25.2% and 24.3%. Among women under 30, visiting famous tourist sites still drew 44.7%. Slow travel is not one youth identity. For planners, it is a useful reason to offer flexible options without treating them as a universal replacement for sightseeing.

### The real test is the extra night

The sharper test is not what people imagine during a dream month, but how many nights appear in a travel plan. With a full month off, “about one week” was the most popular answer, at 24.1%. Yet people planning an autumn trip expected a median stay of 2.0 nights and an average of 1.8 nights. The next question is whether that wish becomes extra nights.

## In one line...

The direction is toward more personal control over travel time, but the evidence shows an emerging preference—not yet a settled change in the length of young people’s trips.
