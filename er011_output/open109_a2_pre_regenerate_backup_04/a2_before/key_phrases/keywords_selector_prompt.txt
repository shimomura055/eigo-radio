# The Phone Can Distract You Before You Look at It

Imagine sitting down to read. Your phone is face down. It makes no sound. You do not reach for it. It seems absent from the moment.

But concentration can be affected in two different contexts: when an alert is presented, and when the phone is nearby even when no notifications are received.

In a 2022 study, 73 university students did a visual letter task. Some trials had a phone notification sound. Others had a neutral sound. The students did not look at or touch a phone. Yet they responded significantly more slowly after the notification sound.

A second study in 2023 looked at the quiet time before any alert. Forty-two university students took a concentration test with a phone nearby and with it in another room. The phone was off, and no notification came. Attention and processing speed were both lower when the phone was on the desk.

Together, these studies show two different parts of the same story. An alert can disturb the moment. A nearby phone can shape the moment before it. This does not mean every person is affected in the same way outside these study settings. But it does show why “I never checked my phone” is not the whole story.

Daily reports show why this matters. In a 2018 survey of U.S. teens aged 13 to 17, 72% said they often or sometimes checked messages or notifications soon after waking. About four in ten said they felt anxious, upset, or lonely when their phone was not nearby. A majority also felt they needed to reply quickly. The report described this as a hard-to-resist habit, not a medical diagnosis.

### The cost you cannot see

Not checking the phone does not prove that the alert had no effect. A brain recording found a larger signal during notification sounds. Researchers linked this to extra mental control: the mind had to keep the task going after the alert pulled at attention. A person may continue working, but the task can still require more effort in that moment.

### Two ways to protect the work moment

The two studies point to two different targets. Silencing alerts deals with the sudden event. Putting the phone in another room deals with the quiet pressure of having it nearby. The second study directly tested this situation, but it did not compare muting with moving the phone. These are different responses, not a proven ranking.

## In one line…

A notification can take attention in a loud moment, while a nearby phone may quietly ask for attention before the sound begins.
