# A War That Is Becoming an Economic Endurance Test

For months, the question around the Strait of Hormuz was simple: can ships still pass?

Now, the harder question is this: can either side keep the pressure going?

The United States and Israel began air attacks on Iran on February 28. A ceasefire understanding in June briefly eased the blockade. But after Iran attacked three merchant ships outside routes approved by the United States, the blockade returned on July 14.

Since then, the conflict has not moved toward a clear finish. The United States has kept economic pressure on Iran. Iran has continued to challenge control of the strait. Talks have not produced a new agreement.

On August 18, President Trump said that no talks with Iran were planned. The 60-day period for talks also ended without an agreement. Eurasia Group, a private political-risk company, moved its expected easing of tensions from September to the end of the year.

This is why the conflict now looks less like a short battle and more like a long economic test. Reports point to damage inside Iran, danger around the shipping route, and a continuing diplomatic deadlock.

CNN reported that a month of relative calm was ending and that the region was moving back toward a dangerous military stalemate that may be hard to sustain.

But the picture is not simple. On September 2, Energy Secretary Chris Wright said that more than 17 million barrels of oil passed through Hormuz on August 31. He called it a wartime record and said Iran was losing its ability to disrupt the strait.

That claim points in one direction. Other evidence points in another.

### The numbers do not tell one story

Wright’s 17 million barrels was a one-day government figure. EIA’s 4.9 million barrels was an average for the second quarter. TankerTrackers.com estimated about 9.14 million barrels for August 31, using a wider sea area. The figures do not measure exactly the same thing. One day cannot prove a lasting recovery.

### A clear lane can still be a dangerous lane

The United States says it cleared the main shipping route, but official figures do not give a number. One report said more than 200 mine-like objects were removed, while only 11 were confirmed as mines. The route was still rated “SEVERE.” An Iranian opposition media outlet also reported a petrochemical shutdown and a loss of about 230 million cubic meters of daily gas capacity. Traffic can rise briefly while danger and damage remain.

## In one line…

The conflict appears to be moving toward a longer economic stalemate, but the evidence is mixed: long-term data show a sharp fall in oil traffic, while one recent government figure suggests a temporary improvement.
