# The Conflict That Keeps Going After the Pause

The most important change in the US-Iran conflict is not one new attack. It is the way the conflict is becoming a test of time.

Pressure now appears in three places: ships, energy, and diplomacy. That is why this story looks less like a short military event and more like a long economic struggle.

The conflict began on February 28, when the United States and Israel started air strikes on Iran. The United States began a blockade in April. Then, on June 14, the two sides agreed to a ceasefire memorandum. The blockade was lifted four days later.

But the pause did not solve the conflict. In July, Iran attacked three merchant ships outside routes approved by the US military. The blockade restarted on July 14.

That turn matters. A quiet period did not bring a clear peace. It was followed by another move against shipping.

By September, one major news report said about one month of calm had ended. The region was moving back toward a dangerous and hard-to-sustain military stalemate.

The main question is no longer simply who can strike next. It is whether either side can keep the pressure going without reaching an agreement. The answer is still unclear, but the costs are building over time.

### A number can change the picture

One day can look better than the longer story. The US energy secretary said more than 17 million barrels passed through Hormuz on August 31. But official data show a second-quarter average of about 5 million barrels a day, down from roughly 20 to 22 million before the war. Another estimate put that day's total across a wider area at about 9 million barrels. These figures are not a clean comparison, so one headline does not prove that normal traffic has returned.

### Damage does not disappear during a pause

A pause is not a reset. US forces said they cleared the main route, but one report said more than 200 mine-like objects were removed, including 11 confirmed mines. The threat level stayed “SEVERE” because danger remained. An opposition-linked report also said a petrochemical plant stopped and gas capacity had fallen by about 230 million cubic meters a day, though that figure was not independently checked. Talks produced no agreement, and a forecasting group moved its peace forecast to the end of the year. Real recovery would mean safe routes, working production, and a clear deal—not only fewer attacks.

## In one line…

The conflict is moving toward a longer economic stalemate, with strong signs of pressure but no clear proof that either side has won control.
