# The Small Refrigerator Setting That Helps Fresh Produce Last Longer

The two drawers in a refrigerator may look like simple storage boxes. But a small slider can change the air around fresh produce.

Many crisper systems offer two choices: low humidity and high humidity. A low-humidity drawer has more ventilation. A high-humidity drawer is closer to sealed. In other words, the refrigerator is creating two different storage spaces.

That matters because produce does not all behave the same way. As some fruit matures, it releases ethylene gas. If other fruit or vegetables stay exposed to that gas, they can ripen too quickly and spoil sooner. Other produce loses water quickly and starts to wilt.

So the setting is not just a label on a drawer. It is a way to match the storage space with the food. Used correctly, it can help fresh produce last longer.

But there is a catch. The familiar shortcut—fruit in one place, vegetables in the other—does not always work. To use the drawer well, you need to look at what the food releases and what it loses.

### Follow the food, not the label

Ask two questions. Does the item release a lot of ethylene and need ventilation? Apples and pears point to low humidity. Or does it lose water quickly and wilt? Leafy greens such as kale and broccoli point to high humidity. For an unfamiliar item, the useful test is simple: ventilation or keeping moisture in?

### The exceptions matter

That shortcut has traps. Strawberries and citrus fruits such as oranges are fruits, but they favor high humidity. UC Davis gives about 90 to 95 percent as the best range for both. Tomatoes and bananas should stay on the counter, not in either drawer. Potatoes, sweet potatoes, onions, and garlic need a cool, dry place instead—not exactly the same treatment as tomatoes and bananas.

## In one line…

A crisper drawer is not simply a fruit-versus-vegetable test. Match the setting to the food’s needs, and remember that a few everyday foods do not fit the usual shortcut.
