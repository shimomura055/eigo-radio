# The Strait Stays Open, but the War Keeps Draining Both Sides

A war can change shape without ending.

The conflict between the United States, Israel, and Iran began with airstrikes on February 28. Soon, the fight moved into the Strait of Hormuz, a key route for energy shipments. The United States began a blockade on April 13.

In June, the two sides agreed to a ceasefire memorandum, and the blockade was lifted on June 18. But the pause did not last. After Iran attacked three merchant ships outside approved routes in July, the blockade was restarted on July 14.

By August, the pressure was no longer only at sea. An Iranian opposition media outlet reported that a petrochemical plant had stopped production and that Iran had lost part of its natural gas production capacity. At the same time, the US Navy reported finding more than 100 objects believed to be mines in a traffic area of the strait.

The diplomatic path also became narrower. On August 18, President Trump said that no talks with Iran were scheduled. The 60-day period for negotiations then ended without an agreement. Eurasia Group moved its forecast for easing tensions from September to the end of 2026.

On September 4, CNN reported that a month-long calm was ending. The region, it said, was moving back toward a dangerous and increasingly unsustainable military stalemate.

But the evidence is mixed. A US energy official reported a strong one-day rise in oil traffic through the strait. That points in a different direction from the wider picture.

The result is not a simple march toward peace or total closure. It is a long struggle in which economic pressure appears to be building, while the evidence about control of the waterway still disagrees.

### One day is not a full recovery

Chris Wright said that more than 17 million barrels crossed the strait on September 1, a wartime record. But EIA statistics show an average of 4.9 million barrels a day in the second quarter, down from 20.4 to 21.6 million before the war. These figures are not interchangeable. A brief rebound is not yet normal trade.

### A partly working route can still sustain a stalemate

The strait does not need to be fully closed for trade to become costly and risky. Selective attacks, suspected mines, reported energy losses, and renewed blockade pressure can make normal commerce and a negotiated exit harder. With no scheduled talks and no agreement, partial access can exist without a clear way out.

## In one line…

The conflict is showing signs of a long economic war, but the evidence remains mixed: pressure is lasting, while the true level of disruption is still changing.
