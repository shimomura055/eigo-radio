# The Strait War Is Becoming a Long Economic Stalemate

At first, this looked like a war measured in air strikes. By September, the more important question was what could still move through the Strait of Hormuz.

The United States and Israel began air strikes on Iran on February 28. The United States then began checking and stopping ships in a blockade on April 13.

In June, the two sides agreed to a written plan to pause the fighting. The blockade was lifted on June 18. But the pause did not last. After Iran attacked three merchant ships outside approved routes in July, the blockade returned on July 14.

The pressure then reached shipping and energy systems. In August, the US Navy found more than 100 objects believed to be mines in a traffic lane in the strait. An opposition media group also reported that a petrochemical plant had stopped production and that Iran had lost a large amount of gas production capacity. That estimate has not been independently confirmed.

Diplomacy did not bring a clear way out. On August 18, President Trump said there were no talks planned with Iran. The 60-day period for negotiations ended without an agreement. One analysis group then moved its expected time for easing tensions from September to the end of the year.

On September 4, one report described the region as moving back toward a dangerous and difficult military stalemate. Together, these signs suggest that the conflict is becoming a long economic drain, not a short battle with a clear ending.

But the picture is mixed.

### One day is not a whole quarter

On September 1, Energy Secretary Chris Wright said more than 17 million barrels of oil crossed the strait in one day. He called it the highest wartime figure. But official data show averages of about 20 to 22 million barrels a day before the war, compared with 4.9 million in the second quarter of 2026. These figures do not measure the same period. The key question is whether the September rise continues through the third quarter.

### The damage is changing the ground

The deeper story is what is being worn down. Mines threaten shipping lanes, while reported damage to petrochemical and gas systems affects production. These pressures do not decide the war, but they change the conditions for a future deal. Even a ceasefire would not automatically return shipping and energy conditions to normal. The stalemate is also a process that can make recovery harder.

## In one line…

The conflict is pointing toward longer economic strain, but the evidence is still mixed: a deep quarterly fall stands beside a strong one-day rebound.
