# A War That Is Becoming an Economic Test

## Main Story

At first, the conflict looked like a direct military crisis.

The United States and Israel began air strikes on Iran on February 28, 2026. But by September, the story had changed. The main question was no longer only who was attacking whom. It was also how long shipping, energy, and diplomacy could keep working under pressure.

On June 14, the two sides agreed to a ceasefire memorandum. The blockade was lifted four days later. But the pause did not last.

In July, Iran attacked three merchant ships outside approved routes. The blockade began again on July 14.

After that, pressure built in several places. Reports described a petrochemical plant stopping production. An Iranian opposition media group also said that Iran had lost about 230 million cubic meters a day in natural gas production capacity. That number has not been independently confirmed.

The sea route was still dangerous. In August, the US Navy found more than 100 objects believed to be mines in a shipping area of the Strait of Hormuz.

Diplomacy did not open a clear path forward. On August 18, President Trump said that no talks with Iran were scheduled. A 60-day period for negotiations ended without an agreement. A later forecast moved its expected time for easing tensions from September to the end of the year.

On September 4, one report said that a month-long quiet period was ending. It described the region as moving back toward a dangerous and increasingly unsustainable stalemate.

But the numbers do not tell one simple story. A senior US energy official said that more than 17 million barrels of oil passed through the strait on September 1, the highest wartime level. Official US statistics, however, show that the average fell from more than 20 million barrels a day before the war to 4.9 million in the second quarter.

This is why the conflict now looks like a long economic struggle, even though its current direction remains uncertain.

### One day is not the whole picture

The two oil figures measure different periods. The 17-million-barrel figure covers one day. The official 4.9-million-barrel figure covers a three-month average. Both can be true. A busy day may show a short recovery, but it does not prove that shipping has returned to normal. The key question is whether higher flows continue.

### The damage can last after the headlines change

The deeper problem is restarting. Better ship traffic cannot quickly replace lost production capacity. The reported plant shutdown and gas losses may keep hurting Iran, while the US Navy is still clearing more than 100 suspected mines. Without an agreed way back to talks, the costs can continue even during a short period of calmer news.

## In one line...

The conflict appears to be moving toward a longer economic stalemate, but the latest evidence is mixed: one strong day at sea has not yet erased months of disruption.
