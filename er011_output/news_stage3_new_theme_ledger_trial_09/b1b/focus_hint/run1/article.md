# A New CAR-T Approach Builds the Cells Inside the Body

Where a treatment is made can change the whole story.

A paper describes a way to make CAR-T cells inside a patient’s body. It appeared online on September 2, 2026, and in the journal’s September 3 issue.

It reports an early-stage study of 16 people with hard-to-treat neurological autoimmune diseases. Seven had progressive multiple sclerosis. Three had MOG antibody-related disease, three had generalized myasthenia gravis, and three had idiopathic inflammatory muscle disease.

Each person received one dose through a vein. The dose delivered a CAR gene into the person’s own T cells. Those T cells then became CD19 CAR-T cells, designed to target B cells. The gene carrier was designed not to reproduce itself and to switch itself off.

This is the unusual part: the cells were made inside the body. They were not taken out, changed in a laboratory, and returned.

After treatment, B cells fell deeply in the blood, bone marrow, and fluid around the brain and spinal cord. Researchers reported improvements in nerve function, symptoms, disease scores, or blood tests across all four disease groups. In the seven people with progressive multiple sclerosis, these reports included better walking, hand movement, and thinking speed during a median follow-up of about six months.

The safety picture was active, but the reported reactions were limited in severity. Eleven of the 16 people developed grade 1 cytokine release syndrome, an immune reaction that included short-term fever in some cases. It appeared between days 5 and 28, and every case eased within two weeks. The study reported no immune-related nerve toxicity, low blood pressure, or low oxygen.

For now, this is an encouraging signal, not final proof. It was a small study with one treatment group and no comparison group.

### A possible temporary reset

The B-cell pattern may be the most important clue. B cells did not stay away forever. They began to reappear after about two months. Yet improvements in clinical measures and lab tests were reported during follow-up. That makes the treatment look less like permanent removal and more like a temporary reset of the B-cell system.

### The real test is time

The next question is not simply whether B cells fall. It is whether disease-specific gains remain after they return. Because this study had 16 people and no comparison group, it cannot answer that. A stronger follow-up would track each condition past B-cell recovery and compare it with a similar group without the treatment. If gains last, a one-time reset looks more practical; if they fade, repeat treatment or another way to extend the effect may be needed.

## In one line…

Following a single dose, CAR-T cells were generated inside the body, and a deep, but apparently temporary, fall in B cells was observed. The report offers an important signal, but its lasting value will depend on what happens after B cells return.
