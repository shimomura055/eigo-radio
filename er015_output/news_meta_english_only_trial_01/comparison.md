## 日本語Baseline(NEWS-META-SOURCE-VOLUME-FORMAT-TRIAL-01 条件2 R2)

AIに電話を頼んだら、舞台裏から「人間」が登場した

もしAIに電話を頼んで、相手と話を進めてもらっている間に、画面の外で人間スタッフが受話器を握っていたら――。そんな、少しだけコントのような仕組みを、Metaが試していました。

米Metaが開発する個人向けAIエージェント「Muse」。利用者に代わって電話をかける機能の社内試験で、一部の通話を人間の契約スタッフが担当していたことが分かりました。AIだけで電話をしているように見えて、その裏では人間が会話を支えていたのです。

この仕組みの名前は、「人間コンシェルジュ」。

名前だけ聞くと、ホテルのロビーで何でも相談に乗ってくれる人を想像します。でも実際には、AIの舞台裏にいるスタッフです。AIが相手の話をうまく理解できないときや、予想外の流れになったときに、人間が対応する。いわば、AIの着ぐるみに人間が入っているようなものです。

なぜ、そんな方法を試すのでしょうか。電話は、AIにとってなかなか手ごわい仕事だからです。相手の話し方は毎回違います。質問が急に変わることもあります。文章を作るのが得意なAIでも、リアルタイムの会話では、思わぬところで立ち止まるかもしれません。そこで人間が助ければ、電話代行はかなりスムーズになります。

ただ、AIの着ぐるみには、笑って済ませられない問題もありました。Metaの従業員から、通話内容が外部に流れることなど、プライバシーへの心配が出たのです。電話の向こうに誰がいるのか。AIなのか、人間なのか。それが分からないままだと、安心して話せない人もいるでしょう。

Reutersが確認した社内投稿によると、Metaの幹部はこの機能をいったん取りやめたと説明しました。

AIの世界では、「賢く見えること」だけでなく、「裏で誰が動いているか」も大事になりそうです。未来の電話は、AIが一人で働くのではなく、人間がこっそり助ける二人三脚になるのかもしれません。問題は、その相棒の存在を、いつ、どう知らせるかです。

## English Original(本Trial)

**The AI Calling for You May Have Been a Human After All**

Hey, isn’t this kind of interesting? We often imagine AI as a machine that can quietly handle small jobs for us. For example: “Call the store and ask if they have this jacket in my size.”

But what if the “AI” making the call is actually a human sitting somewhere behind the scenes?

That appears to be what Meta was testing with Muse, its personal AI agent. Reuters found internal company posts showing that some calls made through Muse were handled by human contract workers. These workers acted as a kind of hidden customer-service team, or “human concierge.”

So, instead of Muse understanding a difficult phone conversation on its own, a person could step in and do the job. The caller might think the AI had made the call. In reality, a human could be listening and speaking for it.

That detail changes the way we think about AI assistants. The impressive part may not always be the software. Sometimes, the software is more like a doorway to human help.

There is a practical reason companies might try this. Phone calls can be messy. People interrupt each other. They speak quickly, change the subject, or ask questions the AI was not trained to expect. A human can deal with those problems much more easily.

But there is also an obvious concern: privacy. If you ask an AI to make a call, you may expect a computer to handle it. You may not expect a contract worker to hear what you say, or to learn details about your plans, purchases, or personal life.

Reuters reported that Meta employees raised concerns about phone-call information being leaked outside the company. After those concerns, Meta executives said the company had rolled back the feature for now.

The bigger question is not simply, “Was this AI really AI?” New technology often uses people while it is still being developed. The important question is whether users know who is listening and who is doing the work.

An AI assistant can save time. But if a human is quietly helping, that is not automatically wrong. It just needs to be clear. Otherwise, the smooth, futuristic experience may depend on a secret that users would have wanted to know.

## English R1(本Trial)

**Plot Twist: The AI Calling the Store May Have Been a Person**

Imagine asking your AI assistant to call a store for you.

“Hi, do you have these shoes in size nine? And what time do you close?”

You expect a robot to make the call. Maybe it speaks in a calm, perfect voice. Maybe it sounds a little too calm and perfect.

Now for the unexpected part: the voice on the other end may have been connected to a human.

Meta was testing this idea with Muse, its personal AI agent. Reuters found internal company posts showing that some calls made through Muse were handled by human contract workers. Meta called this trial a “human concierge” service.

In other words, Muse could have a person quietly helping it make the call. The technology was still presented as an AI service, but some of the actual work was being done by humans behind the scenes.

It is a funny twist on the usual AI story. We are used to hearing that machines may replace people. Here, people were helping the machine look smarter.

And it makes sense why Meta might try it. Phone calls are full of small traps for software. People interrupt each other. They speak too fast. They suddenly ask a new question. A human can handle that kind of chaos much more easily than an AI.

But there is a serious problem hiding inside the clever idea: privacy.

If you ask an AI to make a call, you may think a computer is handling the conversation. You may not expect a contract worker to hear the call or deal with the information involved. That information could include details about what you are buying, where you are going, or what you are trying to arrange.

Reuters reported that Meta employees raised concerns about phone-call information leaking outside the company. Meta executives then said the feature had been rolled back for now.

The interesting question is not only whether Muse was “really” AI. New technology often uses human help while it is being developed. The bigger question is whether users are told clearly when a person is doing the listening and talking.

A hidden human may make an AI assistant work better. But if the whole point is convenience, finding out that your robot helper was secretly a person could be a rather awkward surprise.

# Muse, the AI Caller With a Human Plot Twist

Meet Muse, Meta’s AI agent. It can send emails, book travel, fill in forms, browse websites, and negotiate for its users. It can also call American businesses to book a haircut, check store stock, or ask several companies for prices.

That sounds like a tidy little future: tell the AI what you want, and let it handle the boring phone call.

Then the script takes a sharp turn.

During internal testing in August and September, some requests made through Muse were passed to trained human agents. Those humans made the phone calls instead. Internal messages described a system that would later give the user a record and summary of the conversation.

Why bring a human into an AI phone call? Because some people hung up after realizing they were speaking with an AI. Muse could say the words, but the other person might refuse to stay for the conversation. The machine had learned an awkward lesson: sounding human is not the same as being welcomed as a caller.

In some internal tests, calls handled by humans had a reported success rate of 95% to 98%. Reuters said AI-only calls performed worse, but did not provide an exact number. So the experiment became more than a test of speech technology. It became a test of trust, with Muse giving instructions backstage while a human took the stage.

### The surprise co-star was not ready for opening night

The human-concierge test was enabled for about half of Meta’s employees, and employees who did not want to take part could opt out. Reuters reported that Meta temporarily rolled the feature back after recognizing that contractor-made calls without proper disclosure were a problem. Meta said wider release would wait.

### The backstage helper raised privacy concerns

Some employees worried that personal or sensitive information could reach call-center workers. In one reported case, a call record contained an inappropriate racial remark by a human contractor. Meta said that contractor would not handle its cases again and said stronger privacy and disclosure protections were needed.

## In one line

Muse’s big phone lesson was that an AI may need a human co-star when people refuse to listen to the machine.
