# The Moon’s Wrinkle Case: The Key Is a “Rock Blanket”!

The Moon seems to have shrunk. The clue is the “wrinkles” etched into its surface. So, if we study how the Moon cooled, will we solve the case? But an unexpected thing came to light in the investigation: a thick “blanket” made of broken rocks. Moon, could your secret really be in your bedding?

First, let’s examine the scene. On the Moon’s surface, there are cliffs that are steep on one side and gentle on the other. They are thought to be marks left when one mass of rock pushed up over another. Don’t mistake them for mere steps in the ground. They are landform “wrinkles” that tell us the Moon shrank.

From these wrinkles, researchers estimated that the Moon’s radius shrank by about 42 to 46 meters during a later period in its history. Some studies date the formation of the wrinkles to about 300 to 400 million years ago. However, when they first began to form is not known for sure. And importantly, this is not the total amount the Moon has shrunk since it formed.

Now let’s open another file. Some models that calculate how the Moon cooled predict that the total shrinkage in its radius was at least about 750 meters. About 42 to 46 meters, and at least about 750 meters. Just looking at the numbers, they seem very different.

It’s too soon to slam the desk and shout, “The numbers don’t add up! Reopen the investigation!” The models deal with shrinkage that built up over time, while the wrinkles record shrinkage during a later period. They look at different time spans and use different methods. So we should not pit the numbers against each other as if they were measurements made under the same conditions.

So how did the Moon actually cool? To explain this difference, the research team points to one possibility: a thick layer of broken rock with lots of gaps. At last, the “blanket” we heard about makes its entrance.

A rock blanket doesn’t sound like a good way to sleep. It’s heavy. It sounds hard. Even rolling over would be a major event. But in this study, what matters is not comfort, but how well it keeps heat in. This layer may have helped keep heat from escaping.

If so, the Moon’s interior would cool less easily, and it would shrink more slowly. Rock layers are not all the same. The thickness of the layer and how many gaps it has make a difference. So, when thinking about how the Moon cooled, we must also consider “what it was covered with.”

Of course, this doesn’t mean “case closed: the rock blanket did it!” This is still a hypothesis. The time when the wrinkles began to form is also not known for sure, so it’s too early to close the case file.

Still, who would have thought that following the Moon’s wrinkles would lead us to investigate a blanket? The question has shifted from “How much did it shrink?” to “How did it let heat escape?” To uncover the Moon’s past, it seems we may need an insulation team, not a bedding team.

## In one line
Researchers suggest a thick, porous layer of broken rock may have insulated the Moon, slowing its cooling and shrinkage. This idea may help explain why estimates differ, though they cover different periods and use different methods, and it remains unproven.
