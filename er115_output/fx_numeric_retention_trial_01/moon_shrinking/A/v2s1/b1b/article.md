# The Moon’s “Wrinkle” Mystery: Is That Jacket Too Thick?

The Moon looks calm in the night sky. But its surface has “wrinkles.” Following the clues to find out why leads to a story that makes you want to ask, “What were you wearing?” The focus is a thick “jacket” made of broken rock.

The research team focused on a thick layer of broken rock with many gaps on the Moon’s surface. Rock that keeps heat in? This layer may act like insulation, making it harder for heat inside the Moon to escape. It may have slowed the Moon’s cooling and shrinking.

Now, let’s return to the scene of the wrinkles. The Moon’s surface has cliff-like shapes, with one side steep and the other gentle. They are thought to be traces of faults, where blocks of rock pushed against each other and one pushed up over the other. The Moon didn’t furrow its brow. These marks were left by blocks of rock pushing against each other.

Calculations based on maps of these faults and other information show that the Moon’s radius shrank by about 42 to 46 meters. If you thought, “At last, we know the Moon’s diet record!” pause here. This number is not the total amount the Moon shrank over its whole life.

It shows the shrinkage in a “later period,” recorded in relatively recent faults. And it is still not known for sure when the faults first began to form. It’s like reading pages from partway through a household budget book. You can’t work out a lifetime’s spending from that alone.

Meanwhile, some models that calculate the full history of the Moon’s cooling predict a much greater amount of shrinkage. But it is too soon to pit “the calculations” against “the Moon’s surface” and ask which is right. The total over a long history and the records left in young faults cover different time periods and use different methods.

So the rock jacket appears as one possible clue to the difference. But hold off on playing the “case solved” music and declaring, “Insulation was the culprit!” The research team only raised it as a possibility. The jacket’s role has not been confirmed.

Following the Moon’s wrinkles led us to a story about heat inside it and what it was wearing. The next time you look at the Moon, you may want to ask, “How long have you been wearing that jacket?” Even if it doesn’t answer, the familiar Moon may seem just a little more mysterious.

## In one line
A thick, porous layer of broken rock on the Moon may have acted like insulation, slowing its cooling and shrinkage. That could help explain why young faults record less shrinkage than some models of the Moon’s full history predict, but the idea remains unconfirmed.
