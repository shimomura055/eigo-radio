# Cockroach Milk and Nose-Blowing: Scientists Measure Even That

“Cockroach milk” and “nose-blowing research.” The words alone make you stop and look again. And both studies were chosen for the Ig Nobel Prize. It’s fine to laugh. But it’s too soon to leave just because of the names. The real interest lies in the scientists’ “You measure even that!?” attitude.

First, cockroach milk. If you got a glass ready, please quietly put it back in the cupboard. This isn’t milk for people to drink. The study looked at crystals of a protein that provides nutrients, found in the gut of baby cockroaches growing inside their mothers. The setting isn’t a cafe, but a baby cockroach’s gut. The bar for getting in is high right from the start.

Researchers used X-rays to study these tiny crystals. They looked at their three-dimensional shape. They found, among other things, that fats were joined to the protein and sugar was attached too. If you were making a fuss at the door, asking “What is milk?”, before you knew it, you were on a tour of how molecules are built.

The authors thought that storing nutrients as crystals helps feed the young as they grow. But that role has not yet been confirmed. Hold on before jumping to “Would drinking it be good for your health?” What they are studying here is how nutrients are delivered to the young.

Now, on to nose-blowing. Suddenly, it feels much closer to home. Researchers in ear, nose, and throat medicine in Japan measured how adults blow their noses. They looked at blowing through one nostril at a time, and through both nostrils. The everyday “snort” was divided into different conditions for comparison.

How fast was the air? How much air? How long did it take? They also looked at changes in pressure inside the ears. It was only a nose blow, but one measurement after another came up. Researchers don’t overlook a brief moment that usually ends with a tissue.

The goal was to explain the action to people who do not know much about how to blow their nose. But the study does not set out medical rules for today. It is not “The right way to blow your nose is finally settled!” Instead, the researchers broke down an action people do without thinking into parts they could measure.

One study is about crystals that store nutrients; the other, air passing through the nose. They are about very different things, but the heart of what makes them interesting is the same: To get closer to what they want to know, what do they measure, and how? After laughing and saying, “What is that?!”, the next question is, “So how did they study it?” That is the best part of these studies.

## In one line
Ig Nobel-winning studies examined nutrient crystals in cockroach embryos and measured how adults blow their noses. They show how scientists break even surprising or everyday things into parts they can measure.
