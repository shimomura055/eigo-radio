# Cockroach Milk and Nose-Blowing: Scientists Measure Even That

“Cockroach milk” and “nose-blowing research.” Just these words make you stop and look again. Both studies were chosen for the Ig Nobel Prize. It is fine to laugh. But don’t walk away just because of their names. The real interest is the scientists’ attitude: “You measured even that!?”

First, cockroach milk. If you got a glass ready, quietly put it back in the cupboard. This is not milk for people to drink. The study looked at crystals of a protein that gives nutrients. They were found in the guts of baby cockroaches growing inside their mothers. The setting is not a cafe, but a baby cockroach’s gut. The bar is high from the start.

Researchers used X-rays to study these tiny crystals. They looked at their shape in three dimensions. They found fats joined to the protein. Sugar was attached too. You might make a fuss at the door and ask, “What is milk?” Soon, you were on a tour of how molecules are built.

The researchers thought keeping nutrients in crystals helps feed the young as they grow. But this role has not been proven yet. Do not jump to the question, “Would drinking it be good for your health?” What they study here is how nutrients reach the young.

Now, nose-blowing. Suddenly, it feels much closer to home. In Japan, ear, nose, and throat researchers measured how adults blow their noses. They studied blowing through one nostril at a time, and through both nostrils. They broke the everyday “snort” into different cases and compared them.

How fast was the air? How much air came out? How long did it take? They also checked for changes in pressure inside the ears. It was just a nose blow, but they made one measurement after another. Researchers do not miss a short moment that usually ends with a tissue.

They wanted to explain nose-blowing to people who knew little about how to do it. But the study does not give medical rules for today. It does not say, “We have finally found the right way to blow your nose!” Instead, researchers broke an action people do without thinking into parts they could measure.

One study is about crystals that store nutrients. The other is about air moving through the nose. They are about very different things, but the main point is the same: To get closer to what they want to know, what do scientists measure, and how? After laughing and saying, “What is that?!”, the next question is, “So how did they study it?” That is the best part of these studies.

## In one line
Ig Nobel-winning studies looked at nutrient crystals in cockroach babies before birth and measured how adults blow their noses. They show how scientists measure parts of strange or everyday things.
