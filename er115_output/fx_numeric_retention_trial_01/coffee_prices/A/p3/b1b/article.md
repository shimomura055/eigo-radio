# The News on That Coffee Price Tag Is Arriving Late

“Wow, that’s expensive!” Have you ever felt wide awake at the coffee shelf, before you’ve even had a sip? But if you think that price tag is “today’s news,” you may be jumping to conclusions. It is delivering news about events from a little while ago, with a time lag.

Let’s turn the clock back to February 2025. On the New York market, Arabica coffee futures rose above $4.30 per pound, reaching an all-time high. You might brace yourself and wonder, “What will happen to my cup of coffee?” But this was not the price in stores. It was the price of futures traded on the market. The number at the checkout was still a separate matter.

Behind the scenes, the weather was a problem. According to Reuters, drought in Brazil made it hard to expect the next harvest to recover, and concerns about the bean supply grew. Following coffee prices led us to the weather forecast.

Later, the Associated Press reported, based on US government figures, that the average retail price of ground coffee in the United States had risen 41% from the same month a year earlier. That could make your wallet tremble, not just wake you up. But this was not the price of a cup in a cafe. It was the average price people paid when buying ground coffee.

At this point, you may be thinking, “Sure, it’s because of tariffs, right?” Hold on. You may have the right players, but the order is wrong.

It has indeed been reported that US tariffs pushed up the price of raw coffee beans. But according to Reuters, most of the rise in retail prices was due to supply shortages that began before the tariffs and to rising prices for the beans.

What’s more, at the time of that report, the effects of the tariffs had not yet been fully reflected in the price people paid when they bought the product, as it moved through the steps before reaching consumers.

In other words, even if the “effects of tariffs” were already showing on the raw-bean clock, the clock in the store was mainly showing the earlier bean shortage and price rise. It was the same coffee, but the clock hands were not in sync.

A price tag is not always a breaking-news alert. It is more like a newspaper that arrives in your mailbox a little late. If you link “the cause making news now” directly to “the cause of price rises now,” you may misunderstand the news.

Next time you are surprised at the store and ask, “What just happened?”, try asking, “What happened a little while ago?” That price tag may not be today’s special news report. It may be a front-page story that has arrived from the past.

## In one line
Most of the 41% rise in U.S. ground-coffee prices came from earlier shortages and higher bean costs, not tariffs. At the time, tariff effects had not yet fully reached shoppers, so store prices could reflect past events rather than today’s headlines.
