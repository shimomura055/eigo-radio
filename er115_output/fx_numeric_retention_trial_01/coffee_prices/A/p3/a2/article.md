# The News on That Coffee Price Tag Is Arriving Late

“Wow, that’s expensive!” Have you ever felt wide awake at the coffee shelf before your first sip? But if you think the price tag tells you what is happening today, you may be too quick to decide. It tells you about events from a little while ago. There is a time delay.

Let’s go back to February 2025. On the New York market, Arabica coffee futures rose above $4.30 per pound. This was the highest price ever. You might worry and ask, “What will happen to my cup of coffee?” But this was not the price in stores. It was the price of futures traded on the market. The price at checkout was a separate matter.

Behind the scenes, the weather was causing trouble. Reuters said drought in Brazil made people doubt that the next harvest would recover. People grew more worried about the supply of coffee beans. To follow coffee prices, we had to watch the weather forecast.

Later, the Associated Press reported figures from the US government. They showed that the average price people paid for ground coffee in the United States had risen 41%. That was compared with the same month a year before. That could make your wallet shake, not just wake you up. But this was not the price of a cup in a cafe. It was the average price people paid for ground coffee.

Now you may think, “Sure, it’s because of tariffs, right?” Wait a moment. You may have picked the right causes, but put them in the wrong order.

Reports said US tariffs had raised the price of raw coffee beans. But Reuters said most of the rise in store prices came from other causes. Supply shortages had begun before the tariffs. Bean prices were also rising.

Also, when that report came out, tariff effects were not yet fully shown in the price people paid. The product still had to pass through several steps before it reached shoppers.

In other words, the “effects of tariffs” may already have shown on the raw-bean clock. But the store clock mostly showed the earlier bean shortage and rise in bean prices. It was the same coffee, but the clock hands did not match.

A price tag does not always mean there is breaking news. It is more like a newspaper that arrives late in your mailbox. If you link a cause in the news now to a price rise now, you may misunderstand the news.

Next time a price at the store surprises you, ask, “What just happened?” Then try asking, “What happened a little while ago?” That price tag may not be today’s special news report. It may be a front-page story that has arrived from the past.

## In one line
Earlier shortages and higher bean prices caused most of the 41% rise in U.S. ground coffee prices. Tariff effects had not fully reached shoppers, so store prices could show past events, not today’s news.
