# When Your Pay Goes Up, Does Your Careful Side Switch On, Too? Compare Yourself with “Your Usual Self”

You open your pay slip and look at the numbers. Then you quietly close it. “Would this number go up if I worked harder?” Before you know it, you’re comparing yourself with coworkers in your head. But let’s stop that contest for now. The star of this story is not the top worker beside you. It is “your usual self.”

The research team studied 11 studies about workers’ personalities and job income. The number of people was different in each study. The largest study included 134,515 people. That is quite a crowd.

But the most important point is how the team separated the results. They used studies that measured income and personality at least three times. This helped them tell two things apart. Some differences between people were there from the start. Other changes happened within the same person over time. “That person always earns a lot” may sound like “I earn more than usual.” But they mean different things. The key is not to put both in the same pot.

The study focuses on “conscientiousness.” It means taking care with things and doing them properly. For the same person, earning more than usual was linked to slightly higher conscientiousness later. In the other direction, being more conscientious than usual was also linked to slightly higher income later.

Money and careful work—an unusual team, cheering each other on? But we cannot yet say your pay slip tightens that tie in your mind. The team found only a small link. This does not mean a pay rise makes you more conscientious. Being more conscientious does not guarantee a pay rise.

One thing happening first does not mean it caused what happened later. This study alone cannot show that income changed conscientiousness. It also cannot show that the opposite happened. Other things may affect both, too. We know which came first in time, but not everything that happened behind the scenes.

So do not turn a pay slip into a “personality report card.” Before you take a red pen to write, “I’m not conscientious enough,” try a different way to measure yourself. Do not compare yourself with someone else. Look at how you have changed from your usual self. This study may not offer a trick for getting a raise. It may give you a hint about how to make that comparison.

## In one line
More income than usual was linked to slightly more care later, and the reverse link appeared too. These small links do not prove cause and effect, so compare yourself with your usual self, not your pay.
