# When Your Pay Goes Up, Does Your Serious Side Switch On, Too? The Person to Compare Yourself With Is “Your Usual Self”

You open your pay slip. You look at the numbers. You quietly close it. “If I worked harder, would this number go up too?” Before you know it, you’re holding a ranking contest in your head with your coworkers. But this time, let’s call off the contest for now. The star of the story isn’t the top worker next to you. It’s “your usual self.”

The research team analyzed 11 studies on workers’ personalities and the income they earned from their jobs. The number of people differed from study to study, but the largest included 134,515 people. That’s quite a crowd.

But the real point to notice is how they separated things. The team used studies that measured income and personality at least three times. This let them separate the differences between people that were there from the start from changes that happened within the same person. “That person always earns a lot” and “I earn more than usual” may sound similar, but they are different things. The key is not to put them in the same pot.

The focus here is “conscientiousness,” the tendency to take care with things and do them properly. Looking at changes within the same person, earning more than usual was linked, to a small degree, with higher conscientiousness later on. In the other direction, being more conscientious than usual was also linked, to a small degree, with higher income later on.

Money and conscientiousness—an unexpected team cheering each other on? But it’s too soon to say that your pay slip even tightens the tie in your mind. What the team found was only a small connection. It does not mean that “a pay raise makes you more conscientious” or that “becoming more conscientious guarantees a pay raise.”

Just because one thing happens first doesn’t mean it caused what came later. This analysis alone cannot tell us that income changed conscientiousness, or that the reverse is true. There may also be other factors that affect both. We know the order in time, but we haven’t seen everything happening behind the scenes.

So let’s not turn a pay slip into a “report card for personality.” Before you take a red pen and write, “I’m not conscientious enough,” try using a different measuring stick. Instead of ranking yourself against someone else, look at how you’ve changed from your usual self. What this study may offer is not a trick for getting a raise, but a hint about how to make that comparison.

## In one line
When people earned more than usual, they later showed slightly higher conscientiousness, and the reverse link also appeared. These small links do not prove cause and effect, so compare yourself with your usual self rather than judging your personality by your pay.
