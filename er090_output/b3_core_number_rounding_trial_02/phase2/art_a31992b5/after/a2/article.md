# Does a Higher Income Turn On the “Hardworking Switch”? The Key Is Changing Who You Compare With

“If people earn more, do they take work more seriously?” “No, they earn more because they take work seriously, right?”

It is a chicken-and-egg question about income and personality. Both answers make you wonder. But before we decide, we should check one thing: who is compared with whom?

A research team brought together information from 11 studies. These studies followed workers’ personalities and income from work. They included up to about 135,000 people. The number differed for each personality trait being studied. Each study checked the same people’s income and personality at least three times. So these were not one-time checks of personality and income.

The key feature of this study is that it changes who is being compared.

Comparing Person A, with a high income, and Person B, with a low income also includes differences they had from the start. So the researchers separated out lasting differences between people. These included whether someone usually earned more. They focused on changes within each person. They compared each person with their usual self, not Person A with Person B.

This showed a small but interesting link. When someone earned more than usual, they tended to work harder than usual later. Being hardworking is a personality trait linked to taking your work seriously.

The link also went the other way. Being more hardworking than usual was linked to earning more than usual later. So there was a small link from income to personality, and from personality to income.

So, can we say, “A pay raise turns on the hardworking switch”?

That would be jumping the gun. Even if someone’s income went up first and they worked harder later, that does not prove the higher income caused it. The same is true of the link in the other direction. We know which came first, but not what caused what.

When researchers considered age, the main results and conclusions did not change much. Still, something besides age may have played a part. “It happened afterward” and “it happened because of that” may sound alike, but they mean different things.

If you are about to guess someone’s personality from their income, try changing who you compare them with. Move from “What kind of person are they?” to “What changed in them?” The story of income and personality takes a different turn, not when we add more people, but when we follow the same person closely.

## In one line
Comparing people with their usual selves over time showed small links between income and being hardworking, in both directions. But the study cannot show that either one caused the other.
