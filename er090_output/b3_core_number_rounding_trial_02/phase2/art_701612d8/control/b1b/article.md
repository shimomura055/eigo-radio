# Is Personality to Blame for That Meanness? Question the “Choices,” Too

“When people get bored, they become mean.”

You’ve heard this before. “When people get bored, their true nature comes out, right?” We may want to find their personality guilty. But wait. There’s one more person at the scene we should question.

That suspect is “choice.”

The scene: an online experiment with people in the United States. Participants were randomly split into two groups and watched a five-minute video. One group watched a magician. The other watched a rock that did nothing.

A rock. For five minutes, on video—not a still picture.

The magician might make something disappear, but the rock might make our ability to focus disappear.

What’s more, while watching the video, participants had the power to “reduce,” “increase,” or “do nothing” to another participant’s reward. They could change the amount in 10-cent steps, up to 50 cents.

A menu for idle fingers, letting them control someone else’s money. So, what happened?

Among participants who were more likely to enjoy others’ suffering, the boring video led them to reduce the other person’s reward more often. Among those who were less likely to enjoy others’ suffering, there was no clear increase.

“See? It really is personality!”

We can hear people rushing to pass judgment. But there is more evidence.

In another online experiment in the United States, participants were also bored by watching that video of a rock for five minutes. But this time, they had no option to “increase” the reward. They could reduce it or leave it unchanged.

This time, boredom led both people who were more likely to enjoy others’ suffering and those who were less likely to do so to reduce rewards more often.

If we only questioned personality, we might start to wonder about the menu at the scene, too. It may matter not only “who is choosing,” but also “what they can choose.”

But we should not rush to turn this into a product and say, “Just add a kindness button and the problem is solved!” These were separate experiments, so we cannot say that the presence or absence of the “increase” option alone caused the difference. It is not clear whether choices about money in a short online experiment work the same way as everyday meanness or boredom that lasts a long time.

Still, this may change how we look at apps and services a little. Before asking about a user’s personality, we might ask what actions the screen lets them choose.

If you think, “That person is mean,” take a breath before passing judgment.

Why not question the choices in front of us, too?

## In one line
Boredom increased reward cuts only among people more likely to enjoy others’ suffering when they could also raise rewards, but among both groups when they could only cut them. The results suggest the choices people are offered may matter alongside personality, though the separate experiments cannot show that the menu alone caused the difference.
