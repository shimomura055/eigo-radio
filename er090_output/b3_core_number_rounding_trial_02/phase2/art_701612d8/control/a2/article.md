# Is Personality to Blame for Meanness? Question the “Choices,” Too

“When people get bored, they become mean.”

You’ve heard this before. “When people get bored, their true nature comes out, right?” We may want to blame their personality. But wait. There is one more person at the scene we should question.

That suspect is “choice.”

The scene was an online test with people in the United States. The people taking part were split at random into two groups. They watched a five-minute video. One group watched a magician. The other watched a rock doing nothing.

A rock. On video for five minutes—not a still picture.

The magician might make something disappear. But the rock might make our ability to focus disappear.

While they watched, people could choose to “reduce,” “increase,” or “do nothing” to another person’s reward. They could change the amount by 10 cents each time, up to 50 cents.

A menu for bored fingers, letting them control someone else’s money. So, what happened?

Among people more likely to enjoy seeing others suffer, the boring video led them to cut the other person’s reward more often. Among people less likely to enjoy seeing others suffer, there was no clear rise.

“See? It really is personality!”

We can hear people rushing to judge them. But there is more evidence.

In another online test in the United States, people also watched a video of a rock for five minutes. This bored them. But this time, they could not choose to “increase” the reward. They could cut it or leave it as it was.

This time, boredom led both groups to cut rewards more often. This was true for people more likely to enjoy seeing others suffer, and for those less likely to do so.

If we questioned only personality, we might also wonder about the menu at the scene. It may matter not only who chooses, but also what they can choose.

But we should not rush to make this into a product and say, “Just add a kindness button and the problem is solved!” These were two separate tests. So we cannot say that simply having or not having the “increase” option caused the difference. It is not clear if choices about money in a short online test work the same way as everyday meanness or boredom that lasts a long time.

Still, this may change how we look at apps and services a little. Before we ask about a user’s personality, we might ask what actions the screen lets them choose.

If you think, “That person is mean,” take a breath before you judge.

Why not question the choices in front of us, too?

## In one line
With a raise option, boredom led to more cuts only among people more likely to enjoy seeing others suffer; with no raise option, both groups cut more. The choices may matter as well as personality, but the separate tests do not prove the menu caused the difference.
