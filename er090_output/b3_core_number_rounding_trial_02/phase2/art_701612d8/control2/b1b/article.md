# If You Look at a Stone for Five Minutes, Is Someone Else’s 50 Cents at Risk?

There is a stone on the screen. It doesn’t speak. Nothing happens. No magician appears and pulls out a pigeon. You just watch it for five minutes. “Will something happen soon?” No. It’s a stone.

This is a scene from an online experiment with participants in the United States. The participants were randomly split into groups. For example, in the boredom condition, they watched a video of a stone for five minutes. In the comparison condition, they watched a video of a magician.

The study asked, “Does boredom make people mean?” But the hidden main character in this story is neither the stone nor a person’s character. It is the “menu of choices” in front of them.

In one experiment, while watching a video, participants could choose to reduce another participant’s reward, increase it, or do nothing. They could change the amount in steps of 10 cents, up to 50 cents. In other words, they had three choices: “be a little mean,” “be a little kind,” or “keep things as they are.” The stone does nothing, but the participants have something they can do.

What mattered here was “sadism”—the tendency to enjoy making other people suffer. In the researchers’ analysis, people with more of this tendency chose to reduce the reward more often in the boredom condition. But this increase was not seen among people with less of the tendency.

So, it seemed, this was a matter of personality. Just as that thought came to mind, the story took another turn.

In another experiment, participants could not choose to increase the reward. They could only reduce it or leave it alone. This time, in the boredom condition, participants chose to reduce the reward more often, whether their level of sadism was high or low.

So, the story does not end with personality alone. We also start to wonder not only “Who got bored?” but “What could a bored person do?”

But it is too soon to run off saying, “Add a kindness button, and the world will be at peace!” The two findings came from separate experiments, so we cannot say that the different results were due only to the choices. The study also looked only at short periods of boredom and the act of reducing another person’s reward online. We cannot apply the results as they are to all kinds of everyday meanness or to boredom that lasts a long time.

Even so, this may change how we look at what people do when they are bored. Before ending the conversation with “That person is mean,” take a look at the menu in that situation. What choices are available to someone who is bored?

The stone does nothing to the very end. And yet, it is humans who have raised a rather tricky and interesting question.

## In one line
Bored participants more often cut another person’s online reward, though the pattern varied with sadism and the choices available. The findings suggest we should consider what choices bored people have, though the separate, short-term experiments may not apply to everyday meanness or lasting boredom.
