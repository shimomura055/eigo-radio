# If You Stare at a Stone for Five Minutes, Will You Become a “Villain”?

The star today is a stone. It doesn’t roll. Nothing happens. You just watch a stone for five minutes. If you think, “Enough already—make something happen!” there’s an extra rule: while watching the video, you can take money away from another participant. Now you may be getting a bad feeling.

An online experiment with people in the United States set up this situation. They were randomly assigned to watch either a video of a stone where nothing happened or a video of a magician. One showed magic tricks, the other a stone. Sorry, stone, but you don’t seem to stand much chance as entertainment.

But taking money away wasn’t the only choice. Participants could also “add money” or “do nothing.” The amount could go up or down in steps of 10 cents, up to 50 cents. So you could be mean to pass the time, but you could also be kind.

So, did the people who watched the stone press the “be mean” button?

The answer is, “It depends on the person.” In the boring condition, people who were more inclined to enjoy making others suffer were more likely to take money away. For those with less of this tendency, boredom did not lead to more of this behavior.

So, it’s a matter of personality. But it’s too soon to wrap up with that as today’s lesson. Here comes another experiment.

In this experiment, there was no option to “add money” to the other person’s total. There was also a boring condition where participants watched a stone video for five minutes. In that condition, taking money away became more common. And this time, this increase was seen whether participants had a strong or weak tendency to enjoy making others suffer.

Oh. When we tried to explain things only by personality, the choices raised a hand from the side. It’s not only “who chooses” that matters; we can’t overlook “what they can choose,” either. You might think this is about looking into people’s minds, but it also makes you want to look at the menu on the screen.

But don’t rush to turn this into a magic fix: “Add a kindness button and meanness will disappear!” These were results from separate experiments. It was not shown that giving people the option to add money would stop everyone from being mean. Nor can we apply results from short videos straight to our everyday relationships.

Even so, when choosing something on your phone, it’s interesting to look at the options and ask, “What can I choose here?” before asking, “What kind of person am I?” When I went looking for the switch for being mean, I started wondering about the design behind the choices. The stone that did nothing for five minutes can really get a conversation rolling.

## In one line
When adding money was an option, boredom increased money-taking only among people more inclined to enjoy others’ suffering. Without that option, boredom increased money-taking across personality types, suggesting choices matter too—but the separate experiments do not prove a kind option would stop everyone from being mean.
