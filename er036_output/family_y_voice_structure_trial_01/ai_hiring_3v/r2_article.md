# When AI Sits Between a Job and a Person

## The Score Appears Before the Interview

The screen lights up. An applicant has finished a video interview. A recruiter waits for a score. A business owner watches the hiring clock. In September 2026, this scene is common at many companies. AI can read applications, score tests, arrange interviews, and study video interviews. It promises speed and lower costs. But behind the same screen are three people with three different fears. One wants a fair chance. One must explain the machine. One must protect the whole company.

### Voice 1: The Applicant — “Did I Lose to a Number?”

I press “send” on my application. Then I wait.

Maybe a person will read it. Maybe a computer will read it first. I may not know which words the system wants. I may not know what it sees as a strong answer.

Then comes the video interview. I speak to a camera, not a person. The system may study my face, my voice, and the way I speak. It may give me a score for qualities such as trust and honesty.

But can a face show honesty? Can a voice show how well I will do a job? If the answer is no, I may lose my chance before anyone meets me.

I think about an applicant in Massachusetts. In 2021, he applied for a supply-chain job at CVS Health. He took a video interview that used HireVue technology and AI from Affectiva to study facial expressions and voice tone. He said he could not refuse the AI process. He also said he could not challenge the result. He was not hired.

He sued CVS. Later, CVS agreed to a temporary settlement in a class-action case.

That story stays with me. If an AI score rejects me, what can I do? Can I ask for a human review? Can I explain something the system missed? Can I even find out what the system decided?

I understand why companies use AI. There may be thousands of applications. But I am not a line on a screen. I am a person waiting outside the hiring door. I need to know why the door opened for someone else—and why it closed for me.

### Voice 2: The Recruiter — “Now I Have to Question the Question”

My morning begins with a crowded hiring list. Applications wait. Interview times must be arranged. Managers want names. Candidates want answers.

AI can help. It can sort applications, plan interviews, and help write job posts. In a 2026 survey, 91 percent of HR leaders said they were actively using AI somewhere in hiring. Resume screening was the most common use.

The tool gives me speed. It also gives me a new job inside my job: I must check the checker.

A candidate may ask, “Was I rejected because of my experience, my answers, or an AI score?” I cannot answer, “The computer said no.” That is not an explanation.

Many job seekers already believe AI screening can be biased. So I need more than a friendly message. I need documents that show where AI is used. I need to explain what it measures. I need regular bias checks. I need records that show whether the system treats different groups differently.

I also need a plan for mistakes. If an applicant says, “This score is wrong,” someone must listen. If a person can review the decision, I must know when and how that review happens.

This is the strange part. AI was supposed to make my work lighter. Instead, it adds a new question to every hiring decision: Can I defend this choice?

I still need to fill the job. I still need to move quickly. But if I cannot explain the process, the fast tool becomes a slow-moving problem.

### Voice 3: The Business Owner — “The Clock Is Counting, but So Is the Risk”

I look at an empty position and see a clock.

While the job is open, work may slow down. Customers may wait. A strong candidate may accept another offer. Hiring can take weeks, and weeks cost money.

AI promises a faster race. Hilton has reported that its average hiring time fell from about six weeks to five days after using AI. IBM has reported a reduction in hiring costs of about 30 percent.

Those numbers catch my attention. Five days sounds better than six weeks. Lower costs sound better than higher costs. If the company cannot control its hiring costs, it may not be able to protect its business.

Then another clock starts.

What if the system treats an applicant unfairly? The company may face a discrimination claim. We may face legal responsibility and damages. Our reputation may also suffer. A company can lose trust much faster than it can build trust.

That loss may reach customers, workers, and future applicants. A quick hire may look good this month. A public problem may stay with us for years.

So I ask questions that do not fit neatly on a sales slide. Can we check the system? Can we show how it made a decision? Can a person review a result? Can we answer a complaint without saying, “We do not know”?

These checks cost time and money. They may make the hiring process less fast. But using AI without them may create a much larger bill.

I want the speed. I also want the company to survive the mistake that speed might hide.

## The Countdown Has Three Different Winners

The applicant sees a locked door. The recruiter sees a black box with a question mark. The owner sees a clock and a possible bill.

All three are looking at the same AI system. But their best results are different.

The applicant wants a clear reason and a real chance to challenge the score. The recruiter needs time to check the process and explain it. The owner wants the job filled quickly and the cost kept low.

These goals do not simply join together. A human review may help an applicant, but reviews take time. A bias audit may help a recruiter, but audits cost money. A faster system may help the owner, but speed does not prove fairness.

A report can say that the system works well. It may still fail to explain one person’s rejection. A company can save money on hiring. It may still pay much more after a discrimination claim. A recruiter can believe the process is fair. The applicant may still feel trapped inside a decision they cannot see.

The real contest is not only between people and machines. It is between three kinds of pressure: the need for a chance, the need for an explanation, and the need to protect the business.

## When the Screen Goes Dark

AI can move an application through the hiring process in seconds. It cannot answer every person who waits on the other side of the screen.

The applicant still needs a fair chance. The recruiter still needs proof that the process is fair. The owner still needs to weigh speed against legal, financial, and reputational risk.

That is why the decision is not a simple race between “use AI” and “do not use AI.” The harder question comes after the score appears:

Who will explain it?

If the answer is nobody, the company may save time today. But the applicant may lose a future, the recruiter may lose control of the process, and the owner may discover that the fastest hiring decision was the most expensive one.
