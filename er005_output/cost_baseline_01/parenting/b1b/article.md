# Moving Up, Carrying the Past: Social Mobility and Parenting

Climbing the social ladder changes where a family stands. But is that climb linked to how a parent responds, teaches, and plays? A long-running New Zealand study found both progress and a lasting trace of childhood.

The study, published in *Child Development* in 2026, drew on people born in Dunedin from April 1972 to March 1973. It included **719 parent-child pairs—95% of those eligible**. Parents were 32.7 years old on average, and their children were 3.4.

Researchers used a six-level occupation scale to compare each parent’s social position in childhood and adulthood. This created four paths: staying low, moving up, staying high, or moving down.

They assessed two parts of parenting: sensitive responses and positive involvement, and activities that support a child’s thinking. After basic demographic differences were considered, parents who moved up scored above those who stayed low on both measures. But they scored below those who stayed high.

Childhood helped explain part of the gap. After researchers considered the parenting participants had received as children, the gaps became **13% to 15% smaller**. The upward group still scored above the stable-low group on both measures. Compared with the stable-high group, the gap remained statistically significant for cognitive stimulation, but not for sensitive parenting.

Cognitive ability and self-control in childhood accounted for more of the pattern. With those included, the gap with the stable-low group shrank by about **30%**. The gap with the stable-high group shrank by about **50%**. The gap with the stable-low group remained, while the gap with the stable-high group was no longer statistically significant on either parenting measure.

### The study watched parenting, not just social status

The team combined home interviews with video from three ten-minute situations: free play, a task with competing demands, and a teaching task. Trained coders used seven-point ratings. This gave researchers a wider view of parenting than a single report could provide.

### This is a map of links, not a chain of causes

The study was observational, so it cannot show that moving up changes parenting. Personal traits, genes, culture, or economic conditions may also shape the pattern. Social position was based on occupation, so the findings may not apply to income, education, wealth, or other countries.

## In one line…

Moving up was linked to stronger parenting, but it did not erase the starting line. Where parents arrived mattered—and so did the childhood they carried with them.
