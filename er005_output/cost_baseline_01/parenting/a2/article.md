# Moving Up the Social Ladder—but Does the Past Stay Behind?

A parent’s life can change.

They may grow up in a family with low social status. Later, they may move into a higher social position.

But does this move also change how they raise their own children?

A 2026 study in *Child Development* looked at this question. It was part of the Dunedin Study, which has followed people born in New Zealand from April 1972 to March 1973.

The parenting study included **719 parent-child pairs**. This was **95% of the people who could take part**. The parents were 32.7 years old on average. Their children were 3.4 years old on average.

Researchers used job status to compare each parent’s childhood and adult life. They made four groups: parents who stayed low, moved up, stayed high, or moved down.

Then they studied two parts of parenting.

One was **sensitive parenting**. This means noticing a child’s needs and responding in a warm, positive way.

The other was **cognitive stimulation**. This means helping a child think, explore, and learn.

Parents who moved up showed better parenting in both areas than parents who stayed in the low group.

However, they did not reach the level of parents who stayed in the high group.

In other words, they were in the middle.

Researchers then looked at the parents’ own childhood experiences. After they included the parenting that adults had received as children, the differences became **13% to 15% smaller**. Some differences still remained.

Childhood thinking ability and self-control explained more. These factors reduced the gap with the stable-low group by about **30%**. They reduced the gap with the stable-high group by about **50%**.

After this step, the difference from the stable-high group was no longer statistically clear for either type of parenting. But the upward group still remained above the stable-low group.

### Point One: Parenting quality was watched, not simply reported

Trained researchers watched parents and children during three ten-minute activities. They also used a home interview. This gave them clear measures of warm responses and learning support. The study did not depend only on parents saying whether they believed they were doing well.

### Point Two: A link is not proof of cause

This was an observational study. It cannot prove that moving up changes parenting. Personal, genetic, cultural, or economic factors may also matter. Social position was based on jobs, not income, education, or wealth, and results from New Zealand may not fit every country.

## In one line…

**A parent may move up the social ladder, but in these results, where they started still left a mark on how they raised the next generation.**
