"Twenty Percent—Then Never Mind": Hormuz Fee Reversal Fails to Calm Oil

For a little more than a day, one number hung over the Strait of Hormuz: 20%.

On July 13 at 10:16 a.m. EDT, U.S. President Donald Trump said the United States would seek reimbursement at that rate on "all cargo" moving through the strait, framing it as payment for the cost of providing safety and security. He added that work on the procedures and system would begin immediately.

The announcement sounded forceful. The mechanism, however, was almost entirely blank.

There was no stated collector, payer, valuation method, currency, exemption system, enforcement process, or legal basis. Even the base to which the 20% would apply was unspecified. This was therefore a proposal—not an implemented toll.

Still, the number was large enough to make markets imagine the bill. If 20% were applied to cargo value, a VLCC carrying about two million barrels at roughly $85 Brent would hold around $170 million in crude. Twenty percent would equal about $34 million; another expert estimate put it near $32 million. Those were scenarios, not actual charges.

On July 13, Brent settled at $83.30, up $7.29, or 9.59%.

Then came the reversal. At 11:04 a.m. EDT on July 14—about 24 hours and 48 minutes after the original post—Trump said the 20% reimbursement plan would be replaced by Gulf trade and investment deals with the United States.

Brent ultimately settled at $84.73, up $1.43, or 1.7%, as blockade, attacks, and tanker-safety concerns persisted.
