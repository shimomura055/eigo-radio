# A 20% Strait Fee Appears—Then Disappears, but Oil Markets Stay on Edge

For roughly one day, one of the world’s most important shipping routes faced a striking new question:

Would cargo passing through the Strait of Hormuz suddenly come with a 20% charge?

On July 13, 2026, US President Donald Trump said the United States would seek reimbursement equal to 20% on all cargo moving through the strait. He presented the plan as a way to recover the cost of providing security and protection there.

However, the proposal raised more questions than it answered.

It did not explain who would collect the money, who would be legally required to pay, how cargo would be valued, or how the charge would be enforced. In other words, the rate was announced, but the system behind it was not.

Then came a sharp reversal.

On July 14, about 25 hours after the original post, Trump said the 20% reimbursement plan would be replaced by trade and investment deals between Gulf countries and the United States. He described the change as the result of productive discussions with Middle Eastern leaders.

Later that day, he told reporters that nobody should charge ships for using the Strait of Hormuz and said he did not like the idea of such fees.

The oil market reacted—but it did not simply relax.

After the reversal was announced, Brent crude futures briefly reduced their gains. Soon afterward, however, prices returned to nearly their earlier high levels. At one point, Brent was above $85 a barrel and around 2.6% higher.

It finally settled at $84.73, up 1.7% for the day. Continuing concerns about attacks, a maritime blockade, and tanker safety remained in the market, showing that removing one threat did not remove the wider tension.

### Point One: The legal line was already clear

At its 137th meeting, the IMO Council reaffirmed that ships using the strait for international navigation should face no tolls or charges. Coastal arrangements must protect non-discriminatory and unimpeded transit. However, there is no confirmed evidence that this position caused Trump’s reversal.

### Point Two: Why 20% sounded so large

No calculation method was announced. But if 20% were applied to cargo value, a tanker carrying about two million barrels at roughly $85 each could face a theoretical charge of around $34 million for one voyage. That was only an estimate—not an actual bill.

## In one line…

The 20% fee disappeared almost as quickly as it arrived, but the market’s message was clear: the deeper risks around the Strait of Hormuz had not gone away.
