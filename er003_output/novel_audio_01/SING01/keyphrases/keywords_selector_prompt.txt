# Sam Altman Says We're in the Singularity. Not Everyone Agrees

In a podcast released in July, OpenAI's Sam Altman said, "We're now, like, in the singularity." It echoed something he had written the year before: "We are past the event horizon; the takeoff has started." Elon Musk has made a related but different claim, posting in January that 2026, this year, will be the year the singularity arrives.

So what does the evidence actually show?

The picture is mixed. Researchers who track AI performance found that on hard, graduate-level science questions, leading models now answer correctly just over ninety percent of the time — better than the human experts used as the benchmark, who scored around eighty percent. A Google model also reached a gold-medal-level score at last year's International Mathematical Olympiad. Yet the same research found that a top AI model could correctly read a simple analog clock only about half the time, compared with around ninety percent for humans. Researchers call this mix of brilliance and basic mistakes the "jagged frontier."

Business data tells a similarly uneven story. In a large 2025 survey, nearly nine in ten responding companies said they use AI regularly in at least one part of their work, but only about a third had even begun scaling it across the whole organization — and just four in ten reported any real effect on operating profit. Separately, a large 2023 survey of AI researchers asked something related but not identical: when might machines handle any task a person can, without help, more cheaply and better than people do? Not "the singularity" itself, just that one narrower milestone. The midpoint answer: a fifty-fifty chance by around 2047. The same survey put only a ten percent chance on it happening by 2027 — a sign of just how wide and uncertain that estimate really is.

### Nobody quite agrees on what "singularity" even means

Part of the disagreement comes down to definitions. Computer scientist Stuart Russell says AI hasn't reached that threshold yet. Researcher Roman Yampolskiy argues fast progress alone isn't the singularity. Philosopher Nick Bostrom points to a missing piece: today's models mostly stop learning once they're released, instead of continuing to grow on their own.

### A bold claim and a wide survey are not the same kind of evidence

Altman's and Musk's statements are personal judgments, made in a podcast and a social media post. The 2047 estimate comes from a large, separate survey of AI researchers. Neither source is automatically more correct than the other. But there's a real difference between one confident personal claim and a wide survey with a very spread-out set of answers.

## In one line…

Sam Altman says we're already in the singularity, and Elon Musk says it's landing this year. A large survey of AI researchers puts good odds on something similar, but only decades from now — and even they don't agree with each other on the timing.
