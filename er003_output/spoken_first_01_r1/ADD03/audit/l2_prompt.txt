# “Twenty Percent—Then Never Mind”: Hormuz Fee Reversal Fails to Calm Oil

For a little more than a day, one number hung over the Strait of Hormuz: **20%**.

On July 13 at 10:16 a.m. EDT, U.S. President Donald Trump said the United States would seek reimbursement at that rate on “all cargo” moving through the strait, framing it as payment for the cost of providing safety and security. He added that work on the procedures and system would begin immediately.

The announcement sounded forceful. The mechanism, however, was almost entirely blank.

There was no stated collector, payer, valuation method, currency, exemption system, enforcement process, or legal basis. Even the base to which the 20% would apply was unspecified. This was therefore a proposal—not an implemented toll.

Still, the number was large enough to make markets imagine the bill.

If 20% were applied to cargo value, a VLCC carrying about two million barrels at roughly $85 Brent would hold around $170 million in crude. Twenty percent would equal about **$34 million**; another expert estimate put it near $32 million. Those were scenarios, not actual charges.

On July 13, Brent settled at $83.30, up $7.29, or 9.59%. Reuters linked the jump to concerns surrounding a planned U.S. maritime blockade of Iran and energy shipments through Hormuz—not to the proposed fee alone.

Then came the reversal.

At 11:04 a.m. EDT on July 14—about 24 hours and 48 minutes after the original post—Trump said the 20% reimbursement plan would be replaced by Gulf trade and investment deals with the United States. Later, he told reporters that nobody should charge ships for passage and that he disliked the idea of fees, while maintaining that America’s protective burden was unfair.

The market briefly exhaled—but did not relax.

Brent initially trimmed its gain after the announcement, then returned close to its earlier elevated level. It ultimately settled at **$84.73**, up $1.43, or 1.7%, as blockade, attacks, and tanker-safety concerns persisted.

## What Matters Beyond the Headline

### A Dramatic Percentage Was Never a Complete Policy

The 20% figure dominated attention, but the missing architecture mattered just as much. Without a defined calculation or collection framework, any precise cost claim remained hypothetical.

### The Market Was Pricing a Wider Danger

Removing one proposed fee did not remove the other risks surrounding Hormuz. That helps explain why the initial relief faded and why Brent’s daily settlement still rose.

There was also a legal and diplomatic backdrop: at its 137th session, the IMO Council reaffirmed that passage through the strait should remain free of tolls and charges under international law and IMO conventions, with nondiscriminatory, unobstructed transit guaranteed. The record does not establish that this caused Trump’s reversal.

## In One Line

# “Twenty Percent—Then Never Mind”: Hormuz Fee Reversal Fails to Calm Oil

For just over a day, one number hung over the Strait of Hormuz: **20 percent**.

On July 13, U.S. President Donald Trump proposed that the United States seek reimbursement at that rate on “all cargo” moving through the strait. He described it as payment for the cost of providing safety and security. He also said work on the procedures would begin immediately.

But the announcement gave a rate—not a working system.

It did not identify who would collect the money or who would pay. It did not explain how cargo would be valued, what currency would be used, or how exemptions and enforcement would work. It also gave no legal basis. Most importantly, it did not say what the 20 percent would be calculated from.

So this was a proposed reimbursement, not an implemented fee.

Still, the potential cost looked enormous. One scenario assumed a tanker carrying about two million barrels, with oil at about 85 dollars a barrel. If the proposed 20 percent were applied to the cargo’s value, the estimated charge would be about **34 million dollars** for one tanker.

That was only a hypothetical calculation. It was not an actual charge.

On July 13, Brent crude settled at about 83 dollars a barrel, up nearly 10 percent. Reuters linked the jump to concerns about a planned U.S. maritime blockade of Iran and energy shipments through Hormuz—not to the proposed fee alone.

Then came the reversal.

The following day, July 14, Trump said the proposed 20 percent reimbursement would be replaced by Gulf trade and investment deals with the United States.

Later, he told reporters that nobody should charge ships for passage through Hormuz. He said he did not like the idea of fees, while continuing to argue that America’s burden of protecting the strait was unfair.

Oil briefly eased after the announcement. But the relief did not last.

Brent soon returned close to its earlier elevated level. It settled at about **85 dollars a barrel**, finishing the day higher as concerns about the blockade, attacks, and tanker safety continued.

## What Matters Beyond the Headline

### A Dramatic Percentage Was Not a Complete Policy

The proposed 20 percent rate captured attention. But nearly every practical detail was missing.

Without a clear method for calculating and collecting the money, any precise estimate remained hypothetical. The proposal sounded immediate, but it never became an implemented toll.

### The Market Was Pricing a Wider Danger

Removing the proposed fee did not remove the other risks surrounding Hormuz. That helps explain why Brent’s initial relief faded and why prices still finished higher.

There was also a legal and diplomatic backdrop. The IMO Council reaffirmed that passage through the strait should remain free of tolls and charges under international law and IMO conventions. It also said transit must remain nondiscriminatory and unobstructed for all ships.

However, the record does not establish that the IMO position caused Trump’s reversal.
