# Hormuz: Trump Talks Territory as Iran and Oman Agree on Routes

Two very different stories are developing around the Strait of Hormuz.

One is about political power.

On August 12, President Donald Trump wrote on Truth Social that the United States had “total control” of the strait. He added, “I THINK WE WILL KEEP IT!”

Two days later, at a rally in New York state, Trump joked that he might soon declare the Strait of Hormuz to be US territory.

These statements did not make the strait American territory. Iran also strongly rejected them.

Kazem Gharibabadi, Iran’s deputy foreign minister, said the strait could not be seized “by tweet.” He described Trump’s claims as “fanciful delusions.”

At the same time, a quieter and more practical development was taking place.

On August 15, Iranian state media reported that Iran and Oman had agreed on a **shipping route map** for the strait. Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei announced the agreement.

It followed about three months of technical discussions. The two countries had signed a memorandum on June 18.

Under the reported arrangement, ships entering the strait would follow a route closer to Iran. Ships leaving would travel closer to Oman.

However, the map does not mean that the strait has fully reopened.

Iranian state media did not give details about transit fees or security arrangements. These issues are expected to be discussed in a separate phase of talks.

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi has also said that full reopening would require the United States to ease sanctions and pay compensation for the war. These are Iranian demands, not agreed conditions.

For now, normal shipping remains far away. Before the war, about 130 ships crossed the strait each day. By August, only a few ships a day were making the journey, although reports give different figures.

### “Control” can mean different things
Trump is talking about political and military power, while Iran answers with the language of sovereignty. The route map deals with a narrower question: where commercial ships should travel. These forms of control are connected, but they are not the same.

### Why a technical map matters worldwide
Before the war, about one-fifth of the world’s crude oil supply passed through Hormuz. Brent crude rose from around $72 a barrel before the conflict to the high $80s in early August—an increase of about 20%. Even a limited technical agreement can therefore attract global attention.

**At Hormuz, talk of “control” is moving faster than ships: Iran and Oman now have a route map, but a full reopening remains far away.**
