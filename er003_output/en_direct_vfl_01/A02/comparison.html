Midnight arrives. A phone lights up.

One alert becomes another, one video rolls into the next, and bedtime quietly slips away.

The UK government is planning a new response—not by locking every door, but by changing which doors are open by default.

As of 12 August 2026, the proposal has not yet taken effect. Subject to parliamentary approval, the changes are expected to begin around spring 2027.

Under the plan, social media services would automatically apply nighttime protections for users aged 16 and 17 between midnight and 6 a.m. Alerts and push notifications would be muted during that six-hour window.

But this is not an unchangeable curfew. Users would be able to alter the settings, and the government has not said that social media must become completely inaccessible overnight.

The plan also reaches beyond bedtime. Autoplay and personalised feeds would be switched off by default for 16- and 17-year-olds throughout the day—not only between midnight and 6 a.m.

In other words, the proposal is not simply saying: “Put the phone away.” It is asking whether the phone should work quite so hard to pull young people back in.

Two points worth watching

The pilot offers a clue—but not proof

A 2026 qualitative pilot involved 309 households with young people aged 13 to 17. They were divided by preference among a control group and three interventions.

The 81 households assigned to the night-curfew group were asked to block selected social media apps from 9 p.m. to 7 a.m. Of those, 53 reported full compliance, 23 reported minor changes, and five did not comply.

Participants described this curfew as the easiest of the three interventions to fit into daily routines. They also reported more consistent sleep-related improvements.

Still, the research was small, self-selected and based mainly on personal reports. It suggests possibilities; it does not prove cause and effect.

A quieter night does not necessarily mean less screen time

Many participants reported little major change in daytime social media use. Instead, activity sometimes moved elsewhere: heavier use before the restriction began, early-morning checking after 7 a.m., or switching to television and games.

A nighttime setting may rearrange attention rather than erase it. The final details—including precisely which services will be covered—are still to come.

Britain is not proposing a permanent lock on social media, but a midnight pause: quieter defaults, fewer automatic pulls, and a little more space before the next scroll.

Two Points Behind the Settings

Nighttime quiet is only one layer

Another part of the plan operates all day.

Autoplay and personalised feeds would be off by default for 16- and 17-year-olds—not merely between midnight and 6 a.m. Those settings could also be changed by the user.

That distinction matters. One measure softens the night. The other reduces the app’s ability to keep choosing and playing the next thing, regardless of the hour.

The pilot was a clue—not a verdict

A 2026 qualitative pilot involved 309 households in total across four groups. It was not 309 participants testing a night curfew.

The 81 households in the night-curfew group were assigned an intervention blocking selected social apps from 9 p.m. to 7 a.m.—a different schedule and approach from the planned policy. Reported adherence was 53 complete, 23 with minor changes and five non-compliant.

Among the three interventions, participants described the night curfew as the easiest to fit into everyday routines. They also reported the most consistent sleep-related improvements in that group. Many households said they had kept some modified form of curfew afterward.

Still, the research was small, self-selected and qualitative. It did not prove cause and effect.

Many participants also reported little change in daytime social-media use: scrolling sometimes moved earlier, surged before the cutoff, resumed after 7 a.m., or gave way to television and games.

Britain is not planning to confiscate the night; it is planning to make social media quieter by default—while leaving older teens able to reach for the controls.
