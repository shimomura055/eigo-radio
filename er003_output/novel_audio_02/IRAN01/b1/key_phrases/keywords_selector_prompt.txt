# Hormuz: Trump Talks Territory as Iran and Oman Draw a Route Back

Two very different stories are now competing over the Strait of Hormuz.

One is loud and political.

On August 12, President Donald Trump claimed on Truth Social that the United States had “total control” of the strait and might “keep it.” Two days later, at a rally in New York, he joked that he might declare the waterway US territory in the near future.

Those statements did not establish American ownership. Iran also rejected them. Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said the strait could not be seized “by tweet” and described Trump’s claims as “fanciful delusions.”

But while Washington and Tehran traded words about control, a more practical development was taking place on the water.

On August 15, Iranian state media reported that Iran and Oman had agreed on a **shipping route map** for the strait. The announcement came from Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei, following technical discussions under a memorandum signed on June 18.

Under the reported arrangement, ships entering the strait would use a route closer to Iran, while departing vessels would travel closer to Oman.

This is not a full reopening. The announcement did not settle transit fees or security arrangements, which are expected to be discussed separately. Iran’s foreign minister has also said that fully reopening the strait would require sanctions relief and war compensation from the United States.

For now, the gap between a map and normal traffic remains enormous. Before the war, about 130 ships crossed the strait each day. By August, that number had fallen to only a few.

### Point One: “Control” has more than one meaning

Trump is using “control” as a political and military claim. Iran is answering with the language of sovereignty. Iran and Oman, meanwhile, are addressing the practical question of where ships should sail. These ideas sound similar, but they describe very different kinds of power.

### Point Two: A narrow passage with a global price tag

Before the war, roughly one-fifth of the world’s crude oil supply passed through Hormuz. Brent crude has risen about 20% since the conflict began, reaching the high $80s per barrel in early August. A technical map may sound modest, but the energy market is listening closely.

## In one line…

**At Hormuz, the microphone says “control,” but the map says “not yet”: a route is taking shape, while a true reopening remains out of reach.**
