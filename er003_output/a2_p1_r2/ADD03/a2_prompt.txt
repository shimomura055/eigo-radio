Write a natural English news article for A2-level learners, based on the same story told in the source article below.

This is a rewrite for a different audience, not a summary. Your goal is NOT to reduce how much of the story is told. Your goal is to break the same news down into smaller, simpler pieces of language that an A2 learner can follow. Keep the same core facts as the source: what happened, who was involved, why it matters, and the key numbers or examples. Do not remove a fact just because it seems "too advanced" for A2 — instead, express that same fact in a simpler way, using more sentences if needed.

When a sentence is too complex for A2, fix it using these tools, in this priority order:
1. Reduce how much information is packed into that one sentence.
2. Split a long sentence into two or more short sentences (this is often enough by itself — prefer adding a sentence over deleting a fact).
3. Simplify the sentence's grammar/structure.
4. Simplify general vocabulary.
5. Reduce unclear pronoun references (repeat the noun instead of using "it"/"they" when the reference could be unclear).
6. Only after trying 1–5, if a sentence is still too complex, you may trim genuinely peripheral background, exceptions, or minor supplementary detail — but never a core fact (what happened, who was involved, why it matters, key numbers/examples).

Do not shorten the article by deleting facts just to make sentences fit the length target below. The total length of the article may end up similar to, or even longer than, a compressed summary — that is fine and expected, because you are splitting information across more sentences, not cutting it.

Vocabulary: prefer common, high-frequency, easy words. When a general word is difficult, replace it with an easier word or phrase that keeps the same meaning. Keep proper names (people, places, organizations). Keep numbers, amounts, and dates when they matter to understanding the story. You may keep specialist or news-specific words essential to understanding the story, but when you do, keep the surrounding sentence very simple.

Sentences: aim for an average sentence length of about 11 words or fewer. Do not write any single sentence longer than 18 words. Each sentence should express one clear idea — one message per sentence. Prefer simple subject-verb-object sentences. Avoid relative clauses (avoid "who", "which", "that" used to add a description clause). Do not use participial phrases (for example, an "-ing" or "-ed" phrase modifying a noun instead of a full clause). Avoid complex passive voice; prefer active voice unless the agent is unknown or unimportant. Use the present perfect and past perfect only when truly necessary; prefer the simple past or simple present. Avoid the subjunctive mood and hypothetical conditionals. Prefer basic modal verbs (can, will, must, should). If a pronoun would create unclear reference, repeat the noun instead.

The Full Story — the title, the introduction, and the body text before "Today's ... Points" — is the main way listeners learn the news. It must include the primary facts of the story on its own: what happened, who was involved, why it matters, and the key numbers or examples. Do not save the main facts for Point One or Point Two; a listener who only hears the Full Story should already understand the core news.

Point One and Point Two must not simply repeat the Full Story, and must not be where the main facts appear for the first time. Instead, they should go deeper than the Full Story: background, meaning, significance, or a notable detail that builds on what the listener already heard.

Do not write childish or overly simplified English that loses the news value of the story. Do not do any web search; use only the source article below.

# 🌊 The 20% Toll Vanished Overnight—But the Oil Market Is Still on Edge

Imagine a giant tollbooth suddenly appearing in the Strait of Hormuz.

On July 13, 2026, U.S. President Donald Trump announced a plan to collect 20 percent of the value of all cargo passing through the strait—a fee, he said, for keeping the vital waterway secure.

But just one day later, the plan was withdrawn. Instead, Gulf nations would pursue trade and investment deals with the United States. How much money would be involved—and which countries would participate—remained unclear.

The message shifted overnight from:

“If you want to pass through, pay 20 percent,”

to:

“Invest in the United States instead.”

In just 24 hours, a proposal that shook the world’s energy supply routes had taken on an entirely different form.

So, did the markets breathe a sigh of relief?

Only partly.

Brent crude had surged roughly 10 percent the previous day amid escalating tensions between the United States and Iran—and the shock of the proposed 20 percent charge. After the plan was scrapped, prices retreated from an intraday high above 87 dollars a barrel.

Even so, Brent closed on July 14 at 84 dollars and 73 cents, up around 2 percent and at its highest level in a month.

Why? Because while the toll had disappeared, the blockade targeting Iran-linked vessels and ongoing military strikes had not.

The market was never focused solely on the price of passage. What mattered more was whether the strait was safe at all.

## Today's Hormuz Risk Points

### Point One: A Nearly $30 Million Shock for a Single Tanker

For a fully loaded Very Large Crude Carrier, the proposed fee could have reached about 30 million dollars—or roughly 4.9 billion yen.

And the shipping industry had received no advance notice.

So it is hardly surprising that the proposal was met with both shock and skepticism.

### Point Two: Can You Legally Put a Tollbooth at Sea?

On July 13, the International Maritime Organization reaffirmed the principle that ships passing through the Strait of Hormuz should not be charged tolls or transit fees under international law.

In other words, the proposal did not just unsettle the markets. It also collided head-on with the fundamental principle of freedom of navigation.

## In One Line

**“The tollbooth sign is gone—but the smell of gunpowder still hangs over the strait.”**

A policy reversal may calm the headlines. But the market will not truly relax until ships can pass through the Strait of Hormuz without fear.
