20% Hormuz Fee Dropped, but Oil Market Remains Nervous

Imagine a giant tollbooth suddenly appearing in the Strait of Hormuz.

On July 13, 2026, U.S. President Donald Trump announced a new plan. He wanted to collect 20 percent of the value of all cargo passing through the strait. He said the money would pay for keeping this important waterway safe.

But the plan was withdrawn just one day later. Gulf nations would instead seek trade and investment deals with the United States. It was unclear how much money was involved or which countries would take part.

The message changed overnight from “Pay 20 percent to pass” to “Invest in the United States instead.”

Within 24 hours, the plan had changed completely. But did the oil markets feel calmer?

Only partly.

Brent crude oil had risen about 10 percent the day before. Tensions between the United States and Iran were growing. The proposed 20 percent charge also shocked the market.

After the plan was canceled, oil fell from a daily high above $87 a barrel. However, Brent closed at $84.73 on July 14. That was still about 2 percent higher and its highest level in a month.

The reason was simple. The fee was gone, but other dangers remained.

A blockade against ships linked to Iran was still in place. Military attacks were also continuing.

The market was not only worried about the cost of passing through the strait. The bigger question was whether ships could pass through safely.

Today's Hormuz Risk Points

Point One: One Tanker Could Have Paid Almost $30 Million

A fully loaded Very Large Crude Carrier could have faced a fee of about $30 million. That is roughly 4.9 billion yen.

The shipping industry had received no warning before the announcement.

It was therefore not surprising that the plan caused shock and serious doubts.

Point Two: Is a Sea Toll Legal?

On July 13, the International Maritime Organization repeated an important rule.

Under international law, ships passing through the Strait of Hormuz should not pay tolls or transit fees.

The proposal did more than make markets nervous. It also went directly against the basic principle of freedom of navigation.

In One Line

“The tollbooth sign is gone, but the smell of gunpowder still hangs over the strait.”

Changing the policy may calm the headlines. But markets will not truly relax until ships can pass through the Strait of Hormuz without fear.
