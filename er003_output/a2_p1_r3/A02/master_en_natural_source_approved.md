# Midnight Means Goodnight: The UK’s Quiet Plan to Curb Late-Night Scrolling

At midnight, social media may soon start saying “goodnight” to teenagers in the UK.

On July 15, the British government announced plans to introduce a **digital switch-off period** for 16- and 17-year-olds, running from midnight to 6 a.m.

During those hours, covered apps would be unavailable by default, and notifications would be paused. Autoplay—the feature that keeps videos coming one after another—would also be switched off, along with the personalized recommendation feeds designed to keep users scrolling endlessly.

The new measures are expected to take effect in the spring of 2027.

But this would not be a locked gate.

Teenagers aged 16 and 17 would still be able to change the settings and opt out.

So, wouldn’t everyone simply turn the restrictions off?

Perhaps. But the UK’s strategy is less about issuing commands and more about shaping the **default experience**. To open an app in the middle of the night, users would first have to pause and actively choose to override the setting.

In other words, the plan is to place a small speed bump in front of that automatic urge to watch “just one more.”

## Today's Late-Night Scrolling Points

### Point One: A Nighttime Pause Proved More Practical Than a Total Ban

In a trial involving 309 families, nighttime restrictions from 9 p.m. to 7 a.m. were considered the easiest of three approaches to manage. They also produced the most consistent reports of improved sleep.

However, some teenagers simply shifted their screen time to just before or after the restricted hours. So closing off the night does not automatically make the wider problem disappear.

### Point Two: Even Optional Defaults Can Change Behavior

In a separate experiment by Ofcom, around seven in ten children kept a setting that prevented harmful content from being recommended when it was selected by default.

When no option was preselected, that figure fell to around half.

The setting was not mandatory. But the results suggest that defaults alone can be powerful enough to change the direction of people’s choices.

## In One Line

**“What the UK is trying to switch off is not the glow of the smartphone, but the endless current of scrolling that carries teenagers right up to bedtime.”**

This midnight speed bump sits somewhere between a strict ban and a complete hands-off approach. Its success will not be measured by how many teenagers change the setting, but by how many more nights they leave it untouched—and simply go to sleep.
