Midnight arrives. A phone lights up.

One alert leads to another. One video rolls into the next. Bedtime quietly slips away.

The UK government is planning a new response. It would not lock every door. Instead, it would change which doors are open by default.

As of 12 August 2026, the plan has not taken effect. Subject to parliamentary approval, the changes are expected to begin around spring 2027.

The plan covers social media users aged 16 and 17. From midnight to 6 a.m., nighttime protections would switch on automatically. Alerts and push notifications would be muted.

But this would not be an unchangeable curfew. Users could alter the settings. And the government has not said that social media must become completely inaccessible overnight.

There is also a separate, all-day change. Autoplay and personalised feeds would be off by default—not just overnight.

So the proposal is not simply saying: “Put the phone away.” It is asking whether the phone should work quite so hard to pull young people back in.

Two points worth watching

The pilot offers a clue—but not proof

A qualitative pilot conducted in 2026 involved about 300 households overall. Each included a young person aged 13 to 17 and a parent or guardian.

Families joined a control group or one of three intervention groups, based on their preferences.

Roughly 80 households were assigned to the night-curfew group. The intervention asked families to block selected social media apps from 9 p.m. until 7 the next morning.

Most reported following the rule fully. Some made minor changes, and a few did not comply. Their digital use was not independently monitored.

Among the three approaches tested, participants described the night curfew as the easiest to fit into daily life. They also reported the most consistent sleep-related improvements, including earlier bedtimes and feeling more alert the next day.

But this was a relatively small, self-selected study based mainly on personal reports. It points to possibilities. It does not prove cause and effect.

A quieter night may not mean less screen time

Many participants reported little major change in daytime social media use.

Instead, some activity moved. Young people used social media more before the restriction began, checked again after it ended, or switched to television and games.

That difference matters.

A nighttime setting may rearrange attention rather than erase it. And the planned policy is less rigid than the pilot's access block: it would use default protections that 16- and 17-year-olds could change.

The final details, including exactly which services will be covered, are still to come.

In one line…

Britain is not proposing a permanent lock on social media, but a midnight pause: quieter defaults, fewer automatic pulls, and a little more space before the next scroll.
