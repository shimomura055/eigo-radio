# Over $250 Million, Plus a Demand to Destroy Models: The Heavy Bill in an AI Lawsuit

“More than $250 million.” If you saw that in a headline, you might find yourself checking the number of zeros. But reading the amount alone is not enough to understand this lawsuit. The plaintiffs are also asking for models such as GPT that they say contain problematic content, as well as training sets, to be destroyed.

The plaintiffs are 14 companies and other entities in the group, including USA TODAY Co. In the complaint, they claim that OpenAI got and copied news content without permission and used it to train its models. They also say the models’ output copied or put articles together in new ways, and removed copyright management information.

But let’s pause here. For now, these are only the plaintiffs’ claims. They are not facts that a court has found to be true. What is written in a complaint and what a court accepts as true are two different things. Mixing them up changes the story.

They seek more than $250 million in damages. They also seek the return of profits, a court order to stop the conduct, and costs, among other things. That is already a large claim, but the demand to destroy models and training sets draws even more attention. The focus shifts sharply from “How much should be paid?” to “What can be kept?”

It is like seeing the total on a bill and being shocked, then turning to the next page and finding an item called “how to handle models and data.” “Please pay for using the articles” and “Please also destroy what we claim you made using those articles” are different demands.

Of course, asking for something to be destroyed and having it ordered to be destroyed are two different things. A large demand does not mean it will happen as requested. Focusing only on the “high claim” or jumping to the conclusion that “the destruction of the models has been decided” would both be misreadings.

This is also a story that may interest people who often ask AI questions or have it summarize writing. When we get a useful answer, we tend to focus on “what it can do.” But this lawsuit turns a spotlight on what happens behind the scenes: “Then, what was it made from?”

Still, we should not jump ahead to the ending. The case is still at the filing stage, and the court has not found that there was copyright infringement or accepted the claims. At the time of Reuters’ report, OpenAI had not commented right away. For now, all we know is that demands have been made that cannot be summed up as “pay and it’s over.” How much of that bill will be accepted remains to be seen.

## In one line
Fourteen news companies and related entities are asking a court to destroy OpenAI’s models and training sets they say contain their content.
