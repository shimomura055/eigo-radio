# More Than $250 Million—and a Demand to “Destroy the Models,” Too: AI Lawsuits Are About More Than Money

When you hear “more than $250 million,” you may first want to count the zeros. But in this AI lawsuit, if you focus only on the number, you may miss another major demand. The plaintiffs are seeking more than damages. Their list of demands also includes the “destruction” of models such as GPT.

The lawsuit against OpenAI was filed by 14 companies and other entities under USA TODAY Co. The plaintiffs claim that OpenAI obtained and copied news content without permission, then used it to train AI models. They say their articles may have been used as training material without their consent.

The dispute goes beyond what went into the models during training. The plaintiffs also claim that content was copied or recreated in the models’ outputs. They further accuse OpenAI of removing copyright management information. But let’s pause here: these are all claims made by the plaintiffs, not facts that a court has found to be true.

So what are they seeking? More than $250 million in damages, the return of profits, and a court order to stop the conduct. And one demand that stands out is the destruction of GPT and other models, along with training data sets, that the plaintiffs say include their content.

In a sense, the demands now reach from the “wallet” to the “training material” and even to the “model after training.” This is not just a matter of money: “Pay us for what you used.” It also asks what should happen next to the AI that is said to have used that material. This lawsuit goes that far.

Still, it is much too soon to run a breaking-news banner in your head saying, “GPT to be destroyed.” A claim is not a preview of a court ruling. At this stage, what we know is that a lawsuit has been filed; the court has not found copyright infringement or made a ruling on the main issues. There is a big gap between “they asked for the models to be destroyed” and “it has been decided that they will be destroyed.”

At the time of Reuters’ report, OpenAI had not yet responded to a request for comment.

In AI news, performance and convenience often take center stage. This time, the spotlight is on “what material the AI learned from” behind the scenes, and on what happens to the models after training. The key point is that a dispute over the use of training material has now led to demands about the finished models as well.

It would be a shame to close the article after being shocked by the number of zeros in the damages figure. But it is also important not to jump ahead to the ending. What has now been brought to court is a question about AI training material and how the models should be handled—not the answer.

## In one line
Fourteen USA TODAY-affiliated entities suing OpenAI are asking a court to destroy models and training sets they say include their content.
