# Leave the Phone Calls to Us! But First, Tell Me Who Will Make Them

“Make that call for me.” It would be a welcome help if it took care of an annoying task. But what if the person actually speaking on the call was a human staff member? “That helps. But tell me first who will be doing it!” This time, the main issue is the question of “who does the work?”—hidden behind the convenience.

Meta was testing a system in which trained contract workers made some calls through Muse and completed the conversations with the people they called. In other words, there were cases where a human actually spoke to the person on the other end.

If the task is done, that’s the end of it—or so you might think. But it’s too soon to roll the end credits. “Did the call go well?” and “Who handled my information?” are separate questions. A call doesn’t get a perfect score just because “task completed” gets a gold star on its report card.

The person asking someone else to make the call wants to know who will be involved with their information. The person receiving the call may also want to know who they are talking to before they speak. That’s a point worth thinking about; you can’t just close the curtain by saying, “That’s behind the scenes.”

In fact, employees were concerned that sensitive user information might be shared with contract workers by mistake. But we mustn’t jump to saying, “Information was shared!” Concern about something possibly happening and proof that it actually happened are two different things. We cannot assume from this story that any information was shared.

In other words, the issue is not “It’s wrong for a human to make calls.” If a human is involved, what will be shared, when, and how? The reveal, “Actually, it was a human,” might get applause in a magic show. But with a service you trust to handle your request, it could make you say, “You should have told me first.”

A vice president in Meta’s Superintelligence Labs division admitted that it was a “mistake” to start a test in which contract workers made calls without giving people proper information. The human concierge feature was then rolled back for the time being.

But wait before putting up a big caption that says, “Muse completely shut down!” What was rolled back was the human concierge feature, not all of Muse. If we make the story sound bigger than it is, the main issue can disappear off the screen.

What we want from a convenient service is not just efficiency. Who will handle the task, and who will join the conversation? Explaining that is also an important part of the service. Explain things before connecting the call. Before “Please handle it,” there should be an “I see.” That may be the right order for trusting someone with a task.

## In one line
Meta rolled back Muse’s human concierge feature after admitting it had tested contractors making some calls without proper disclosure.
