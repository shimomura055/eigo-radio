# Wastewater’s Journey Is Changing: From Underground Main Arteries to Behind Homes

Water from toilets and kitchens has two routes through a town. One route goes through underground pipes to a large treatment plant. The other route is treated in a septic tank near the home.

In areas with large populations and many homes close together, a public sewer system that collects everyone’s wastewater in pipes is useful. In a way, it is a main artery running under the town.

But when the population falls, this main artery runs into a problem. Fewer people use the sewer system, so the fees paid to local governments also fall. At the same time, the underground pipes and treatment plants grow old. The cost of repairing or replacing old equipment keeps rising.

The number of passengers is falling, but the cost of repairing the railway is rising. For a sewer system, this is a very difficult situation.

So a new way of thinking has appeared: instead of using the same method everywhere in a town, choose the route again based on how closely the homes are built. Public sewers are suitable where homes are close together. Where homes are spread out, it may be more economical to treat the wastewater at each home than to extend long pipes and connect them to a large plant.

Kitakata City in Fukushima Prefecture reviewed its wastewater treatment plan because the cost of maintaining its aging facilities was rising, while income from user fees was falling as the population decreased. Areas that are not served by a shared treatment system will become areas for individual treatment using combined-treatment septic tanks. The city plans to provide extra financial support for the cost of installing the tanks.

A combined-treatment septic tank is like a small treatment plant near the home. It treats not only toilet water but also household wastewater from the kitchen, bath, and laundry. Instead of continuing to send the water to a large treatment plant, this system ends the wastewater’s journey near the home.

However, septic tanks need care. They are not devices that can be left alone after they are installed. Users must have them inspected and cleaned. In principle, they need to be managed at least once a year, and the number of times varies according to the type and treatment method.

In an age of population decline, sewer systems are not disappearing. They are trying to change their form. One is a large route running under the town, and the other is a small route working behind homes. An age is beginning in which these two routes are used in different ways to match the shape of each region.

## In one line
As populations decline, towns are using shared sewers in dense areas and septic tanks near scattered homes.
