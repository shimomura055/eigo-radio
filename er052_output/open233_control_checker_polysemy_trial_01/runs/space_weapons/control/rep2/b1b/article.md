# Space Weapons Have Addresses. That Is Where Things Get Complicated

When it comes to weapons in space, it may be easier to understand them by looking at an address book than a list of performance figures. Are they on the ground, or are they orbiting? That alone changes the meaning of the same space weapon.

On September 14, 2026, the US secretary of the Air Force acknowledged that the United States was deploying space-control weapons in orbit to protect its joint forces from the actions of hostile opponents. It was the first time a US government agency had officially acknowledged the Space Force's deployment of weapons in space.

But the details are still hidden. The weapon's name, what kind of attack ability it has, and what it targets have not been revealed. The only thing we know is that it is being deployed in orbit.

This brings us to ground-launched weapons that attack satellites. In 2021, Russia launched a missile from the ground and destroyed a satellite. As a result, more than 1,500 pieces of trackable debris were created. The missile flew into space, but the weapon's address was still the ground. That is the difference from the deployment in orbit that the United States acknowledged this time.

Also, counterspace, which means operations against space, is not limited to work in outer space. The stage can be a satellite's orbit, the communications linking satellites and the ground, or facilities on the ground. You can jam radio signals, launch cyberattacks, or attack facilities on the ground. There are ways to stop a satellite from working without shooting it directly. In other words, you can make trouble for space without going into space.

Defense, too, is not only about flashy weapons. It can mean warning of danger early, making equipment harder to destroy, spreading out its functions, keeping backups, or moving a satellite so that an attack misses. The US Space Force divides these measures into active defense and passive defense.

There is also an easy-to-miss line in the law. Article IV of the Outer Space Treaty bans placing nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction in Earth orbit or on celestial bodies. However, it does not ban all space weapons. Space security changes depending not only on whether weapons are fired, but also on the weapon's address, where the attack takes place, and how defense is carried out. Our daily lives, which rely on communications and location information, are not unrelated to that address book.

## In one line
The key to understanding space weapons is their address: attacks can affect space without entering it.
