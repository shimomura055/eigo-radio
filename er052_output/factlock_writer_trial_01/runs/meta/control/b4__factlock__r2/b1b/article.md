# A Human Appears Behind the Scenes of Phone Calls. Where the Information Goes Also Matters

Let’s take a look backstage. An unexpected performer was on the stage of a system that handles phone calls for you. Of course, from here on, the stage is a metaphor.

Meta was testing a system in which, for some calls through Muse, trained contract workers—not AI—made the calls and completed the conversation with the person on the other end.

Let’s imagine the information needed to move a call forward as stage props. If backstage staff handle those props, would users in the audience be able to see it?

Employees were worried that sensitive information about users, needed for the calls, might accidentally be shared with contract workers. Now, let’s take out a detective’s magnifying glass. This is not a story about a major information leak. The concern was that information might be shared.

And the curtain came down on the test. Meta executives admitted it was a “mistake” to start testing a system in which contract workers made calls without properly explaining it, and rolled back the human concierge feature for now. But the curtain came down only on the human concierge feature. Meta did not stop Muse as a whole.

The main point this time is not just a guessing game about who is on the other end of the call: an AI or a person. If you let someone handle a call, you want to know who will do it, what information might be passed along, and whether that has been explained to you. It is the kind of story that makes you want to see the cast list and prop list—in other words, who is responsible and where the information goes.

## In one line
Meta paused its human call feature after failing to explain that contractors, not AI, handled some calls.
