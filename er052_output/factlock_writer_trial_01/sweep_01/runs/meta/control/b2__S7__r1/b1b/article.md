# A Human Was Making the AI’s Phone Calls

Hey, imagine asking AI to make a phone call, only to find out that a human was the one who made it. Isn’t that a little surprising? For some calls through Muse, Meta tested having trained human contractors, rather than AI, make the calls and finish the conversations with the other person.

The interesting part is that, instead of AI helping people, people were playing the role of AI. And they weren’t just making calls in its place. Meta employees raised privacy concerns that sensitive information about users, needed to keep the conversation going, might be shared with call center contractors by mistake.

Of course, this does not mean that “information was leaked on a large scale.” The concern was only that this kind of information sharing might happen. But if people are involved in a call you thought you had asked AI to make, you’d want to know first who would handle it and what information would be shared, right? More important than having a human help is whether users were told about it.

Meta’s vice president of the team in charge also admitted that starting a test in which contractors made calls without giving proper notice was a “mistake,” and rolled back the human concierge feature for now. However, Muse as a whole was not shut down.

You think it’s an AI service, but people are working behind it. Learning how this convenience works is a little interesting, but it also makes you think, “I wish they’d told me that first.” Whether users can tell if they are dealing with AI or a person may be more important than we think for future services.

## In one line
Meta tested having human contractors, not AI, make some Muse calls without properly telling users.
