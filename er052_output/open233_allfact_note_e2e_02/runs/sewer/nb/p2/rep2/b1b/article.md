# Let the City's Density Decide What Comes After the Toilet

In the morning, you turn the toilet handle. Water flows out of the house. What happens after that?

The plan revised by Matsuyama City is about exactly what happens after that. Should wastewater be collected through pipes spread under the city, or should it be treated by equipment at each home? The city decided that the answer should differ by area.

Behind this are the financial problems of sewer services. When the population falls, income from user fees falls too. At the same time, as facilities get older, the cost of rebuilding and replacing them rises. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism explains that this drop in income and rise in costs make it harder to run sewer services.

So Matsuyama City decided not to use the same method across the whole city, but to change its approach to fit how homes are grouped.

In urbanized areas, public sewers are used in principle. Wastewater from areas where homes are close together is collected by a large underground road—the pipes—and then treated together. According to the Ministry of the Environment, this kind of shared treatment may be more economical in areas where homes are densely packed.

On the other hand, in urbanization control areas, household wastewater treatment tanks are used in principle. These are systems placed at each home. They treat not only toilet wastewater, but also water from the kitchen, bath, and washing machine. They are small treatment plants for each home that use the work of tiny living things.

The Ministry of the Environment also says that the method should be chosen by looking at the shape of the city. In areas where homes are spread out, septic tanks may be more economical. In crowded areas, public sewers and other shared treatment systems may be more economical.

In other words, a sewer system is not simply an underground pipe. In places with many homes, everyone's wastewater is put into one flow. In places where homes are far apart, each home treats its own wastewater. The population density of the city decides the route the wastewater takes.

But this point is important. Matsuyama City did not decide to dig up the old sewer pipes already in place and immediately replace them with septic tanks. What it revised was the planned area for the public sewer system as a whole, and the method to be used there.

Every time we flush the toilet, the shape of the city and changes in its population affect the system underground. When you think about it that way, the familiar sound of running water starts to sound a little different.

## In one line
Matsuyama City will choose shared sewers or household treatment tanks according to how densely homes are grouped.
