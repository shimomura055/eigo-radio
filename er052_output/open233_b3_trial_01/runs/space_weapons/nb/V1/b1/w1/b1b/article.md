# The Address Book of Space Weapons: Ground-Launched and Orbital Weapons Are Not the Same

Here is a small space quiz. If a missile flies up and destroys a satellite, does that mean a weapon has been deployed in space? Not always. The main point this time is not the explosion, but the weapon’s address.

On September 14, the U.S. Secretary of the Air Force said that the United States was deploying space-control weapons in orbit to protect the entire U.S. military from the actions of hostile opponents. An official government article recorded this as the first statement admitting that the Space Force had deployed weapons in space.

Now let’s open the address book.

In the past, Russia used ground-launched ASAT missiles, or anti-satellite missiles, to destroy its own satellites. This also created more than 1,500 pieces of trackable orbital debris.

This was an event in which a weapon on the ground made a business trip into space. The weapon itself did not start living in orbit. Weapons that attack space from the ground and weapons placed in space may look similar, but they have different addresses.

“Counterspace” is another term whose address is easy to confuse. According to the U.S. Space Force, it includes not only attacks on an opponent’s satellites in orbit, but also blocking communications with electromagnetic waves, cyberattacks, and attacks on facilities on the ground. It is a broad category covering methods of attack involving space.

Just seeing the name “counterspace” does not mean that the weapon is in orbit.

On the other hand, providing location information, tracking missiles, monitoring space, and making satellites harder to destroy are also missions of the Space Force. These are jobs that involve operating or protecting satellites. Wearing a Space Force uniform does not mean that everyone is in charge of attacks. That is the difference.

There is also a rulebook called the Outer Space Treaty. What Article IV clearly bans is placing objects carrying nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction in orbit around Earth, or placing such weapons on celestial bodies such as the Moon. It also bans military bases, fortifications, weapons testing, and military exercises on celestial bodies.

However, this article does not ban all conventional weapons in outer space. We also cannot decide that this announcement alone is a violation of the treaty. What we need to look at is the type of weapon, its address, and how it is placed.

Space security is not only a contest over weapon performance. Is it an attack from the ground, interference with communications, protection for satellites, or deployment in orbit? When you see a news report, first check the weapon’s resident record. In space, an address itself becomes a title.

## In one line
A missile that attacks satellites from Earth is not the same as a weapon stationed in orbit.
