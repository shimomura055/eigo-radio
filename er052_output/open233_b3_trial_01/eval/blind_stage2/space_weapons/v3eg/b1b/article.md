# The Space Contest Does Not End When a Satellite Is Shot Down

When you hear about war in space, don't you picture a satellite being targeted from the ground, followed by a huge explosion in the sky?

Attacks like that do happen. They are ground-launched ASATs, or anti-satellite weapons. In 2021, Russia destroyed a satellite in this way. As a result, more than 1,500 pieces of trackable space debris were created.

Even though only one satellite was destroyed, nearby orbits also became dangerous. Like a game on the ground, a space war does not end just because you take the other side's piece.

What the United States officially acknowledged for the first time this time was not a weapon fired from the ground. On September 14, the Secretary of the Air Force said that the United States was deploying space-control weapons in orbit to protect the U.S. military's joint forces from adversaries. An official article by the U.S. Space Force recorded the statement as the first instance in which deployment in space was acknowledged.

This suddenly makes the playing field of the space contest much wider. The counterspace attacks mentioned by the U.S. Space Force are not limited to destroying satellites.

One setting is orbit. The satellite itself can be targeted. Another is the communication link between a satellite and the ground. Electromagnetic attacks and cyberattacks can interfere with that connection. Ground facilities can also be targets.

In other words, even without making a satellite explode, it is possible to disrupt communications or target the ground side. Although this is a battle in space, the battlefield is not only above us.

The defending side's operations are not limited to dramatic exchanges of fire, either. The U.S. Space Force divides defense into active and passive measures. It lists warnings about threats, hardening equipment to make it harder to damage, spreading functions across systems, and redundancy, or keeping backups.

Do not put everything on one satellite. Even if it is destroyed, another satellite or system can keep working. Space defense is also a surprisingly low-key but persistent strategy.

So, if a weapon is placed in orbit, is that immediately a treaty violation? Article IV of the Outer Space Treaty bans nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction in orbit. It does not ban all space weapons.

Destroy satellites, cut communications, target the ground, and keep functioning even after being attacked. This news shows us that the space contest is taking place on a playing field much wider than we might have thought.

## In one line
The United States has acknowledged deploying weapons in orbit, showing that space warfare extends beyond destroying satellites.
