# The Fee Plan That Disappeared the Day After It Appeared, and the Return of Oil Prices

If you only follow the news, it is a short political drama. The main story on the first day was a plan to charge a 20% reimbursement fee on cargo passing through the Strait of Hormuz. The main story the next day was the plan that replaced it. And finally, oil prices made an unexpected move.

First came July 13. President Trump announced a policy to seek a 20% reimbursement from all cargo passing through the strait, as payment for the cost of keeping the Strait of Hormuz safe. The idea was to get back the costs of the safety and security services provided by the United States.

However, while the figure of 20% was announced, the details of the system were still unclear. Who would pay? What would the amount be based on? How would the money be collected? No details like these were given. A large sign had been put up, but the way the store would work still had to be decided. That was the situation.

But the next day, July 14, the story took a sudden turn. President Trump posted that the 20% US reimbursement fee would be replaced by trade and investment deals with the United States by Gulf countries. He explained that the decision was based on “very productive talks” with Middle Eastern leaders.

He also told reporters that no one should charge ships passing through the Strait of Hormuz, and that he did not like the idea of a fee itself. The fee plan that had appeared the day before had left the main stage by the next day.

This brings us to Brent futures, an international standard for crude oil prices. After the announcement of the withdrawal and replacement, Brent futures first gave back some of their gains. But they did not continue to fall sharply. Soon, they returned to a high level close to where they had been before the announcement.

At the time the article was published, they were about 2.6% higher than the day before. The price was above $85 a barrel. Even though the fee plan had disappeared, the market did not become completely calm.

Of course, there is not enough evidence to say that the withdrawal pushed prices higher. What can be confirmed is that the gains narrowed for a while, and then recovered.

At the same time, attacks between the United States and Iran, a sea blockade, and concerns about tanker safety continued. Even after the fee plan left the stage, other causes for concern remained. That is why the market's reaction was not simply, “The plan was withdrawn, so prices fell.”

The news was about a faraway strait, but the international market is not a stage where one announcement decides the whole story. Even when the main actor changes, the tension in the background does not always disappear. The oil market showed this in a short time今回.
