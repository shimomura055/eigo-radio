# A 20% Reimbursement Fee Plan Turns into Investment Talk the Next Day: A Sudden Turn in Strait News

This news story changes its main subject in just one day.

On July 13, President Trump introduced a plan to seek a 20% reimbursement fee from all cargo passing through the Strait of Hormuz. The idea was to get back the money the United States spends to keep the strait safe.

Looking only at the number, it was very strong. For those carrying goods, it looked as if a huge 20% price tag had suddenly been placed in front of them.

But there was no detailed explanation behind this price tag. Who would collect it, and who would pay it? What would the 20% apply to? What currency would be used? Would there be exemptions? How would it be enforced? What would its legal basis be? No concrete plan for how the system would work was shown.

The fee sign was put up. But there was still no payment counter or way to calculate it. That was the situation.

Then, the next day, the scene changed. Trump announced that he would replace the 20% reimbursement fee plan with trade and investment deals between Gulf countries and the United States. He explained that the decision was based on “very productive discussions” with Middle Eastern leaders.

The day before, the topic was cargo passing through the strait. The next day, it was trade and investment with the United States. It was as if the 20% plan had been put on a different vehicle and sent toward a new destination.

So how did oil prices react? This is the most interesting part of the news.

After the replacement was announced, Brent crude futures briefly lost part of their rise. But soon they returned to a high level close to where they had been before the announcement. At the time of reporting, they were up about 2.6% from the day before and had passed $85 a barrel.

The story did not simply become: oil would fall because the 20% plan had disappeared.

On July 13, Brent rose 9.59% in one day and settled at $83.30 a barrel. Reuters linked this rise to a U.S. naval blockade of Iran that was expected to begin the next day, as well as concerns about energy shipments through the Strait of Hormuz. The next day, attacks by the United States and Iran, the naval blockade, and worries about tanker safety continued.

One useful way to see this is not to focus too much on the 20% plan that disappeared. The plan changed shape the next day, but the question of whether ships could pass safely remained. A flashy policy post may change its destination, but the tension at sea did not move along with it. The price movement in this case shows that sudden turn.

## In one line
Although Trump replaced the 20% Strait fee with investment deals, oil prices stayed high as shipping risks remained.
