# Interest Rates: Watch Only the Central Bank, and You’ll Miss the Action

Think of interest-rate news like a sports show on TV. When the central bank makes a move, everyone watches. But bond markets and worries about inflation also affect mortgage rates. A policy-rate rise does not mean mortgage rates rise by the same amount.

On September 16, the U.S. central bank met to set money policy. It raised the policy rate by 0.25 percentage points, to 3.75%–4.00%. The economy and spending at home are strong. But inflation is still high. The aim was to bring rising prices back to the 2% target sooner. But this is what officials hope will happen. It does not mean the rate rise has already had an effect.

Let’s look at the mortgage-rate figures. For a 30-year fixed mortgage, the national average rate was 5.98% in late February. By October 8, it had risen to 7.40%. The Associated Press estimated the effect for a $400,000 loan. The rate change would add about $376 to each monthly payment. Of course, this is an example based on an estimate. It does not mean everyone who borrows will pay the same extra amount each month.

Let’s also check the rules of the game. With a fixed-rate mortgage, the rate in the contract stays the same during repayment. Even if the policy rate rises, payments do not automatically go up for people who have already borrowed. With an adjustable-rate mortgage, the rate may change under the contract’s terms.

So, who moves the mortgage-rate game? The players are inflation, central bank policy, and what bond-market investors think about the economy. The recent rise was also linked to changes in the bond market and greater worries about inflation because of higher oil prices. In other words, it is too soon to blame only the September rate rise.

When you look at mortgage rates, watch not only what the central bank says, but also what the market worries about. When planning your budget for a big purchase, remember that just one player does not decide the interest-rate game.

## In one line
Mortgage rates depend not only on central bank decisions, but also on inflation and what bond-market investors expect.
