# Leave the Phone Calls to Us! But First, Tell Me Who Will Make Them

“Make that call for me.” It would be nice if that took care of an annoying task. But what if a staff member was really speaking on the call? “That helps. But tell me first who will make it!” This time, the main question is, “Who does the work?” It is hidden behind the convenience.

Meta was testing a system where trained contract workers made some calls using Muse. They also finished the conversations with the people they called. In some cases, a real person spoke to the person at the other end.

If the task is done, you might think that is the end. But it is too soon to roll the end credits. “Did the call go well?” and “Who handled my information?” are different questions. A call does not get a gold star on its report card just for being marked “Task completed.”

The person asking someone else to make the call wants to know who will handle their information. The person receiving the call may want to know who they are talking to before they speak. That is worth thinking about. You cannot close the curtain by saying, “That is behind the scenes.”

In fact, employees worried that private user information might be shared with contract workers by mistake. But we must not jump to saying, “Information was shared!” Concern that something might happen is not proof that it happened. We cannot assume from this story that any information was shared.

In other words, the issue is not, “It is wrong for a human to make calls.” If a human is involved, what will be shared, when, and how? The reveal, “Actually, it was a human,” might get applause in a magic show. But with a service you trust with your request, you might say, “You should have told me first.”

A vice president in Meta’s Superintelligence Labs division called it a “mistake” to start a test. Contract workers made calls in the test, without people getting proper information first. The human concierge feature was then rolled back for now.

But wait before putting up a big caption that says, “Muse completely shut down!” What was rolled back was the human concierge feature, not all of Muse. If we make the story sound bigger than it is, the main issue can disappear off the screen.

A useful service should do more than work quickly. Who will do the task? Who will join the conversation? Explaining this is also an important part of the service. Explain it before you connect the call. Before “Please handle it,” there should be an “I see.” That may be the right order when trusting someone with a task.

## In one line
Meta rolled back Muse’s human concierge feature after admitting it tested contractors making some calls without telling people enough.
