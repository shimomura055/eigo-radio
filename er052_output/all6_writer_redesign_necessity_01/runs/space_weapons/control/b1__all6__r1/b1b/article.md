# Try Sorting Space News

“US deploys weapons in space.” Seeing this headline might make a chase between satellites start playing in your head. But it is too soon to start that movie on your own. What we learned this time is that the US acknowledged deploying space-control weapons in orbit. It has not said what they are aimed at or what kind of attack they would carry out.

In September, the US Secretary of the Air Force said the deployment was meant to protect joint forces from the actions of hostile parties. US government agencies recorded the statement as the first time the US had acknowledged the Space Force deploying weapons in space. If we stick a “space weapon” label on everything and push it all onto one shelf, we will miss the differences.

In the US Space Force’s framework, “counterspace activities” are not just about things in orbit. They also include attacks on, and defenses of, satellite communications and ground facilities. These can include blocking communications with radio waves or carrying out cyberattacks. On the other hand, there are ways to protect satellites, such as warning about threats, making satellites stronger, and spreading out their functions. Even when it is all about space, we should sort weapon deployments and protective measures under different labels. Satellites are also linked to communications and location data, so this is not just a quiz on military terms.

Russia’s satellite-destruction test is a good one for practicing how to sort these things. Russia launched a missile from the ground and destroyed a satellite, creating more than 1,500 pieces of space debris that could be tracked. But this was a destructive test launched from the ground. It is a different card from a story about weapons deployed in orbit.

The treaty card also needs careful reading. The Outer Space Treaty bans placing objects carrying nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction in orbit around Earth, putting such weapons on the Moon or other celestial bodies, or deploying them in space in any other way. It also bans military bases and fortifications on celestial bodies, as well as weapons tests and military exercises on celestial bodies. In other words, it is not a treaty that bans all space weapons. This statement alone also cannot tell us whether any particular weapon is legal or illegal.

The trick to reading space news is to check what kind of card it is before jumping at a dramatic headline. Is it a weapon in orbit, a ground-launched weapon, interference with communications, or a way to protect satellites? Once we have sorted the cards, we can read stories about space with our feet on the ground.

## In one line
The US has acknowledged weapons in orbit, but hasn’t revealed their targets or what attacks they might carry out.
