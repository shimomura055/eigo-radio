# Space Weapon Property Listings: Ground Launch and Putting Weapons in Orbit Are Different Options

If military news about space confuses you, do not start with the performance table. Look at the address first. Is the weapon on the ground? Is it in orbit? Or does it target communication links and ground facilities? Even weapons that look alike are different cases when their addresses differ.

On September 14, 2026, the U.S. Secretary of the Air Force spoke. He said the United States was putting space-control weapons into orbit. They would protect joint forces from actions by hostile opponents. An official U.S. government article recorded this as the first statement. That statement admitted that the U.S. Space Force had put weapons in space.

But the public learned only that the property existed. We do not know the specific system's name. Its attack power and targets have not been confirmed. We learned that there was new property in space. But we have not seen its floor plan yet.

This is where the traditional anti-satellite missile comes in. Russia destroyed its satellite COSMOS 1408 with a direct-ascent ASAT missile. The missile was launched from the ground. More than 1,500 pieces of trackable debris were created.

In this test, the destroyed satellite was in space. But the missile used in the attack had its address on the ground. So a ground-launched missile can destroy a satellite in space. That is not the same as placing the weapon itself in orbit forever. The sign may say “space attack,” but the stores are in different places.

Also, “counterspace” does not mean only weapons in orbit. In the U.S. Space Force framework, it means attacks and defenses in three areas: orbit, communication links, and the ground. It may include electromagnetic attacks, cyberattacks, and attacks on ground facilities. It is a very large umbrella.

Systems used for protection are on a different shelf. Warning about threats, making satellites stronger, spreading out their functions, and keeping backups are defensive measures. Satellites that look for and track missiles are also examples of defensive sensor technology. They are not the same as attack weapons in orbit. However, active space defense may include weapons. A “defense” label does not always mean that something is not a weapon.

The Outer Space Treaty bans putting nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction into orbit around Earth. It also bans putting them on bodies in space, such as the Moon, or sending them into outer space in other ways. The treaty also bans military bases on such bodies, testing weapons, and military exercises there. However, this does not mean the treaty bans all space weapons from orbit. The rule is not the same for conventional weapons.

GPS and communications are also connected to our daily lives. In the end, military news about space is a game. We look at more than a weapon's power. We also look at what kind of property it is and where it has been placed.

In space warfare, the key question is not just a weapon’s power, but also where it is located.
