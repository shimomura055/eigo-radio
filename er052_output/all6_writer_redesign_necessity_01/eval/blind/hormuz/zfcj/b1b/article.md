# A Passage Fee Plan Became an Investment Deal the Next Day: Two Days in the Strait of Hormuz

It was as if only the news had been put on fast-forward for two days.

On July 13, U.S. President Trump said that he wanted all cargo passing through the Strait of Hormuz to pay a 20 percent “reimbursement fee.” He explained that the money would pay back the costs the United States spends to keep the strait safe.

It was as if a plan to put a toll booth in the strait had suddenly appeared. But no toll booth was actually built. This was only a proposal, not a fee that had really been collected. Who would pay it? What would be used to calculate it? How would it be collected? No details of the system were given. It was as if only the sign had been put up first.

But on the next day, July 14, the scene suddenly changed. Mr. Trump posted that the 20 percent reimbursement fee would be replaced by deals involving trade with and investment in the United States by Gulf countries. He explained that this decision was based on very productive talks with Middle Eastern leaders.

He also told reporters that no one should charge a fee to ships passing through the Strait of Hormuz, and that he did not like the idea of a fee itself.

The day before, it was “20 percent on cargo passing through the strait.” The next day, it was “trade and investment by the Gulf countries.” In the same news story, the talk changed from a fee to deals between countries, almost like a change of clothes. Before the policy was settled, the stage set seemed to have been replaced. It was a very sudden turn.

The oil market also did not have a simple ending. After the announcement that the fee plan had been withdrawn and replaced, Brent futures briefly gave up some of their gains. But soon they returned to a high level close to where they had been before the announcement. At the time of the report, they were above $85 a barrel, up about 2.6 percent.

During that time, concerns continued about attacks between the United States and Iran, a blockade at sea, and the safety of tankers. In other words, the main point this time was not that the 20 percent fee was put into effect. It was that the fee plan was replaced by another proposal in one day, and oil prices still returned to a high level. News about the strait may seem far away, but when oil prices move, the strait appears in our daily lives through fuel costs and transportation costs.

## In one line
A proposed 20 percent Strait of Hormuz fee was replaced the next day by Gulf trade and investment deals.
