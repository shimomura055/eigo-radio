# Human Staff Behind AI Calls: Were the Human Performers Introduced?

Is the voice on the other end of the phone AI, or a person? The story around Meta’s Muse is like a quiz to guess whose voice it is. But sometimes the answer is “a human.” What matters is not whether users can tell the voices apart, but whether they were told beforehand.

In an experiment involving some calls through Muse, trained contract workers, not AI, made the calls and handled the conversations from start to finish. It was like adding human performers to the cast of an AI phone service. When a person speaks, some conversations may go more smoothly.

But a phone call does not always stick to a script in what gets discussed. People may share not only the reason for the call, but also details about their lives and personal information. People feel differently when they speak thinking the other person is AI and when they know a human staff member is listening.

Meta employees raised concerns that having contract workers handle calls could lead to users’ personal information being unintentionally shared with call center staff. What matters here is that no actual leak was confirmed. The concern was that users might not know enough about who was listening.

The vice president of the department in charge admitted that it was a mistake to start testing calls handled by contract workers without a proper explanation. The feature that used human guides to handle calls was put on hold for now.

There is nothing wrong with people helping AI services. Human help can make them more useful in some cases. But a change in casting—when users think the caller is AI but it is actually a person—only works if users are told. As voice AI becomes more familiar, telling users who is on the other end of the phone is an important thing to make clear before the curtain rises.

## In one line
Meta paused a phone-call test after human workers, not AI, handled calls without users being properly informed.
