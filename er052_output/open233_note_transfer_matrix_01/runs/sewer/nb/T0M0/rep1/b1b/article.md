# Behind the Scenes of Sewage Treatment: Microbes Working Near Our Homes

Wastewater flows underground, through long pipes, toward treatment facilities far away. That is the image many people have of a sewer system.

But in areas where homes are spread out, the situation may be different. Instead of connecting homes with large pipes, methods that treat wastewater near the homes are also being considered.

The background is a falling population and aging facilities. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism explains that when the population falls, income from sewer fees also falls. At the same time, rebuilding and replacing old facilities costs money, making the service harder to run.

This is where combined-treatment septic tanks come in. They are not noticeable from the outside, but microbes work inside them. They treat not only waste from toilets, but also household wastewater from kitchens, baths, and laundry.

In other words, they are small wastewater treatment plants placed near homes. They shorten the distance that water must travel to a large facility and make use of the power of microbes near each home. It may seem unexciting, but as an idea, it is bold.

This does not mean that septic tanks are better in every area. The Ministry of the Environment says that in areas where homes are spread out, individual septic tanks may be more economical, while in areas where homes are close together, public sewers and other systems that treat wastewater together may be more economical. The method is chosen by looking at the way homes are arranged, construction costs, maintenance costs, and other factors.

Kitakata City reviewed its wastewater treatment plan because the aging of its facilities had increased maintenance costs, while the falling population had reduced income from sewer fees. It designated areas outside the areas served by group treatment as areas for individual treatment with combined-treatment septic tanks, and said it would also provide extra subsidies for installation costs. This does not mean removing all the public sewers that have already been built. It means reviewing the areas and the plan.

And the story does not end there. A septic tank is not a box that keeps working automatically once it is put in place. For a small household septic tank, maintenance checks are basically needed at least once every four months, and cleaning is needed at least once a year. The number of times changes depending on the treatment method and the number of people using it. In addition to maintenance checks and cleaning, an inspection required by law is also needed once a year.

The burden of sewer service does not disappear; it simply moves from underground to near the home. Only when someone takes care of it to the end can the microbes keep putting on their fine performance.

## In one line
In areas with fewer, widely spread homes, septic tanks can treat wastewater nearby but still require regular care.
