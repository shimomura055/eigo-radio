# Choosing the Main System for Wastewater Treatment: Large Sewers and Septic Tanks for Each Home

After you flush the toilet, where does that water go?

One route carries it through a large sewer system to a local treatment facility. The other treats it in a septic tank at each home. Wastewater treatment has these two styles.

Now, this choice of which system should take the lead is drawing attention.

As income from sewer fees falls because of population decline and other factors, costs for rebuilding and renewing old facilities rise. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism explains that these two trends make it harder to run sewer services.

The Ministry of the Environment is calling for a choice of system that fits the shape of each area. In areas where homes are close together, collective treatment, such as public sewer systems, may be more economical. In areas where homes are spread out, individual treatment with septic tanks may be more economical. This does not mean that septic tanks are always cheaper. The choice is made by looking at how the homes are arranged, the cost of building the system, and the cost of maintaining it.

Kitakata City reviewed its wastewater treatment plan because maintenance costs were rising as its facilities grew old, while fee income was falling as the population declined. After the review, areas outside the collective-treatment areas were designated as areas for individual treatment with combined-treatment septic tanks. The city also decided to offer an extra subsidy for the cost of installing septic tanks.

The combined-treatment septic tank mentioned here is not a system that treats only toilet wastewater. It also takes in household wastewater from the kitchen, bathroom, and washing machine. Microorganisms do the work inside it and treat the wastewater. It is a small treatment facility located near the home.

But being small does not mean that it has less work to do. Rather, that is where management comes in.

Those responsible for septic tanks must carry out maintenance checks and cleaning. For small household septic tanks, maintenance checks are basically required at least once every four months. Cleaning is required at least once a year. During cleaning, built-up sludge and other material are pumped out. There are also legally required inspections, separate from maintenance checks and cleaning.

In a nationwide survey for fiscal 2024, the rate of maintenance checks for all septic tanks was a little over 70 percent, while the rate for cleaning was a little over 60 percent. The rate at which combined-treatment septic tanks received legally required inspections was also below 70 percent.

When a large sewer system is replaced by small septic tanks, what changes is the size of the facilities. The work of managing them does not disappear. Even if the main system for wastewater treatment becomes smaller, inspections and cleaning still have to be done.

## In one line
Switching from large sewers to household septic tanks makes facilities smaller, but maintenance is still required.
