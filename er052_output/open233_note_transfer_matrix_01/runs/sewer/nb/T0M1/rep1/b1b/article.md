# From a Giant Sewer System to a Microbe Team for Each Home

Where does the water go after you flush the toilet? Many people may picture it traveling through long pipes to some large treatment facility.

This is an effective method in areas where many homes are close together. Everyone's wastewater can be collected in one large system and treated together.

But when the population falls, things change. If fewer people use the sewer system, income from user fees also falls. Even so, the cost of repairing or replacing old pipes and facilities keeps rising. There are fewer users, but the equipment cannot wait. It is like maintaining a large stadium with a team that has fewer members.

This is where combined-treatment septic tanks come in.

These are small wastewater treatment facilities placed near homes. They treat not only toilet water, but also water from kitchens, baths, and washing machines. The main workers are microorganisms inside the facility. We cannot see them, but they work to clean household wastewater.

In areas where homes are close together, treating wastewater together through a large sewer system is often more economical. In areas where homes are far apart, it may be better to treat the water near each home than to lay out long pipes. In this way, the wastewater treatment team is reorganized to fit the shape of the town.

Kitakata City in Fukushima Prefecture reviewed its plan because maintenance costs had risen as facilities grew old, while income from user fees had fallen as the population decreased. Areas outside those served by shared treatment will become individual treatment areas using combined-treatment septic tanks, and the city will provide more help with installation costs.

However, this does not mean removing existing public sewers all at once. It means reorganizing which areas will use which treatment method from now on.

Small facilities also have work to do. Household septic tanks basically need maintenance checks at least once every four months and cleaning at least once a year. The number of times varies depending on the treatment method and the size.

From large sewer systems to small septic tanks. Even when the system is spread out, the work of invisible microbe teams and care by people remain essential. In an age of population decline, wastewater treatment may become smaller in scale, but the work itself does not disappear.

## In one line
As populations shrink, some towns are shifting from large sewer systems to smaller septic tanks near homes.
