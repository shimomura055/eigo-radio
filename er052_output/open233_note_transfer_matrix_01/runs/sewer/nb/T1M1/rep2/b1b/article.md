# Taking on the Big Sewer System: A Microbe Team for Each Home

In the world of wastewater treatment, there is an unexpected player: the combined-treatment septic tank. It does not look flashy. But it treats not only human waste from toilets, but also household wastewater from kitchens, baths, and washing. Microbes do the work inside. It is like having a small water-treatment team in every home.

The reason this player is getting attention is the sewer system’s tight financial situation. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism explains that falling income from sewer use fees due to population decline and other factors, along with rising costs for rebuilding and renewing aging facilities, can make sewer system operations more difficult.

Fewer people use the system. But old facilities still need care. Income shrinks, while renewal costs grow. For sewer systems, it is a tough game.

This is where choosing a system suited to each area comes in. According to the Ministry of the Environment, individual septic tanks may be more economical in areas where homes are spread out. In areas where homes are close together, shared treatment systems such as public sewers may be more economical.

In other words, sewer systems do not always win, and septic tanks do not always win. The best system changes depending on how homes are grouped. The shape of the area determines the choice.

Kitakata City reviewed its wastewater treatment plan because the cost of maintaining its aging facilities had risen and income from use fees had fallen due to population decline. It says that areas outside shared treatment zones will become individual treatment zones using combined-treatment septic tanks, with extra subsidies for installation costs.

However, this does not mean that all existing public sewers will be removed at once. It means reviewing the areas and the plan. Rather than destroying the whole large system, the city will choose smaller systems for different areas. That is how the game is being played.

Of course, the microbe team gets no days off. For a small household septic tank, maintenance inspections are basically needed at least once every four months, and cleaning is needed at least once a year. The number of times varies depending on the treatment method and the number of people served. Maintenance inspections and cleaning are separate tasks.

The smaller and more local the equipment is, the more important regular care becomes. Infrastructure in a society with a shrinking population is not just about competing in size. Can we properly manage the small players in each home? The key to winning is the power of microbes and human hands.

## In one line
As population decline makes public sewers harder to maintain, Kitakata is turning to household septic tanks in some areas.
