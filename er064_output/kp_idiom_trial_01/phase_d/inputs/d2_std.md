# A Bike Lane Plan Divides One Small Street

At Tuesday's town meeting, nobody expected a fight over paint on a road. The mayor wanted to add a bike lane to Maple Street. A shop owner, Mr. Ortiz, lost his temper almost at once. "You must be pulling my leg," he told the mayor. "Where will my customers park?"

His neighbor, Ms. Chen, bit her tongue for a while. She teaches at the school on the corner. For years she has watched cars rush past the gate. At last she spoke. "Parents kept an eye on their kids all last year, but a bus still came too close to a boy."

The mayor said the city counted 4,200 cars on the street each day. Only 300 bicycles used it. But he also said a bike lane would slow the cars and make the street safer. He promised that the plan would not take away more than six parking spaces.

Then a man in the back row stood up. He said a nearby city had built a similar lane two years ago. Shops there did not lose customers, he said. Mr. Ortiz did not believe him. "Prove it," he said. The man agreed to bring the numbers to the next meeting.

By the end of the night, the room was calmer. Some people still disagreed, but almost everyone agreed on one thing: they needed facts before a vote. The mayor said he would not decide until the report was ready.

Ms. Chen said she might change her mind if the numbers were clear. Mr. Ortiz said the same, but he laughed and added, "Don't spill the beans to the newspaper before I read it."

## In one line
A small town will wait for traffic and sales numbers before deciding on a bike lane.
