# When the Feed Fits — and Closes In

## The Question

A phone lights up on a morning train. One person sees stories that fit the few minutes she has. Another scrolls through a familiar stream and wonders what is missing. Across news apps, social media, and search, past clicks and interests help decide what appears first. The question is not simply good or bad. It is what this system feels like from two different seats.

### One voice: the commuter who counts on relevance

I open a news app’s “For you” feed on my commute, and stories linked to my interests and followed sources are already there. My past activity on connected services helps shape the list. I do not have to search through everything to find news relevant to my life.

I can follow a topic, hide a source, or ask for more or less of a subject. That gives me some control. In one survey, 37% of US respondents called automatic selection a good way to get news, more than those who chose editors.

I still worry that an important story could pass me by. I do not know every signal behind the ranking, and I rarely have time to adjust every setting. Still, the feed helps me keep up.

### Another voice: the reader who feels the feed closing in

I scroll through my feed and see the same kind of opinion again and again. After a while, I stop asking only whether a story is useful. I ask who decided this was what I should see. I cannot inspect the ranking rules or change their deeper settings. The system can be adjusted for clicks and advertising goals, while I am left with the result.

I am not saying every post changes my mind. I worry that my view of the world could narrow quietly. On one major social platform, ranking reduced exposure to news from different political sides, though users’ own choices played an even larger role. In one survey, 59% of US respondents worried about missing different views. I want to know what is outside my usual corner.

## Why They See It Differently

Both believe what they see can shape how they understand their lives. But one is protecting scarce time and access to relevant news; the other is protecting a wide view before a narrow one becomes invisible.

Their power is unequal. The first experiences ranking through choices like following and hiding. The second receives rules she cannot see. Their reasonable wishes do not make one answer: one asks for control over time, the other asks who controls the view. Short-term feed changes have not shifted tested political attitudes, but studies cannot show what repeated exposure may build over longer use.

## What This Reveals

This is not only a debate about better recommendations. It is about who shapes the first draft of daily reality: the person choosing what fits, or a system tuned to hold attention. Personalised news is therefore also a question of control—how much of the world remains available before convenience quietly becomes the boundary.
