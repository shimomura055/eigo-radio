# A Company Tries Three Days in the Office

A software company with 800 employees has spent a year testing a new schedule: three days in the office and two at home. The company said it would publish the results. Its first report came out this week.

Managers first had to figure out how to measure good work. They chose three signs: how fast projects were finished, how many mistakes customers found, and how workers rated their own stress. At first, many managers put off the study because they feared a drop in teamwork.

The results were mixed. Projects were finished two days earlier on average. Customer complaints went down by 9%, and sales picked up in the third quarter. But the company also ran into trouble. Younger workers said it was harder to learn from older colleagues when everyone was at home.

The new schedule comes with costs as well as gains. Office rent fell by 15%, but the company spent more on video tools and home equipment. The savings only show up in the report after the second quarter, when the extra spending slowed.

Some workers asked for full remote work, and the company turned down the request. A spokesperson said that meeting in person still matters for new employees. The numbers do not add up to a clear winner, she added.

The company plans to run the test for another year. If the results hold, other firms may follow. If the early gains fade, the company will go back to a five-day office week.

## In one line
A one-year test of a three-day office schedule shows faster projects and lower rent, but weaker learning for younger staff.
