# The Twist in an AI Phone Call: A Human Was on the Other End

A phone rings. On the screen, it looks as if Muse, an AI agent, is calling. The person on the other end would expect an AI conversation to begin.

But then the story turns.

In a test of Muse’s phone feature, some calls were not handled by AI. Human contract staff handled them instead. After training, they made the calls and completed the conversations. So behind a phone service with an AI label on it, human performers were working backstage.

At first, this may look like a small reveal. You may think AI is speaking, but it is a person. A human may sometimes be easier to understand than AI. If a conversation does not go well and the same explanation has to be repeated, it may be more helpful to have a human from the start.

But the real problem comes when we ask what callers think is happening.

### Who is listening changes what people share

On a phone call, people may share personal details they would rather keep private. Speaking to AI is different from possibly being heard by a contract call-center worker. If callers think AI is listening, they decide whether to speak on that basis. Knowing who is on the other end is a key condition, not a minor service detail.

That concern leads to another question: how was this change introduced?

### A problem in how the change began

The test with contract workers began without properly telling users. Inside the company, privacy concerns were raised: users’ sensitive information might reach staff without users meaning to share it. A Meta executive admitted that starting without enough notice was a mistake. The human-call feature was then rolled back for the time being.

## In one line

The deeper twist is that, just when we expected AI to replace humans, humans had entered AI’s backstage, so as AI phone calls become more common, it will likely matter to tell users who is on the other end, not only how smartly the phone can talk.
