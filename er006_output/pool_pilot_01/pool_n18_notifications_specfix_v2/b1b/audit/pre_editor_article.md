# The Phone Can Distract You Before You Look at It

You hear an alert. You keep your eyes on the work in front of you. You do not touch the phone. Is your attention really still yours?

That is the puzzle behind two experiments.

In a study published in PLOS ONE on November 17, 2022, 73 university students completed a visual task with 960 trials. Some trials included a notification sound. Others included a neutral sound. The students did not look at or touch a phone. Still, they reacted more slowly when the notification sound played. Brain-wave readings also showed a larger response. The researchers understood this as a sign that the students needed extra mental control to keep working.

A second study, published in Scientific Reports on June 8, 2023, moved the question to an even quieter moment. Forty-two university students took an attention test with a smartphone on the desk and with it in another room. The phone was switched off, and no notification arrived.

The scores were lower when the phone was on the desk. On the attention score, the average was 99.71 with the phone on the desk and 108.95 when it was elsewhere. For processing speed, the averages were 98.48 and 108.57.

The first experiment captures a sudden pull. The second points to a quieter cost before a sound arrives. Looking at a screen is not required for a phone to affect performance in these tests.

### Silent is not the same as away

That distinction matters when we think about protecting concentration. Stopping a sound and moving the phone away are different choices: one removes an incoming cue, while the other reduces the device’s immediate availability. The experiments did not test these choices as solutions, so they cannot tell us which is stronger. But “silent” and “out of reach” are not identical.

### A habit with social pressure

Outside the lab, the phone also carries a social expectation: respond soon. A 2018 Pew survey found that 72% of U.S. teens often or sometimes checked messages or notifications soon after waking. About four in ten felt anxious, upset, or lonely without a phone nearby. A majority felt they needed to reply quickly. Most did not call their use an addiction; the report framed checking as a hard-to-resist habit.

## In one line

A phone does not need to be opened to compete for attention. In these studies and this survey, the hard-to-ignore pull appeared as an alert, a nearby device, and an expectation to answer.
