# 📦 The Strange Pull of the Refresh Button

Why do we open a delivery page again and again?

The answer may begin with the waiting, not the package.

When the result is unknown, checking for new information can feel like taking a small action in a situation we cannot control.

In 2025, Howell and Sweeny reported a long-term study in *Emotion*. They followed three groups for several weeks or months: people waiting for the result of the 2020 U.S. presidential election, people waiting for California bar exam results, and people waiting for academic job decisions.

They found a clear pattern. As people’s worry grew, they checked the news and searched for updates more often.

This does not prove that worry directly caused the checking. But it shows that worry and information-seeking moved together. The finding is surprising because people often think anxiety makes us avoid information. In these waiting periods, people searched more when their worry was at its highest.

Checking may create a weak and temporary feeling of control. It does not change the final result. Still, it gives the person something to do. When that feeling disappears, another check may seem necessary.

The studies did not test delivery tracking pages directly. But they offer a possible explanation for why a simple refresh button can become so hard to ignore.

### Uncertainty makes checking more tempting

A 2021 study in *Scientific Reports* looked at this question in a lab. Twenty-eight healthy people, aged 19 to 29, watched moving dots and judged their direction. Before answering, they could replay the dots as many times as they wanted.

When the movement was harder to understand, they checked more often. The reported result was strong: *b* was 0.90, with *p* below 2 × 10⁻¹⁶. This supports a link between uncertainty and checking, but it does not prove the same direct effect on delivery tracking.

### Noticing doubt may keep the loop going

The same study found that people who could consciously notice their own uncertainty were more likely to change their checking as the task became harder. A measure of uncertainty outside conscious awareness did not predict the pattern.

In simple terms, the thought “I am not sure” may bring the hand back to the button. The study was small, so this is a clue about the pattern, not a diagnosis of any person.

## In one line…

When the outcome is out of our hands, refreshing can feel like taking control. But that brief feeling may also keep the waiting—and the checking—alive.
