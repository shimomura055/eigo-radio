# The Bench Question: Who Gets to Pause?

A public bench looks like a small piece of street furniture. But for an older person, it can help turn a difficult journey into a possible one.

It offers a place to rest, enjoy a park or waterfront, and stay close to neighborhood life.

A 2016 qualitative study in Vancouver reported these experiences among adults aged 60 and older. Benches were not only seats. They could also be informal meeting places, where casual contact happened without a plan.

A 2023 review examined 28 articles published from 2020 to 2023. It looked at how public parks can support active aging and well-being. Older park users preferred more benches and associated them with relaxation. Well-maintained parks with seating, pleasant paths, toilets, and cafés were also reported to encourage older visitors.

But in some public spaces, benches are changed or removed as part of “hostile,” or defensive, architecture. Some designs are intended to discourage sleeping or lying down. A bench can then become a way to shape who can stay, and for how long.

That is the central tension. Public space must be usable, but small design choices can decide how it is used.

Evidence about these designs is limited. A summary said that, as of March 2020, no wide-scale empirical study had measured their impact on the well-being of homeless people or other targeted groups.

### A seat can work like social infrastructure

Ottoni and colleagues’ 2016 qualitative study looked at three nearby Vancouver neighborhoods. It used seated and walk-along interviews at two time points, with 28 participants in 2012 and 22 in 2014. Its phrase “benches become like porches” captures a wider idea: one place can support both a pause and casual social contact.

### Small design choices need careful checking

Hostile architecture can include sloped benches, center armrests, or outright removal. At Malmö’s Triangeln station, a tilted bench led to a complaint reported on March 4, 2011; officials said it had arrived too steeply and was later corrected. In New York, a 2021 post said subway benches were being removed to stop homeless people sleeping, but it was deleted and the MTA called it an error.

## In one line…

A bench can be a place to rest, a “porch” for neighbors, or a barrier. Its design raises a larger question: will public space support connection and access, or control who can stay?
