# One Click In, a Maze Out: Why Subscriptions Can Be Hard to Cancel

Starting a subscription can feel like walking through an open door.

A free trial appears. The price is clear. A few clicks later, the service is running.

Then you try to leave.

The door is no longer easy to find. There may be more pages, confusing choices, requests to stay, or even a phone call. No single step looks huge. Together, however, they can make the exit feel heavy.

This kind of friction has a name: **sludge**.

In choice design, sludge means barriers that make it harder for people to do what they want or what helps them. For a customer who no longer wants a subscription, a difficult exit can fit this idea. Researchers also study these screens as **dark patterns**.

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission tried to change this structure.

On October 16, 2024, it finalized its **Click-to-Cancel** rule. It covered auto-renewing subscriptions, free trials that become paid plans, and continuing product deliveries.

Its main message was simple:

**Canceling should be about as easy as signing up.**

If someone joined online, there should be a simple online way to leave. The rule also called for clear key terms, separate consent, no false statements about important facts, and consent records kept for at least three years.

The plan followed a March 2023 proposal and more than 16,000 public comments.

But the legal story changed.

On July 8, 2025, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals canceled the full 2024 rule because of problems with the rule-making process. This followed a challenge from industry groups.

After that decision, FTC guidance returned to the narrower Negative Option Rule from the 1973 era. The broad Click-to-Cancel rule is therefore no longer in place.

### The real cost is the whole journey

The useful question is not only, “Can I cancel?” It is also, “How much work does canceling demand?” Richard H. Thaler’s 2018 *Science* article, “Nudge, not sludge,” gives this burden a useful name. The idea looks at the full exit journey, not only the final button.

### Researchers mapped the exit maze

A 2025 conference study used a review and a user experiment. It identified 58 dark-pattern types, then created a system of 44 patterns in 10 groups. It used six services as cases: The Economist, Bloomberg, Scribd, Adobe Creative Cloud, Amazon Prime, and Audible.

## In one line…

The entrance to a subscription may be wide and bright, while the exit is hidden behind friction—and the U.S. attempt to make both doors equally easy did not survive.
