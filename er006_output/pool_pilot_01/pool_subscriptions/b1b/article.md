# One Click In, Many Steps Out: The Hidden Friction of Subscriptions

Starting a subscription often feels like an open door. One click, and you are inside.

Leaving can feel very different. The path may include extra screens, a phone call, or messages asking you to stay.

This gap has a name: **sludge**.

Richard H. Thaler discussed the idea in his 2018 *Science* article, “Nudge, not sludge.” A nudge helps people make a useful choice. Sludge adds friction when people try to do what they already want to do. For someone who no longer wants a subscription, that friction may stand between “cancel” and “cancelled.”

A 2025 paper for the European Association of Cognitive Ergonomics annual conference examined dark patterns in subscription cancellation. The study used a review and a user experiment. It also analyzed six services as case examples: The Economist, Bloomberg, Scribd, Adobe Creative Cloud, Amazon Prime, and Audible.

The issue also reached US regulators.

On October 16, 2024, the Federal Trade Commission finalized its “Click-to-Cancel” rule. It covered automatic renewals, free trials that become paid plans, and continuing product deliveries.

Its central idea was simple:

**Leaving should be about as easy as joining.**

Cancellation should also be available through the same kind of channel used to sign up. The FTC had received more than 16,000 public comments since its March 2023 proposal.

But the story did not end there.

On July 8, 2025, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals vacated the whole rule for procedural reasons. After that decision, FTC guidance said the narrower Negative Option Rule from the 1973 era returned. The broad click-to-cancel standard was finalized—but it is not now in force.

### Friction has many forms

The 2025 study first identified 58 dark patterns in academic and grey literature. It then standardized them into 44 patterns across 10 categories. These included complexity, misleading interfaces, forced continuation, retention efforts, and mental overload. Cancellation trouble is not just one annoying button.

### A cancel button is only part of the contract

The FTC rule went beyond the final click. It also called for clear key terms before billing, separate and sufficient consent, no false statements about important facts, and consent records kept for at least three years. An easy exit was part of a wider plan for informed agreement.

## In one line…

A subscription can begin with a tap, yet end in a maze. The debate over sludge asks a plain question: if joining is easy, why should leaving be hard?
