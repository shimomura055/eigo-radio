# When “Recommended” Means More: The Quiet Pressure of Tip Screens

## Main Story

At a café or restaurant, the bill may end with a question: how much would you like to tip? A screen often shows a few suggested percentages and asks you to pick one.

The word “recommended” sounds helpful. But it can also become the first number in your mind. Before you decide what the service was worth, the screen may have already set a starting point.

A 2014 study looked closely at this idea using more than 13 million credit card transactions in New York City taxis. Around a $15 fare, the recommended tip menu changed suddenly. The researchers used this change in a regression discontinuity design.

Passengers who happened to see a menu with higher default tip rates left more money. The menu shown right before payment shaped the final choice.

But there was also a warning. When the recommended tip was set too high, some customers did not simply choose a smaller custom amount. They chose zero instead.

That makes the tip screen a risky tool. A higher default may increase a tip from some customers. For others, it may turn the whole decision into a refusal.

The taxi study shows how suggested numbers can shape a single choice. Similar screens are now common in restaurants and other services, where a different question has emerged: how much social pressure do these screens add?

### The choice is no longer private

One policy expert says U.S. restaurant tipping rose from about 10 to 15 percent in the early 1970s to about 20 percent by the late 1990s, and that today's digital screens can suggest tips as high as 35 percent. He describes the social pressure of choosing in front of a worker as “guilt tipping”: with a cash tip jar, the choice felt more private, while a digital screen can make it public.

### Customers are starting to push back

A survey released in 2026 found that 78 percent of respondents said tipping had become ridiculous, while 44 percent said they were tipping less than a year earlier. Fifty-nine percent still felt they had to tip when prompted by a digital screen, down from 66 percent earlier. Thirty-six percent chose a custom amount instead of a preset option.

The taxi study shows a screen's first number can shape a tip, and other evidence points to added social pressure—but when a screen asks for too much, some customers choose zero instead.
