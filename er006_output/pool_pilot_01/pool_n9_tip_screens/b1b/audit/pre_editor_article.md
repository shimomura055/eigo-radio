# When “Recommended” Means More: The Quiet Pressure of Tip Screens

## Main Story

At a café or restaurant, the bill may end with a small test. A screen shows several suggested tip rates and asks you to choose one.

The word “recommended” sounds helpful. But it can also become the first number in your mind. Before you decide what the service was worth, the screen has already set a starting point.

Researchers Kareem Haggag and Giovanni Paci studied this effect in New York City taxis. Their 2014 study examined more than 13 million credit card transactions. Around a $15 fare, the recommended tip menu changed suddenly. The researchers used this change in a regression discontinuity design.

The result showed a strong effect. Passengers who happened to see a menu with higher default tip rates left more money. In other words, the first option on the screen influenced the final choice.

But there was also a warning. When the recommended tip was set too high, some customers did not simply choose a smaller custom amount. They chose zero instead.

That makes the tip screen a risky tool. A higher default may increase a tip from some customers. For others, it may turn the whole decision into a refusal.

The issue has now moved far beyond taxis. Digital payment screens are common in restaurants and other services, and some screens show tips as high as 35 percent. The question is no longer only how much people want to give. It is also how much the screen is asking them to consider.

### The choice is no longer private

Michael Lahr of Rutgers University’s New Jersey State Policy Lab says U.S. restaurant tipping rose from about 10 to 15 percent in the early 1970s to about 20 percent by the late 1990s. He describes the social pressure of choosing in front of a worker as “guilt tipping.” A cash tip jar felt more private; a digital screen can make the decision public.

### Customers are starting to push back

A Popmenu survey released in April 2026 found that 78 percent of respondents said tipping had become ridiculous, while 44 percent said they were tipping less than a year earlier. Fifty-nine percent still felt they had to tip when prompted by a digital screen, down from 66 percent in September 2025. Thirty-six percent chose a custom amount instead of a preset option.

## In one line…

The tip screen sets the opening number and adds social pressure—but when it asks for too much, customers may choose the custom button, or no tip at all.
