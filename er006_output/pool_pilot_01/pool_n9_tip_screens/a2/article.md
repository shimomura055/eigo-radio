# Why Tip Screens Keep Asking for More

At restaurants, cafés, and in taxis, payment screens may ask how much you would like to tip.

One large study looked specifically at New York City taxis, where a screen shows several suggested rates before payment.

In 2014, researchers published this peer-reviewed study in the *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*. They studied more than 13 million credit card payments in New York City taxis.

Before payment, the screen showed a small menu of suggested tip rates. When the fare was just below or just above 15 dollars, the menu changed suddenly.

This gave the researchers a useful comparison. They used a method called regression discontinuity. In simple terms, they compared rides that were almost the same but showed different tip menus.

The result was clear: passengers who happened to see higher suggested rates left more money. In this study, the first number on the screen became a starting point for their decision.

But a high suggestion does not always bring a higher tip.

When the suggested rate feels too high, some customers do not choose a smaller custom amount. Instead, they leave no tip on the credit card.

So the screen has two possible effects. It can guide some people toward a larger tip. But it can also make other people choose zero.

### The pressure is social, not only mathematical

A 2022 policy report says restaurant tipping in the United States rose from about 10 to 15 percent in the early 1970s to about 20 percent in the late 1990s, and that digital screens can show tips as high as 35 percent. With a cash tip jar, the choice was more private; on a screen, the worker may be watching. The report calls this feeling “guilt tipping.”

### Customers are starting to push back

A survey released in 2026 found that 78 percent of respondents said tipping had become ridiculous. Forty-four percent said they had reduced their tips from the year before. Among people who saw a digital tip screen, the share who felt they had to leave a tip fell from 66 percent in September 2025 to 59 percent in 2026. Also, 36 percent said they chose a custom amount instead of a preset choice.

## In one line…

In the taxi study, a higher first number increased tips, but an excessively high suggestion could also lead to no tip at all. The “suggested” tip is no longer just a choice—it is part of the checkout experience.
