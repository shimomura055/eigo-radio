# A Place to Pause—or a Signal to Move On?

A public bench is easy to ignore—until you need one.

For older adults, a place to sit can shape how they experience a neighborhood. A study in three nearby Vancouver neighborhoods interviewed adults aged 60 and older. They said benches gave them places to rest during walks. Benches also helped them enjoy green areas and waterfront spaces.

But the role of a bench did not end there.

The study described how benches can “become like porches.” They are neither fully private nor fully social. They create a simple place where neighbors can stop, talk, or just spend time near one another.

A 2023 review added a wider view. It examined 28 articles published from 2020 to 2023. The review reported that older park users preferred more benches, linked to their desire to relax. Other literature in the review said that well-kept parks with seating, pleasant paths, toilets, and cafés encouraged older visitors.

At the same time, some public benches have been changed to limit how people use them.

Sloped seats and center armrests can prevent people from lying across a bench. In other cases, benches are removed completely. These choices are often discussed as “hostile architecture” or “defensive architecture.”

This creates a difficult question:

**Is a bench only a seat—or is it part of someone’s ability to move through the city?**

A design that blocks one activity may also remove a resting place for someone else. Yet the evidence about the wider harm remains limited. As of March 2020, one article reported that no wide-scale empirical study had measured the effects of hostile architecture on homeless people or other targeted groups.

### Lived experience, not a final verdict

The Vancouver researchers used seated interviews and walk-along interviews at two times. There were 28 participants in 2012 and 22 in 2014. Their accounts show how people experienced their neighborhoods. They do not prove that adding benches will create the same results in every city.

### Famous cases need careful checking

Malmö’s Triangeln station opened in December 2010, and a formal complaint about its tilted bench was reported on March 4, 2011. Yet officials said it was steeper than ordered and later corrected. In 2021, an NYC Transit post linked bench removals to homeless people sleeping. It was deleted, and the MTA called it an error—not a confirmed policy.

## In one line…

A bench may be a small piece of city furniture, but it can serve as a rest stop, a neighborhood porch, or a signal that some people should move on. How cities design the pause helps shape who can remain in public space.
