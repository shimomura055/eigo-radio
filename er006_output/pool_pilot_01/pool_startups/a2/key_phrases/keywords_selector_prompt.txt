# The User Rush: Why Some Startups Choose Speed Before Profit

At first, the plan sounds strange: lose money now, grow fast, and think about profit later.

But for some startups, user growth can change the product itself. This happens when a business has a **network effect**.

Think of a service that helps people connect. When more people join, the service can become more useful for each person. Katz and Shapiro explained this idea as “network externalities” in their 1985 work.

Then a powerful circle can begin.

More users create more value. More value attracts more users.

An early lead can build on itself and become a lasting advantage. A late start can also become a bigger problem over time. In such a market, even the product with the best technology may not win.

This is why speed can be a rational choice. Pete Flint of NFX wrote in 2020 that a company with a true network effect may first run at a loss. It tries to reach a large enough network before rivals do. After that, it may be able to move into profit.

But this logic has a clear limit.

Growth is not magic. If each new customer does not make the product stronger, fast spending may only make a weak business larger. Easy money can also hide problems and reduce the pressure to become efficient.

NFX therefore suggests changing the goal as a startup grows. Once people clearly want the product, a Seed or Series A company may aim to grow three to five times in one year. At Series B or C, growth should come with efficient ways to get customers. Later, the company should move toward profit.

In 2020, Kuratko, Holt, and Neubert also examined blitzscaling in the peer-reviewed journal *Business Horizons*. Blitzscaling means choosing very fast growth over efficiency. The real question is not simply, “Can we grow fast?” It is, “Does speed create a lasting advantage?”

### Speed needs a real engine

NFX uses Groupon as a warning. Its fast expansion hid a serious problem: merchants and sellers were leaving. NFX also discusses some mattress, shoe, and meal-kit companies. They chased blitzscaling without network effects or another strong defense. The article links these cases to down rounds and investor losses.

### Growth must pass a health check

Investors also watch efficiency. NFX gives the Rule of 40: revenue growth plus profit margin should total more than 40%. It also says customer costs should come back within three to six months. Private-equity acquisitions rose from 4% of relevant deals in 2008 to 11% in 2019. NFX presents this as part of a wider return to efficiency.

## In one line…

Growth before profit makes sense when every new user strengthens the business. Without that engine, speed can turn hidden weakness into a larger and more expensive problem.
