# Growth Now, Profit Later: When the Startup Race Makes Sense

For some startups, profit is not the first finish line.

They spend heavily to gain users, enter markets, and grow faster than competitors. This can look dangerous. But when a company has a real network effect, speed may have a clear economic purpose.

Research by Michael Katz and Carl Shapiro in 1985 explained the basic idea. A product may become more valuable to each user as more people use it.

That can start a powerful cycle.

More users create more value. More value attracts more users. An early lead may then build into a lasting advantage. In these markets, even the product with the best technology does not always win. Starting late can become a growing problem.

Pete Flint of NFX argued in 2020 that companies with genuine network effects may have good reasons to choose growth before profit. Some markets can become “winner-take-most” races. After reaching critical mass, a company may also have a chance to move from losses to profit.

But there is a trap hidden inside the word “growth.”

A rising customer count does not prove that a strong network is forming. NFX presents Groupon as a warning. Its fast expansion hid the loss of merchants and sellers that could not be sustained.

The article also points to some direct-to-consumer companies in mattresses, shoes, and meal kits. They used blitzscaling-style growth without strong network effects or other clear defenses. Several later faced lower-value funding rounds and investor losses.

In other words, speed can build a fortress—or simply make a weak business burn money faster.

### Speed needs a dashboard

NFX suggests annual growth of three to five times after product-market fit at the Seed or Series A stage. Later, growth should work together with efficient customer acquisition. Useful checks include the Rule of 40 and recovering customer acquisition costs within about three to six months.

### The market is asking harder questions

NFX notes that private equity acquisitions rose from 4% of relevant transactions in 2008 to 11% in 2019. This does not prove a complete return to profit, but it signals greater interest in efficiency. A 2020 *Business Horizons* paper by Kuratko, Holt, and Neubert also critically examined blitzscaling.

## In one line…

“Growth before profit makes sense when every new user strengthens the business. Without that engine, speed may only help the problems arrive sooner.”
