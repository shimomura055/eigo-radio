# Smartphones Handed In in the Morning: Different Voices

I support the rule that the school takes students’ phones in the morning and gives them back when school lets out. I get home late from the worksite, and it’s exhausting to argue with my daughter, who’s in high school, about her phone every night. If I could say, “The school has the same rule,” I wouldn’t have to be the only bad guy. We agreed she would put it in the charging basket in the living room, but she didn’t stick to it. The morning after I took it away, it was hard to talk to her over breakfast. She also said, “If I’m the only one who replies late, I’ll be left out.” I couldn’t argue back. I want her to stick to what we decide. I don’t want to yell like my dad did. Still, even if the school keeps her phone during the day, things at home won’t change at night. Maybe I just want to ask the school to make things easier for me. I’m a site supervisor who can’t even make a plan at home.

I see the faces of the children who come to the nurse’s office first thing every morning. “I’m sleepy,” they say. “My head hurts,” they say. The other day, a phone screen was glowing beside the pillow of a child resting on a bed. When I hear they were awake until dawn, I wonder what I should say. I also worry about the child who stopped coming to the nurse’s office the day after their phone was taken away at school. Does not seeing them mean they aren’t having a hard time? If the school decides to collect all the phones in the morning and keep them until the end of the day, will we end up thinking that takes care of it? That is what scares me. I don’t take phones away in the nurse’s office. I want to leave a place where students can talk about what happened at night. After we take their phones, who will listen to that child?

I’m against collecting phones in the morning and keeping them until school is over. I work at a warehouse during the day and go to evening high school at night, so I can’t promise to be out of touch the whole time I’m at school. During breaks, I get messages about changes to my shift. If I reply late, the work goes to someone else. Once, calls kept coming from work during class, but I couldn’t answer. Later, they told me it was a problem that they couldn’t reach me. My shifts almost got cut, too. I want to graduate from here. I need to give money to my family, too. Now that I’ve started to understand my classes, I don’t want to choose between work and school. I promise to keep my phone deep in my bag and not touch it during class. But before deciding to collect everyone’s phones, please hear about how I work, too.

All three want to avoid having their lives or going to school become harder because of their phones. The times and places of trouble are different: the basket in the living room at night, the pillow by the bed in the nurse’s office in the morning, and shift messages during breaks. What they want to protect is conversation between parent and child, a place to talk about what’s hard, and daily life while going to school and working. A rule about handing in phones may help parents and children, but it might also cut off conversations and contact. Neither the father nor the teacher thinks that taking phones during the day alone will make problems at night go away. When and where today would it be hard for you to give up your phone?

## In one line
Three people explain how collecting students’ phones at school could help some, but make life harder for others. The rule may ease family conflict, yet it could also cut off conversations and contact without solving problems at home.
