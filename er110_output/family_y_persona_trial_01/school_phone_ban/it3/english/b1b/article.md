# No Phones Even at Break Time: Different Voices

I'm for a rule at my daughter's high school that keeps phones off during breaks and lunch, too. Banning them only during class isn't enough. I get home late from the job site, and fighting about phones every night is exhausting. If the school has the same rule, then I'm not the only bad guy. My daughter didn't keep our agreement to put her phone in the living-room charging basket. The morning after I took it away, it was hard to talk to her at breakfast. She also said, “If I'm the only one who replies late, I'll be left out.” Her words left me speechless. Still, a promise is a promise, and I want it kept. Yelling like my father is out of the question, too. But even if she can't use her phone at school during the day, nothing changes at home at night. Maybe I just want to ask the school for help so things are easier for me. I'm a site supervisor who can't even get things organized at home.

First thing every morning, I see the faces of the kids who come to the nurse's office. “I'm sleepy,” they say. “My head hurts,” they say. The other day, a phone screen was glowing beside the pillow of a student resting on the bed. When I hear they were up until dawn, I wonder what I should say. I also keep thinking about a student who stopped coming the day after their phone was taken away at school. If they don't come, does that mean they aren't struggling? A teacher asked me, “If we ban phones, will fewer kids come to the nurse's office?” I couldn't answer. If the school makes phones off-limits even during breaks, won't it feel it has dealt with the problem? That really scares me. I don't take phones away in the nurse's office. I want to keep a place where students can talk about what happened at night. After phones are off-limits, who will listen to that child?

I'm against a rule that makes phones off-limits even during breaks. I work at a warehouse during the day and go to evening high school at night. So I can't promise to cut off contact the whole time I'm at school. During breaks between classes, I get messages about changes to my shifts. If I reply late, the work goes to someone else. Once, my workplace kept calling during class. I couldn't answer, and later they told me it was a problem that they couldn't reach me. I almost had my shifts cut, too. I want to graduate from here. I also need money to give to my family. Now that I can follow my classes, I don't want to choose between work and school. I promise to keep my phone at the bottom of my bag and not touch it during class. But before you decide that phones are off-limits even during breaks, I want you to hear how I work, too.

At the breakfast table, beside the pillow in the nurse's office, and in work messages during breaks, people want to stay able to talk to someone. Making phone-free time longer may mean fewer arguments over the basket at night. But a student whose phone is taken away may stop opening up beside that pillow. He can follow the rule during class, but if the shift messages he gets during breaks stop, his workplace may lose trust in him. The father asking the school for help also doesn't want to go through the silence after taking the phone away again. They may disagree about how long phones can be used, but they share a wish to keep someone to talk to. What message do you want to answer right away? When does it arrive, and who is it from?

## In one line
A break-time phone ban may ease fights at home, but it could also stop students from opening up or answering work messages. The parent, nurse, and student disagree about when phones should be allowed, but all want someone they can still talk to.
