# Schools Where Students Can’t Use Phones Even During Breaks: Different Voices

During class, I put my phone away in my bag, as I should. At lunch, the desks around me are pushed together. I open my lunchbox and send a photo of my model to my friend from middle school. My friend now goes to a different high school. I show the part where I messed up the paint. They send back a photo of paint outside the lines. I laugh a little and pick up my chopsticks again. It is not a big thing, but I need that time. Sometimes the break ends while I am still thinking about how to answer messages from people in my class. I could turn off message alerts, but I fear I will miss something. So I check them. It would be a problem if we could not use phones at school, even during lunch. I keep the sound off, and I do not take pictures of people around me. I should not have to choose between talking to classmates and staying in touch with a friend outside school.

It is not that I mind messages from work. My bag has a folded apron from the deli section and my textbooks. There are too few workers at the counter. So I am glad when they ask me to work. Just the other day, I got a message in the hall outside my classroom. It asked if I could start earlier the next day. I checked the bus times and replied. The break ended before I could open the math notebook I had borrowed. I would feel a little relieved if the rule let me bring my phone but not use it at school, even during breaks. But I worry they might give me fewer shifts if I do not reply. I want my workplace to understand that I cannot reply while I am at school. Then, I want to think about the rest of the equation, not tomorrow’s food prep.

When I get a message that someone cannot come in, I leave the supermarket floor and go back to the office. There, I change the next morning’s schedule in pencil. I message the student who stocks shelves and goes to evening school: “You don’t need to check during class. Just check during break.” Just the other day, I traced the empty spaces with my finger and waited for an answer. They said they could come in, so I changed who would get the store ready to open. Honestly, it would be a problem if they could not use phones during breaks, even if they could bring them. I’ll find a way to handle messages before they come to school. But it is hard for me to come in early every time someone cannot come to work. I say there is no rush, while counting on their reply. I do not interrupt classes, so I want them to be able to check messages during breaks. The school has its reasons, I suppose.

All three do not want to lose the time they spend moving chopsticks over lunch, opening a math notebook, and filling in a work schedule. At lunchtime in the classroom, a photo of a model gets the chopsticks moving. A message in the hallway stops work on an equation. In the store’s sales area that evening, a reply fills in the work schedule. They disagree about whether students should check messages during breaks. Both students also fear leaving messages unanswered. The person at the store says there is no rush, but still relies on that reply to fill in a blank in the next morning’s schedule with a pencil. When you open your lunch at noon, what kind of message would make you put down your chopsticks and reply?
