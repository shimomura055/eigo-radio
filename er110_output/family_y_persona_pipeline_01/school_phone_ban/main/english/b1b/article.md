# Schools Where Students Can’t Use Phones Even During Breaks: Different Voices

During class, I put my phone away in my bag like I’m supposed to. At lunch, while desks around me are pushed together, I open my lunchbox and send a photo of my model to my friend from middle school, who goes to another high school. I send a picture of the part where I messed up the paint, and they send back a photo of paint that ran outside the lines. I laugh a little and start moving my chopsticks again. It’s not a big deal, but I need that time. Sometimes the break ends while I’m still thinking about how to reply to messages from my class. I could just turn off the notifications, but I’m afraid I’ll miss something, so I check them. But it would be a problem if we couldn’t use our phones at school even during lunch break. I keep the sound off, and I don’t take pictures of people around me. I don’t think I should have to choose between talking to someone in class and staying in touch with a friend outside school.

It’s not that I mind messages from work. My bag has a folded apron from the deli section and my textbooks in it. They’re short-staffed at the counter, so I’m glad when they ask me to work. Just the other day, I got a message in the hall outside my classroom asking if I could start earlier the next day. I checked the bus times and replied, and the break ended before I could open the math notebook I’d borrowed. If the rule were that I could bring my phone but not use it at school, even during breaks, I’d feel a little relieved. But I worry they might give me fewer shifts if I don’t reply. I want my workplace to understand that I can’t reply while I’m at school. During that time, I want to think about the rest of the equation, not tomorrow’s food prep.

When I get a message that someone can’t come in, I go from the supermarket floor back to the office and change the next morning’s schedule in pencil. I message the student who stocks shelves and is heading to evening school: “You don’t need to check during class. Just check during break.” Just the other day, I traced the empty spaces with my finger and waited for a reply. They said they could come in, so I changed who was assigned to get the store ready to open. Honestly, it would be a problem if they couldn’t use their phones during breaks, even if they were allowed to bring them. I’ll work out a way to handle messages before they come to school. But it’s hard to cover a sudden absence by coming in early myself every time. I say there’s no rush, while counting on their reply. I don’t interrupt classes, so I want them to be able to check messages during breaks. The school has its reasons, I suppose.

All three don’t want to lose the time they spend moving their chopsticks over their lunch, opening a math notebook, and filling in a work schedule. At lunchtime in the classroom, a model photo gets the chopsticks moving; a message in the hallway stops the work on an equation; and in the store’s sales area in the evening, a reply fills in the work schedule. They disagree about whether messages during breaks should be allowed, but both students also fear leaving notifications unanswered. The person at the store says there’s no rush, but still relies on that reply to fill in the blank in the next morning’s schedule with a pencil. When you open your lunch at noon, what kind of message would make you put down your chopsticks and reply?
