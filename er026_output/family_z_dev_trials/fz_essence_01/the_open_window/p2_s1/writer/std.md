# The Open Window

Framton sat with an introduction letter on his knee. He had come to the country to calm his nerves. Now he wondered how long this visit should last. Meeting strangers did not seem very helpful.

“My aunt will join us soon,” said fifteen-year-old Vera. “How much do you know about her?”

“I know almost nothing about her.”

“Then you probably haven’t heard her sad story. It happened three years ago, on this very day.”

Vera looked across the room towards the large, open window.

“Her husband and two young brothers left through that window to go shooting. That summer’s rain had made the ground dangerous. They sank into deep mud, and nobody found their bodies.”

Framton moved in his chair as he listened to her.

“My aunt still believes they will come back home. She leaves that window open until dark, like that evening. A white coat lay across her husband’s shoulders. Their brown spaniel went with them, too. The youngest brother sang, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always sang that song to annoy her.”

Vera continued her story, but now her voice was quieter.

“On quiet evenings, I sometimes feel they might—”

Then her aunt came in, and Vera stopped speaking.

“Has Vera looked after you?” her aunt asked happily. “I hope the open window doesn’t bother you. My husband and brothers will soon be back. They’ll get mud all over the carpets.”

With no answer to that, Framton began talking about his health. His doctors wanted him to rest completely and avoid all excitement.

She listened, but kept looking out across the grass.

“At last, they’re back!” she said with pleasure.

Framton looked at Vera and saw her staring outside in horror.

He turned in his chair to see what was outside.

In the fading daylight, three men with guns crossed the lawn. A white coat lay across one man’s shoulders. A brown spaniel walked along behind them.

A young man’s voice rang out: “Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton grabbed his hat and stick and rushed out. His hostess had no time to ask him why. He was already hurrying down the drive.

The men entered through the window, muddy and wanting supper.

“Why did your visitor leave so quickly?” her husband asked.

“He seemed to have seen a ghost,” his wife replied.

“That dog scared him,” Vera said in a calm voice. “He told me he was terribly afraid of dogs. Some dogs chased him into a graveyard in India. He hid in an open grave all night as they growled above him.”

Vera was very good at inventing believable stories without any preparation.
