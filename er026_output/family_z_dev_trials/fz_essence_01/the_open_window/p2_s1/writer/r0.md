# The Open Window

Framton sat with his letter of introduction on his knee. He had come to the country to rest his nerves, and meeting strangers was not a promising beginning.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Almost nothing,” Framton admitted.

“Then you won’t know about her tragedy. It happened three years ago today.”

She looked towards the long French window, which stood open to the lawn.

“Her husband and her two young brothers went out through that window to shoot. They never came back. There was a terrible wet summer, and they sank into a bog. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton shifted in his chair.

“My aunt still thinks they’ll return,” Vera continued. “That’s why the window stays open until dark. Her husband carried his white waterproof coat over his shoulders. Their little brown spaniel went too. And her youngest brother sang, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always sang it to annoy her.”

Her voice broke.

“Sometimes, on quiet evenings, I almost feel they might—”

She stopped as her aunt entered.

“I hope Vera has been keeping you company,” her aunt said brightly. “You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be home soon. They’ll bring half the countryside’s mud in with them.”

Framton tried to find a suitable answer.

Instead, he began explaining what his doctors had forbidden: excitement, hard exercise, anything that might upset him. His hostess listened with only part of her attention. Her eyes kept returning to the lawn.

Then her face brightened.

“Here they are at last!”

Framton turned towards Vera. She was staring through the window, her eyes wide.

He looked round.

In the fading light, three figures were crossing the grass. Each carried a gun. One had a white coat hanging over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel followed at their heels.

They approached without speaking.

Then a young voice called, “I said, Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton seized his hat and stick. He rushed through the hall and out into the road, leaving the front door swinging behind him.

The men came in through the window, muddy and hungry.

“What sent your visitor running?” asked the aunt’s husband.

“One would think he’d seen a ghost,” said his wife.

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. In India, a pack once chased him into a graveyard. He spent the night in an open grave, with the dogs growling above him. Anyone would be nervous after that.”

Romance at short notice was her speciality.
