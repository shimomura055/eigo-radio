# The Open Window

Framton sat with his letter of introduction on his knee, wondering how long a polite visit ought to last. He had come to the country to rest his nerves. Calling on strangers seemed an uncertain way of doing it.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Very little.”

“Then you won’t have heard about her tragedy. It happened three years ago today.”

She looked towards the open French window.

“Her husband and her two young brothers went out through there to shoot. After all the rain that summer, the ground was dangerous. They sank into a bog. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton shifted in his chair.

“My aunt still thinks they’ll come back. She keeps the window open until dark, just as she did that evening. Her husband had his white coat over his shoulders. Their brown spaniel went too. And the youngest brother was singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always sang it to annoy her.”

Her voice became quieter.

“Sometimes, when the evening is still, I almost expect—”

She stopped as her aunt entered.

“I hope Vera has been looking after you,” her aunt said cheerfully. “You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be home soon. They’ll make a terrible mess of the carpets.”

Framton searched for a suitable reply and found none. Instead, he explained that his doctors had ordered complete rest. He must avoid excitement of every kind.

His hostess listened, though her attention seemed chiefly fixed on the lawn.

“Here they are at last!” she said.

Framton glanced at Vera. She was staring through the window, her face full of horror.

He turned in his chair.

Three figures were crossing the lawn in the fading light, carrying guns. One had a white coat over his shoulders. A brown spaniel followed them.

Then a young voice called, “Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton seized his hat and stick. Before his hostess could ask what was wrong, he was out of the room and hurrying down the drive.

The men came through the window, muddy and ready for supper.

“What made your visitor rush off?” asked the husband.

“He looked as though he’d seen a ghost,” his wife said.

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. In India, a pack chased him into a graveyard. He spent the night hiding in an open grave while they growled above him.”

Inventing convincing stories at a moment’s notice was Vera’s speciality.
