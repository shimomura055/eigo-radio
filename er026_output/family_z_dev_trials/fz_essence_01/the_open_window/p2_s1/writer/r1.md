# The Open Window

Framton sat with his letter of introduction on his knee, wondering how soon he could politely leave. He had come to the country to rest his nerves. So far, meeting strangers had not helped.

“My aunt will be down shortly,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Very little.”

“Then you won’t know about her tragedy. It happened three years ago today.”

She looked towards the French window, open to the lawn.

“Her husband and her two young brothers went out through there to shoot. It had been a terribly wet summer. They sank into a bog, all three of them. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton moved a little in his chair.

“My aunt still expects them back. That’s why she leaves the window open until dark. Her husband had his white coat over his shoulders, and their brown spaniel went with them. The youngest brother was singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He used to sing it to annoy her.”

Vera’s voice grew unsteady.

“On quiet evenings, I sometimes think they might come walking—”

She stopped. Her aunt had entered.

“I hope Vera has kept you company,” she said. “You don’t mind the window being open? My husband and brothers will be back soon. They’ll bring mud over everything.”

Framton could think of nothing suitable to say. He began instead to explain his treatment. His doctors had forbidden excitement and anything likely to upset him.

His hostess listened, but her eyes kept moving towards the lawn.

“At last!” she said suddenly. “Here they are!”

Framton looked at Vera. She was staring through the window, her eyes wide with horror.

He turned.

Three figures were crossing the grass in the fading light. They carried guns. One had a white coat over his shoulders. Behind them came a brown spaniel.

For a moment there was no sound. Then a young voice called, “Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton grabbed his hat and stick. He was through the hall and out of the house before anyone could speak.

The men came in through the window, muddy and ready for supper.

“Why did your visitor run off?” asked the husband.

“He looked as if he’d seen a ghost,” said his wife.

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said. “He told me he was afraid of dogs. In India, a pack chased him into a graveyard. He hid in an open grave all night while they growled above him.”

Inventing convincing stories at a moment’s notice was Vera’s speciality.
