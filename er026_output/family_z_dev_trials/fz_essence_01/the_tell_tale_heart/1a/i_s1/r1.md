# Listen Carefully

He was not mad; he was certain of that. Illness had sharpened his senses, especially his hearing. He could hear everything in heaven and earth. He asked his listener to judge how calmly he could tell the story.

He loved the old man who shared his home. The old man had never hurt him. He wanted none of his gold. Only the eye troubled him: pale blue, covered by a thin film, like a vulture’s. Whenever it turned towards him, his blood went cold. That eye had to close forever.

For seven nights, at midnight, he opened the old man’s door. Slowly, carefully, he slipped his covered lantern inside and let one thin ray fall across the eye.

Always closed.

He could not act. It was the eye he hated, not the man. Each morning, he greeted his housemate warmly.

On the eighth night, his thumb slipped on the lantern’s fastening.

“Who’s there?”

He froze. For an hour, the old man sat listening in the dark. Then came a low groan of terror.

He opened the lantern a little. A narrow beam fell on the eye.

Wide open.

Now he heard a low, quick beating, like a watch wrapped in cotton. The old man’s heart. Faster. Louder. What if a neighbour heard?

He rushed forward with a yell. The old man shrieked once. He dragged him to the floor and pulled the heavy bed over him. The beating continued, then stopped.

He hid the body beneath three floorboards and replaced them neatly. Nothing showed. He was calm. Surely such care proved he was sane.

At four in the morning, three police officers knocked. A neighbour had heard a shriek.

He welcomed them with a smile. The cry, he explained, had come from his own bad dream. The old man was away in the country. He showed them every room, then brought chairs into the bedroom. Confidently, he sat directly above the hidden body.

The officers chatted pleasantly. He answered easily.

Then his ears began to ring.

He spoke louder, but the sound grew clearer: low and quick, like a watch wrapped in cotton.

He rose and paced. He scraped his chair across the boards. Still the beating grew louder.

The officers went on smiling and talking as if they heard nothing.

But they must hear it. They knew. Those smiles were a game, a cruel game at his expense.

He could bear it no longer.

“Stop pretending!” he screamed. “I killed him! Tear up the planks! Here, beneath my feet! It is the beating of his hideous heart!”
