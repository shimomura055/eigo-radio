# The Quiet House

You call me mad? You mistake sharp senses for madness. My illness has only improved my hearing. I hear everything in heaven and earth. Now listen, and judge how calmly I tell you what happened.

The old man shared my house, and I loved him. He had never wronged me; I wanted none of his money. Only his eye troubled me: pale blue, covered by a cloudy film. A vulture’s eye. Whenever it turned towards me, my blood went cold. That eye had to go.

For seven nights, at midnight, I opened his bedroom door so slowly that you would have admired my patience. Then I let one thin ray from my lantern fall across his face. Each night, the eye was closed. I could do nothing. It was the eye I hated, you understand—not the man.

On the eighth night, I smiled at my own cleverness. Then my thumb slipped against the lantern fastening.

The old man sat up. “Who’s there?”

For an hour, I stood without moving. He stayed awake, listening. Then I heard a small groan of terror. I knew that sound; I had made it myself in the dark.

At last, I opened the lantern a little. The light fell straight upon the eye.

Wide open.

Then I heard his heart: a low, quick beat, like a watch wrapped in cotton. Faster it went, and louder. What if a neighbour heard it?

I threw open the lantern and rushed in. He shrieked once. I dragged him down and pulled the heavy bed over him. Soon the beating stopped. I checked carefully. He was dead.

I hid him beneath three floorboards and fitted them back perfectly. Not a mark remained. Would a madman have worked so neatly?

At four in the morning, three police officers knocked. A neighbour had heard a shriek. I welcomed them warmly. The cry was mine, I explained—a bad dream. The old man was away in the country.

I showed them every room, then brought chairs into his bedroom. I sat directly above the hidden body. You see? I had nothing to fear.

They sat chatting. Soon my ears began to ring. I spoke louder, but beneath my voice came a low, quick beating.

I rose and paced. I scraped my chair across the boards. Still it grew louder. Louder!

The officers went on talking and smiling. Not one seemed to notice. But they heard it. They must! They knew everything, and their smiles were mocking me. How could you expect me to bear that?

“I did it!” I screamed. “Tear up the planks! Here, beneath my chair! It is the beating of his hideous heart!”
