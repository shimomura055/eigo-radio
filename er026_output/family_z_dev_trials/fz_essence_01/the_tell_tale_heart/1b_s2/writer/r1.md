# Listen to Me

Nervous, yes—but not mad. My illness sharpened my senses, especially my hearing. I hear everything in heaven and earth. Listen carefully, and judge how calmly I tell you what happened.

I loved the old man who shared the house with me. He had never harmed me, and I wanted none of his gold. Only his eye troubled me: pale blue, covered by a film. A vulture’s eye. Whenever it turned towards me, my blood froze. That eye had to go.

For seven nights, at midnight, I eased open his door and let a thin ray from my lantern fall on his eye. It was always closed. You understand? I could not act. Each morning, I greeted him kindly. Would a madman have been so careful?

On the eighth night, I smiled at my skill. Then my thumb slipped on the lantern’s tin fastening.

“Who’s there?” he cried, sitting up.

I stood still. For an hour he sat listening in the darkness. Then came a low groan of terror. I knew that sound well.

At last I opened the lantern a little. Its narrow beam fell on the eye—wide open. Fury burned through me.

Then I heard a low, quick beating, like a watch wrapped in cotton. His heart. It grew louder. What if a neighbour heard? I could wait no longer.

I rushed in. He shrieked once. I dragged him down and pulled the heavy bed over him. When the beating stopped, I checked: no movement, no pulse.

I hid his body beneath three floorboards and fitted them neatly back into place. Not a mark remained. You would have admired my work.

At four in the morning, a knock came. Three police officers stood outside; a neighbour had heard a shriek. I welcomed them with a smile. A bad dream, I explained. The cry had been mine. The old man was away in the country.

I showed them every room, then brought chairs into his bedroom. I placed mine directly above the hidden body. You see how little I feared? The officers sat chatting pleasantly, quite satisfied.

But my head began to ache. A ringing filled my ears. No—not ringing. A low, quick beating.

I spoke louder. The beating grew louder still. I paced, scraping my chair across the boards. The officers kept smiling and chatting as if they heard nothing. How could they sit there?

They heard it. They knew! Those smiles were meant to mock me. I could not bear another moment.

“Stop pretending!” I screamed. “I did it! Tear up the planks! Here, beneath my chair! It is the beating of his hideous heart!”
