# Three Wishes

Rain hit the windows as Herbert played chess with Mr. White. Mrs. White left her knitting to answer the door.

Their old friend, a retired sergeant-major, showed them a dry monkey’s paw.

“I brought it from India,” he said. “It gives you three wishes, but I’ve used mine already.”

Mr. White reached out, but his friend covered the paw.

“Leave it alone, because changing your fate brings only sadness.”

He threw it into the fire, but Mr. White quickly pulled it out.

“Then don’t say this is my fault.”

“Two hundred pounds could pay for our house, Father,” Herbert said, laughing.

“I wish to have two hundred pounds,” Mr. White said, smiling and raising the paw.

He cried out and dropped the paw when it twisted in his hand.

“There’s no money down here,” Herbert said, looking under the table.

After more jokes at breakfast, Herbert went off to work.

That afternoon, a man from Herbert’s firm visited. He turned his hat around in his hands.

“Your son died after a machine caught him,” the man said.

Mrs. White took hold of Mr. White’s arm.

“The firm will pay you two hundred pounds for your loss.”

Mr. White fell off his chair.

A week after they buried Herbert, she woke her husband at night.

“You can still make two more wishes with that paw.”

“I won’t do it.”

“Wish for him to come back to life.”

Mr. White stayed where he was.

“You can’t know what will come back,” he told her.

“But he’s our own son.”

Mr. White finally went downstairs to find the paw. He raised it in his hand.

“I wish to have my son alive again.”

His hand opened, and the paw dropped.

They listened in bed as a wooden board made a sound. Then they heard a soft, slow knock.

“It’s Herbert!”

She rushed downstairs. He caught her sleeve, but she got away.

He heard a chair move noisily across the hall.

“I can’t reach the door’s top lock,” she called.

This time, the knocking was louder.

Mr. White went down on his knees and felt around. His hands touched floorboards, then the rug’s edge.

The knocks made the whole door shake.

“Please wait,” he said, as she moved the chair.

He grabbed the paw as she pulled back the last lock. As she reached to open the door, he whispered his third and last wish.

There was no more knocking.

Mrs. White opened the door, but no one stood outside. Cold wind blew inside, and she gave a long, sad cry.

The street lamp across from them lit a quiet, empty road.
