# Three Wishes

Rain beat against the windows. By the fire, Herbert smiled across the chessboard at Mr. White while Mrs. White put down her knitting to answer a knock.

Their visitor, an old friend and retired sergeant-major, took a dried monkey’s paw from his pocket.

“From India,” he said. “It gives a man three wishes. I’ve had mine.”

Mr. White reached towards it, but his friend closed his hand.

“Those who interfere with fate find sorrow. Leave it alone.”

He threw it into the fire. Mr. White quickly pulled it out.

“Don’t blame me, then.”

Herbert laughed. “Two hundred pounds would pay for the house, Father.”

Smiling, Mr. White raised the paw. “I wish for two hundred pounds.”

It twisted in his hand. He cried out and dropped it.

Herbert bent to search beneath the table. “No money here.”

They were still joking at breakfast. Afterwards, Herbert left for work.

That afternoon, a stranger from the firm came to the door. Inside, he stood turning his hat in his hands.

“Your son was caught in the machinery,” he said. “He was killed.”

Mrs. White held her husband’s arm.

“The firm offers two hundred pounds in compensation.”

Mr. White fell from his chair.

A week after the burial, Mrs. White woke him in the dark.

“The paw. There are two wishes left.”

“No.”

“Wish him alive again.”

He did not move.

“You don’t know what might come back,” he said.

“He’s our son.”

At last, Mr. White went downstairs. He found the paw and held it up.

“I wish my son alive again.”

He let it fall.

They returned to bed and lay listening. Somewhere in the house, a board creaked. Then came a slow, quiet knock.

“Herbert!”

Mrs. White ran downstairs. Her husband caught her sleeve, but she pulled away.

He heard a chair scrape across the hall.

“The bolt,” she called. “I can’t reach it.”

Another knock came, louder now.

Mr. White dropped to his knees. His hands swept across the floor, touching bare boards, then the edge of the rug.

The door shook under the blows.

“Wait,” he said, but his wife was moving the chair.

His fingers closed around the paw just as she drew back the last bolt. As she reached to open the door, he whispered his third and last wish.

The knocking stopped.

Mrs. White opened the door. Nobody was there. Cold wind swept into the hall, and from the doorway came her wail of loss.

The street lamp opposite shone on a quiet, deserted road.
