# Three Wishes

Wind drove rain against the windows of the lonely country house. By the fire, Herbert trapped his father’s king.

“You let me win,” he said.

“Then let me win the next one.”

Mrs. White laughed over her knitting. Then someone knocked.

Their old friend, a retired sergeant-major, came in from the rain. Later, he took a dried monkey’s paw from his pocket.

“Brought it from India. It gives a man three wishes.”

“Have you tried?” Mr. White asked.

“I’ve had my three.”

“And?”

The old friend looked into the fire.

“Those who interfere with fate find sorrow. Don’t use it.”

He threw the paw into the flames. Mr. White pulled it out.

“Keep it, then. But don’t blame me.”

“Two hundred pounds would pay for the house,” Herbert said. “Try that, Father.”

Mr. White raised the paw with a smile.

“I wish for two hundred pounds.”

It twisted in his hand. He cried out and dropped it.

Herbert looked under the table.

“Not a penny,” he said.

The next morning, they laughed about it before Herbert left for work.

That afternoon, a stranger in a silk hat stood at their gate. Mr. White watched him lift his hand, then lower it.

At last he came in.

“I’m from the firm,” he said. “Your son was caught in the machinery. He was killed.”

Mrs. White gripped her husband’s arm.

“We offer two hundred pounds in compensation.”

Mr. White fell from his chair.

A week after the burial, Mrs. White woke him in the dark.

“The paw. We have two wishes left.”

“No,” he said.

“Wish him alive again.”

He sat without moving. He was afraid of what might return.

“Please,” she whispered. “He’s our son.”

Downstairs, he found the paw.

“I wish my son alive again.”

He let it fall. They returned to bed, but neither slept.

A slow, quiet knock came from below.

“Herbert!”

Mrs. White ran downstairs. Mr. White caught her sleeve, but she pulled away.

A chair scraped across the hall. The knocking grew louder.

“The bolt,” she cried. “I can’t reach it.”

He dropped to his knees, feeling across the floor for the paw. The knocking shook the door.

His fingers closed around it as she drew back the last bolt. He whispered his third and last wish.

The knocking stopped.

She opened the door. Cold wind swept in, and her wail of loss reached him from the doorway.

The street lamp opposite shone on a quiet, deserted road.
