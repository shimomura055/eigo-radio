# Three Wishes

Rain beat against the windows of the country house. Beside the fire, Herbert moved a chess piece and smiled.

“You let me win.”

“Then return the favour,” said Mr. White.

Mrs. White laughed over her knitting. A knock interrupted them.

Their old friend, a retired sergeant-major, stood outside in the rain. Later, by the fire, he took a dried monkey’s paw from his pocket.

“From India. It gives a man three wishes.”

“Have you tried it?” Mr. White asked.

“I’ve had mine.”

The old friend fell silent, turning the paw over.

“Those who interfere with fate find sorrow. Don’t use it.”

He threw it into the fire, but Mr. White pulled it out.

“Keep it, then. But don’t blame me.”

Herbert leaned forward.

“Two hundred pounds would pay for the house. Nothing too grand, Father.”

Mr. White raised the paw, smiling.

“I wish for two hundred pounds.”

It twisted in his hand. He cried out and dropped it.

Herbert looked under the table.

“Not a penny.”

They laughed about it again over breakfast. Then Herbert left for work.

That afternoon, a stranger in a silk hat paused at their gate. Twice he raised his hand before finally knocking.

Inside, he stood holding his hat.

“I’m from the firm. Your son was caught in the machinery. He was killed.”

Mrs. White’s hand closed around her husband’s arm.

“The firm offers two hundred pounds in compensation.”

Mr. White fell from his chair.

A week after the burial, Mrs. White woke him in the dark.

“The paw,” she said. “We have two wishes left.”

“No.”

“Get it. Wish him alive again.”

He sat still. He was afraid of what might return.

“He’s our son,” she whispered.

At last he went downstairs and found the paw.

“I wish my son alive again.”

He let it fall.

They returned to bed. Neither slept. Then a slow, quiet knock sounded below.

“Herbert!”

Mrs. White ran downstairs. Mr. White caught her sleeve, but she pulled away.

A chair scraped across the hall.

“The bolt,” she called. “I can’t reach it.”

The knocking grew louder.

Mr. White dropped to his knees, sweeping his hands across the floor. His fingers touched wood, then the edge of the rug.

The door shook beneath the blows.

He found the paw just as Mrs. White drew back the last bolt. He whispered his third and last wish.

The knocking stopped.

She opened the door. Nobody was there. Cold wind swept into the hall, and her wail of loss reached him from the doorway.

The street lamp opposite shone on a quiet, deserted road.
