[Story Seed -- inspired by a real, rights-cleared source, freely reimagined;
this is NOT a summary to reproduce, just raw material]
- On a wet, windy night in a lonely house, Mr. White and his son Herbert play chess by the fire while Mrs. White knits; Herbert wins with a quick mate, and the family jokes warmly with each other as they wait for an old friend to arrive.
- The visitor is Sergeant-Major Morris, home after twenty-one years in India. Over whiskey he talks of wars and strange lands; when pressed, he reluctantly shows a dried, mummified monkey's paw that an old fakir enchanted so that three men could each have three wishes, to show that fate rules people's lives and those who interfere do so to their sorrow.
- Morris says he has had his three wishes and will not say what they cost, only that the first owner's third wish was for death; he throws the paw on the fire, Mr. White snatches it out, and Morris warns him: if you must wish, wish for something sensible, and do not blame me for what happens.
- Herbert jokes that his father should wish for two hundred pounds to clear the house. Mr. White holds up the paw and wishes aloud; it twists in his hand like a snake and he cries out. Nothing else happens, and the household goes to bed uneasy while the wind rises outside.
- The next day the Whites laugh at their fears. Herbert goes to work at the firm Maw and Meggins; his mother watches from the window for the postman, and a stranger in a new silk hat hesitates at the gate three times before coming in.
- The stranger from Maw and Meggins tells the parents, with great reluctance, that Herbert was caught in the machinery and is dead; the firm admits no liability but offers two hundred pounds in compensation. Mr. White faints. The sum is exactly what he wished for.
- After the burial, the old couple live in silent grief. A week later Mrs. White wakes in the night and begs her husband to use the paw to wish Herbert alive again; he refuses at first, knowing how terribly the body was hurt, but she drags him to it, and in the dark parlour he is terrified of what the wish may bring.
- He makes the second wish. Nothing happens, and they lie in the dark. Then a quiet, stealthy knock sounds on the door, followed by louder knocks. Mrs. White cries that it is Herbert and runs to the door; her husband, in terror of what stands outside, searches the floor on his hands and knees for the paw, finds it just as the last bolt slides back, and breathes his third and last wish. The knocking stops, the door opens on a cold wind, and the street lamp shows only a quiet, deserted road.

[How to convert it]
Keep the English country house at night in the original period and the original order of events; do not modernize the setting. Write it mainly in natural third-person English at about 400 words, as a quiet horror story whose fear comes from restraint: the wind, the silence, the knock and what the characters say and do. Use short, clear sentences and concrete sounds (wind, a knock, a bolt, a chair), and let the dialogue carry the dread; do not describe gore, and do not add monsters, sound-effect words, or any new supernatural event. Mr. White is the main character; his wife is Mrs. White and his son is Herbert; the old soldier who brings the paw and the man from the firm are described by their roles (the old friend, the sergeant-major, the stranger, the man from the firm), not by name. Preserve the central story, the loving family relationship, the cause-and-effect chain and the ending: the monkey's paw gives three wishes; the old friend warns them that it brings sorrow and tells them not to use it; Mr. White wishes for two hundred pounds as a joke and the paw twists in his hand; the next day the man from the firm brings news that Herbert was killed in the machinery, with two hundred pounds as compensation; Mrs. White later demands a wish to bring Herbert back; Mr. White fears what would return; after the second wish a slow knock comes at the door; Mrs. White runs to open it; Mr. White, terrified, finds the paw and makes the third and last wish; the knocking stops; when the door is opened the road is quiet and empty under the street lamp. Keep the original's ending exactly as unexplained as it is: never say what the third wish was and never say what was outside the door. Do not explain or hint at the death of Herbert or the meaning of the money before the man from the firm says it, and do not add a moral or a comment of your own. Compress by trimming the travel talk, the family banter, the visitor's formalities and the funeral, not by cutting the warning, the three wishes, the compensation scene, the second wish and the knocking.
Level and length override: write natural upper-intermediate (B1-B2) English, about 400 words (roughly 330-470); ignore any earlier A2 / word-count instruction.
