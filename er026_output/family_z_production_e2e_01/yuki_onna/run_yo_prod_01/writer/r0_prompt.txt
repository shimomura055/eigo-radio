[Story Seed -- inspired by a real, rights-cleared source, freely reimagined;
this is NOT a summary to reproduce, just raw material]
- Two woodcutters, the old Mosaku (the master) and his eighteen-year-old apprentice Minokichi, work in a forest across a wide river and cross by ferry; one very cold evening a great snowstorm catches them on the way home.
- The boatman has gone, leaving his boat on the other side, so they shelter in his small hut: one door, no window, no fire. They fasten the door and lie down under their straw rain-coats. The old man falls asleep at once; the young man lies awake a long time listening to the wind and the river, then falls asleep too.
- Minokichi is woken by snow in his face. The door has been forced open, and by the snow-light he sees a woman all in white bending over Mosaku and blowing her breath on him; her breath is like bright white smoke.
- She turns to Minokichi and bends lower and lower until her face almost touches him. He cannot cry out. She is very beautiful, though her eyes make him afraid. She smiles and whispers that she meant to treat him like the other man, but she pities him because he is so young; she will not hurt him now.
- Her warning: if he ever tells anybody, even his own mother, about what he has seen this night, she will know it, and she will kill him. 'Remember what I say!'
- She leaves through the doorway and is gone. Minokichi can move again, bars the door, and wonders whether he only dreamed it. He touches Mosaku's face in the dark and finds it ice: the old man is dead.
- At sunrise the ferryman finds Minokichi lying senseless beside the frozen body. Minokichi recovers after a long illness, badly frightened by the old man's death, but he says nothing about the woman in white. He goes back to work, alone, and his mother helps him sell the wood.
- One evening in the winter of the following year, on the road home, he overtakes a tall, slim, good-looking girl with a lovely voice. She says her name is Yuki (O-Yuki), that she has lost both parents, and that she is going to Edo to look for work as a servant with some poor relations. They talk, feel drawn to each other, and when they reach the village he asks her to rest at his house.
- His mother makes her welcome, takes a sudden fancy to her and persuades her to stay; Yuki never goes to Edo and becomes his wife, a very good daughter-in-law. When Minokichi's mother dies some five years later, her last words are of affection and praise for her son's wife.
- Yuki bears Minokichi ten children, handsome and very fair of skin. The country-folk think her a wonderful person, different by nature from themselves: most peasant women age early, but after ten children she looks as young and fresh as on the day she came.
- One night after the children are asleep, Yuki is sewing by the light of a paper lamp. Watching her, Minokichi says that the light on her face makes him think of a strange thing that happened when he was eighteen: he saw somebody as beautiful and white as she is now, very like her. Without lifting her eyes, Yuki says: 'Tell me about her... Where did you see her?'
- Minokichi tells her everything: the terrible night in the ferryman's hut, the White Woman who stooped over him, smiling and whispering, and the silent death of old Mosaku. He says that was the only time he saw a being as beautiful as she is; of course the woman was not human and he was very afraid of her, but she was so white; and he has never been sure whether it was a dream or the Woman of the Snow.
- Yuki flings down her sewing, stands, bows over him where he sits and shrieks into his face: 'It was I - I - I! Yuki it was! And I told you then that I would kill you if you ever said one word about it! But for those children asleep there, I would kill you this moment! And now you had better take very, very good care of them; for if ever they have reason to complain of you, I will treat you as you deserve!'
- As she screams her voice becomes thin, like the crying of wind; she melts into a bright white mist that spires to the roof-beams and shudders away through the smoke-hole. Never again was she seen.

[How to convert it]
Keep the original's three scenes in the original order and the original period and place (old Japan, a village by a river): (1) the snowstorm night in the ferryman's hut, where a woman in white kills the old woodcutter and spares the young one on one condition; (2) the next winter and the following years, when a girl named Yuki joins the young man's family and becomes his wife; (3) one night years later, by the lamp, when he breaks the condition and learns who she is. Do not modernize the setting, do not add a narrator who knows more than the story, and do not add a new character, a new supernatural event, a motive or explanation beyond the original, or a moral. Do not change the ending: she leaves, melting into white mist, and is never seen again. Write it in natural English at about 400 words as a ghost story told in the third person and the past tense by a calm storyteller who speaks to a listener. Make the story easy to follow by ear: open each of the three scenes with one short, plain sentence that says when and where it is (for example the storm night in the hut; the winter of the next year, on the road home; a night years later, in the house), keep the order of events strictly chronological, and say clearly who does what (Minokichi, the old woodcutter, the woman in white, Yuki, Minokichi's mother). Use the names Minokichi and Yuki (these two only; use Yuki, not O-Yuki) and role names for everyone else (the old woodcutter, Minokichi's mother, the woman in white, the children); the old woodcutter, the mother and the children have no spoken lines. Only two characters speak, and each speaks in quoted speech with a plain speech tag that names the speaker (for example '"...," whispered the woman in white.' or '"...," said Minokichi.'): the woman in white in the hut, and Minokichi and Yuki in the last scene; Yuki and the woman in white are the same speaker. Give the dialogue a real part of the story, roughly a third of the words, so that the two voices carry the fear: the woman's whispered warning in the hut, a short exchange between Minokichi and Yuki by the lamp, and Yuki's final outburst. Make the promise unmistakable and repeat its exact idea at the end: in the hut she says that if he ever tells anybody, even his own mother, what he has seen this night, she will know it and she will kill him; in the last scene Yuki says that she told him then that she would kill him if he ever said one word about it. State plainly, in one short sentence, that Minokichi told nobody about the woman in white after the old man's death, so that the listener keeps the promise in mind through the second scene. In the second scene, move quickly: they meet, he brings her home, his mother welcomes her, they marry, they have many children, and she seems never to grow old (one plain sentence). Keep the eerie, quiet atmosphere with short, concrete sentences and sensory details (the wind, the snow in his face, white breath like smoke, the dead man's icy face, the lamp light on her face, the voice like wind); do not explain who or what the woman in white is. Do not reveal before the last scene that Yuki is the woman in white: the original's quiet clues (Yuki's white skin, her fair children, her never growing old) may appear as plain facts, but the narrator must not point at them. In the last scene, let the reveal land at once and clearly: Minokichi tells the story of the hut, Yuki cries out that it was she, repeats the condition he broke, says that only the sleeping children save his life, tells him to take good care of them, and then her voice thins like wind and she melts into white mist.
Level and length override: write natural upper-intermediate (B1-B2) English, about 400 words (roughly 330-470); ignore any earlier A2 / word-count instruction.
