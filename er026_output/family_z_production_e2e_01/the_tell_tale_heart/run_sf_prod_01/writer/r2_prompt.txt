# Listen Carefully

The old man’s housemate insisted he was not mad, only nervous. His illness had sharpened his senses, especially his hearing. He heard everything in heaven and earth. Surely that proved he was sane. He asked his listener to judge how calmly he told the story.

He loved the old man, who had never harmed him. He wanted none of his money. Only the eye troubled him: pale blue, covered by a thin film, like a vulture’s. Whenever it turned towards him, his blood went cold. He would kill the old man to close that eye forever.

For seven nights, at midnight, he gently lifted the bedroom latch. He pushed a covered lantern through the doorway, then slowly put his head inside. A narrow ray found the eye.

Always closed.

He could not act. It was the eye he hated, not the old man. Each morning, he greeted him kindly.

On the eighth night, his thumb slipped on the lantern’s metal fastening.

“Who’s there?” the old man cried.

His housemate froze. For an hour, he stood motionless while the old man sat listening. Then came a small groan of terror.

He opened the lantern a little. Light fell across the eye.

Wide open.

A low, quick beating reached his ears, like a watch wrapped in cotton. The old man’s heart. It beat faster, louder, until he feared a neighbour would hear.

With a yell, he rushed forward. The old man shrieked once. He dragged him onto the floor and pulled the heavy bed over him. Beneath it, the beating continued.

Then it stopped.

He hid the body beneath three floorboards and carefully replaced them. Nothing showed.

At four in the morning, someone knocked. Three police officers stood outside. A neighbour had heard a shriek.

He welcomed them with a smile. A bad dream, he explained; the shriek had been his own. The old man was away in the country.

He showed them the house, then brought chairs into the bedroom. Calm and confident, he sat directly above the hidden body. The officers settled down and chatted.

Then a ringing troubled his ears.

He spoke louder, but the sound became clearer: low and quick, like a watch wrapped in cotton.

He rose and paced. He scraped his chair across the boards.

Louder. Still louder.

The officers kept smiling and talking. They seemed to hear nothing.

But they knew. They must know. Those pleasant smiles were a cruel game. How could he bear another second?

“Stop pretending!” he screamed. “I did it! Tear up the planks! Here, beneath my feet! It is the beating of his hideous heart!”
