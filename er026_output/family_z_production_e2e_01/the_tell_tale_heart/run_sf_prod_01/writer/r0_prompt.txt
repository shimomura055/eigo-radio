[Story Seed -- inspired by a real, rights-cleared source, freely reimagined;
this is NOT a summary to reproduce, just raw material]
- A narrator who speaks directly to a listener ('you') opens by insisting he is not mad: he is only very, very nervous, and the illness has sharpened his senses, above all his hearing. He asks the listener to hear how calmly and healthily he can tell the whole story.
- He says he loved the old man, who never wronged or insulted him, and he did not want the old man's gold. It was the eye: a pale blue eye with a film over it, like a vulture's. Whenever it fell on him his blood ran cold, and little by little he decided to kill the old man and be rid of the eye for ever.
- He argues that a madman could not have been so wise: he was never kinder to the old man than in the week before the deed. Every night at about midnight he turned the latch, opened the door very slowly, put in a dark lantern shut tight, then his head, then let one thin ray fall on the eye. For seven nights the eye was always closed, so he could not do the work, because it was the Evil Eye, not the old man, that troubled him. Every morning he went in boldly and spoke warmly to the old man, asking how he had slept.
- On the eighth night he is more careful than ever and feels proud of his own cunning; he even chuckles, and the old man moves in bed. In the pitch-dark room he keeps pushing the door open. Then his thumb slips on the tin fastening of the lantern, the old man sits up and cries out, 'Who's there?', and the narrator keeps perfectly still, saying nothing, for a whole hour, while the old man sits listening in the dark.
- The narrator hears a slight groan and knows it is the groan of mortal terror, a sound he knows from his own nights. He pities the old man while chuckling inside, and imagines him telling himself it is only the wind in the chimney or a mouse or a cricket, all in vain, because Death has stalked before him.
- At last the narrator opens a tiny crevice in the lantern and a single thin ray, like a spider's thread, falls full on the vulture eye, which is wide open. His fury grows. Then he hears a low, dull, quick sound, like a watch wrapped in cotton: the beating of the old man's heart. It grows quicker and louder every moment, and the narrator, himself in terror, fears a neighbour will hear it.
- With a loud yell he throws open the lantern and leaps into the room. The old man shrieks once. The narrator drags him to the floor and pulls the heavy bed over him. The heart goes on beating, muffled, for many minutes, then stops. He examines the body: stone dead, no pulsation. The eye will trouble him no more.
- Proud of his caution, he hides the body under three planks taken up from the floor of the chamber and replaces them so cleverly that no eye could see anything wrong; there is no stain, no blood-spot. It is four o'clock when a knocking comes at the street door. Three men enter and say they are police officers: a neighbour heard a shriek in the night, and they have been sent to search the house.
- The narrator, with a light heart, welcomes them. He says the shriek was his own, in a dream, and the old man is away in the country. He shows them through the whole house, even the old man's room and his treasures, brings chairs into the room, and, in the wild audacity of his triumph, sets his own seat on the very spot above the hidden body.
- The officers are satisfied and chat of ordinary things, but the narrator grows pale and his head aches; he feels a ringing in his ears that becomes more distinct until he understands that the noise is not inside his ears. It is the same low, dull, quick sound. He talks faster and louder, argues about trifles, paces, raves, swears, and grates his chair upon the boards, but the noise rises over everything and grows louder, louder, louder, while the men go on chatting pleasantly and smiling.
- He decides they hear it, they suspect, they know, and they are mocking his horror; he cannot bear their smiles any longer and feels he must scream or die. He shrieks, 'Villains! dissemble no more! I admit the deed! Tear up the planks! Here, here! It is the beating of his hideous heart!'

[How to convert it]
Keep the original's night-time house, the first-person voice and the original order of events; do not modernize the setting, do not change the narrator into a third person, and do not add any new character, motive, explanation or supernatural event. Write it in clear, natural English at about 400 words as a short horror monologue whose fear comes from the narrator's calm, over-confident voice and from sound and silence: the latch, the thin ray of light, the old man's cry, the long silence, the low quick beating like a watch wrapped in cotton, the knock at the door, the officers' chatting. Use short, concrete sentences; let the narrator keep addressing the listener ('you', 'you fancy me mad'); keep his insistence that he is not mad and that his hearing is sharp, because that claim is the heart of the story. Neither the narrator nor the old man nor the officers have personal names: refer to them as I, the old man, and the officers (the police), exactly as the original does. Preserve the central events in order and the cause-and-effect chain: the narrator, who insists he is calm and not mad, is driven only by the old man's pale blue 'vulture eye' and not by money or hatred; for seven nights at midnight he looks in with a single thin ray of light but the eye is always closed; on the eighth night the old man wakes, cries 'Who's there?' and sits listening in terror while the narrator stays still; when the ray falls on the open eye the narrator hears the old man's heart beating louder and louder, fears a neighbour will hear, rushes in, kills the old man and hides the body under the floorboards, leaving no trace; three officers come because a neighbour heard a shriek, and the narrator calmly shows them the house and even sits above the hiding place; as they chat he hears the low, quick beating again, louder and louder, which the officers do not seem to hear; unable to bear it, certain they are mocking him, he screams that he did it and tells them to tear up the planks. Keep the ending exactly as the original leaves it: the story ends on the narrator's confession, with no aftermath, no arrest, no explanation of whether the sound was real or only in his mind, and no moral or comment of your own. Do not describe the body in graphic detail: the hiding of the body beneath the floor may be told in one plain, calm sentence. Do not reveal before the right moment that the narrator will confess, or that the sound in the last scene is the heartbeat again; do not add sound-effect words. Compress by trimming the narrator's repeated arguments that he is wise, the old man's imagined thoughts and the details of the cleaning up, not by cutting the vulture eye, the seven midnight nights, the eighth night, the heartbeat, the killing, the hiding under the floor, the officers, the sound that grows or the confession.
Level and length override: write natural upper-intermediate (B1-B2) English, about 400 words (roughly 330-470); ignore any earlier A2 / word-count instruction.
