[Story Seed -- inspired by a real, rights-cleared source, freely reimagined;
this is NOT a summary to reproduce, just raw material]
- A self-possessed fifteen-year-old girl keeps a nervous visitor company while her aunt is late, and the visitor, who has come only to deliver a letter of introduction for his nerve cure, knows nothing about the household.
- The girl tells him a calm, tragic tale about the large French window left open: three years ago to the day, the aunt's husband and two young brothers walked out through it to go shooting and were lost in a bog, their bodies never found, and the aunt still waits for them every evening.
- The girl's story is built on small convincing details (the husband's white waterproof coat, the youngest brother singing 'Bertie, why do you bound?' to tease his sister, the little brown spaniel) and a sudden break in her voice, which makes the visitor believe her and makes the open window feel eerie.
- The aunt then enters, cheerful and busy, speaks of the men coming home any moment, keeps looking toward the open window, and barely listens to the visitor's long talk about his illness, so everything she does seems to confirm the girl's story.
- At twilight, three figures with guns, one with a white coat over his shoulders and a tired brown spaniel at their heels, walk silently across the lawn toward the window; then a young voice chants 'I said, Bertie, why do you bound?' and the terrified visitor flees past a cyclist on the road.
- The men come in, ordinary and muddy; the aunt says the visitor must have seen a ghost, and the girl calmly says he was afraid of the spaniel, backing it up with a vivid invented tale about his past; the closing line says that romance at short notice was her speciality.

[How to convert it]
Keep the English country house setting and the original order of events; do not modernize the setting. Write it mainly in natural third-person English at about 400 words, with a nervous visitor (Framton Nuttel) as the only person with a personal name and role descriptions such as the girl (the niece), her aunt, the aunt's husband and her two brothers for the others. Preserve the central story, the relationships between the characters, the cause-and-effect chain and the ending: the girl's calm ghost story about the open window; the aunt's cheerful behavior that seems to confirm it; the three figures appearing in the twilight and the familiar song; the visitor running away in panic; and the girl's smooth lie about the dog, which reveals that she made up the whole tragedy. Keep the suspense of the moment the figures appear and keep the dry humor of the final lie. Do not explain the trick, hint that the girl is lying, or comment on the twist before the ending, and do not add a moral or an explanation after the last line; the surprise is the point. Compress by trimming the visitor's background and the aunt's small talk, not by cutting the foreshadowing about the open window.
Level and length override: write natural upper-intermediate (B1-B2) English, about 400 words (roughly 330-470); ignore any earlier A2 / word-count instruction.
