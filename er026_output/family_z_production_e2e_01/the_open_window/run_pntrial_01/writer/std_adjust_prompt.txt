# The Open Window

Framton sat stiffly in the drawing room, turning his letter of introduction between his fingers. His doctor had ordered rest and quiet, but visiting strangers in the country only made him more nervous.

“My aunt will be down shortly,” said the fifteen-year-old girl. “Do you know much about her?”

“Nothing, really.”

“Then you won’t know about the tragedy.”

She glanced towards the French window, standing open to the lawn.

“Three years ago today, her husband and her two brothers went through that window to shoot. They crossed a bog, and the ground gave way beneath them. None came back. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton’s fingers became still.

“My aunt believes they’ll return. She leaves the window open every evening. Her husband carried a white waterproof coat over his arm. Their little brown spaniel went too. The youngest brother kept singing, ‘I said, why do you bound?’ He knew it annoyed her.”

The girl’s voice shook.

“Sometimes, on evenings like this, I almost feel they’ll come walking—”

She broke off as her aunt entered.

“Forgive me for keeping you waiting,” the aunt said brightly. “I hope you don’t mind the open window. My husband and brothers will be back from shooting soon. They always come in this way. Such muddy boots!”

Framton struggled to find something to say.

“The doctors agree I must avoid excitement—”

“Indeed?” Her attention had already moved beyond him. Suddenly she smiled. “Oh, here they are at last!”

The girl stared through the window, her lips parted in horror.

Framton turned.

Three men were crossing the darkening lawn, carrying guns. One had a white coat over his shoulders. A small brown dog followed.

For a moment, Framton heard nothing.

Then a young voice called through the twilight:

“I said, why do you bound?”

He snatched his hat and stick and fled through the hall. Outside, he rushed into the road, forcing a cyclist to swerve sharply to avoid him.

The men stepped through the window, bringing cold air and the smell of wet earth.

“Who was that running away?” asked the husband.

“A most extraordinary visitor,” said the aunt. “He talked about his nerves, then rushed off without a word. You’d think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” the girl said calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. Once, in India, a pack chased him into a graveyard. He spent the night in an open grave, with the dogs growling above him. A terrible experience.”
