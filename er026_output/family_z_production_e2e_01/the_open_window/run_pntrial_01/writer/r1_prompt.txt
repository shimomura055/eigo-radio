# The Open Window

Framton sat stiffly in the country-house drawing room, holding his letter of introduction. His doctor had ordered rest and quiet, but visiting strangers did little for his nerves.

“My aunt will be down shortly,” said the fifteen-year-old girl. “Do you know much about her?”

“Nothing, really.”

“Then you won’t know about the tragedy.”

She looked towards the large French window, which stood open to the lawn.

“Three years ago today, her husband and her two brothers went out through that window to shoot. They never came back. They crossed a bog, and the ground gave way beneath them. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton looked at the open window.

“My aunt still thinks they’ll return,” the girl continued. “She leaves it open every evening. Her husband carried a white waterproof coat over his arm. Their little brown spaniel went too. And her youngest brother kept singing, ‘I said, why do you bound?’ He knew it annoyed her.”

Her voice suddenly broke.

“Sometimes, on quiet evenings like this, I almost feel they’ll walk through that window…”

She stopped as her aunt entered.

“Do forgive me for keeping you waiting,” the aunt said brightly. “I hope you don’t mind the window open. My husband and brothers will be home from shooting soon. They always come in this way. Such muddy boots!”

Framton tried to discuss his illness.

“The doctors agree that I must avoid excitement—”

“Indeed?” She was looking past him towards the lawn. “Oh, here they are at last!”

The girl stared through the window, her lips parted.

Framton turned.

Three figures were moving across the darkening lawn. Each carried a gun. One had a white coat over his shoulders. A small brown dog followed close behind.

For a moment, no sound reached the room.

Then a young voice called through the twilight:

“I said, why do you bound?”

Framton seized his hat and stick. He rushed through the hall and out of the front door. On the road, a cyclist swerved sharply to avoid him.

The men came through the window, bringing cold air and the smell of wet earth.

“Who was that running away?” asked the husband.

“A most extraordinary visitor,” said the aunt. “Talking about his nerves, then off without a word. You’d think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” said the girl calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. Once, in India, a pack chased him into a graveyard. He spent the night in an open grave, with the dogs growling above him. A terrible experience.”

Inventing stories at short notice was her speciality.
