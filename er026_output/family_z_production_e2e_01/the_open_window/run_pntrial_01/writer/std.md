# The Open Window

Framton sat very straight in the sitting room with his letter of introduction. He turned it between his fingers. His doctor had ordered quiet rest, but meeting strangers in the country made him more nervous.

“My aunt will come down soon,” the fifteen-year-old girl said. “How much do you know about her?”

“I don’t know anything about her.”

“So you haven’t heard about the terrible thing that happened.”

She looked at the open window leading onto the lawn.

“Three years ago today, her husband and two brothers went out through that window to shoot. They crossed soft, wet land, but it sank under them. They never returned, and no one ever found their bodies.”

Framton’s fingers stopped moving the letter.

“My aunt keeps that window open every evening, because she thinks they’ll return. Her husband had a white raincoat over his arm. Their little brown spaniel went with them. The youngest brother kept singing, ‘I said, why are you jumping?’ He knew this bothered her.”

Her voice began to shake as she spoke.

“Sometimes, on evenings like this, I almost think they’ll walk back—”

Then her aunt came in, and the girl stopped talking.

“Sorry I made you wait,” her aunt said cheerfully. “I hope the open window is all right with you. My husband and brothers will soon be home from shooting. They always enter here, with such muddy boots!”

Framton found it hard to say anything.

“All my doctors say I must stay away from excitement—”

“Really?” She was already looking past him, but suddenly smiled. “Look, they’re finally here!”

The girl looked through the window, her mouth open with fear.

Framton turned to look, too.

Three men carrying guns crossed the lawn as daylight faded. One had a white coat over his shoulders, and a little brown dog followed them.

For a moment, everything seemed silent to Framton.

Then, in the fading light, a young voice called out:

“I said, why are you jumping?”

Framton grabbed his hat and stick, then ran through the hall. Outside, he rushed into the road, making a cyclist turn sharply to miss him.

Cold air and the smell of wet earth followed the men through the window.

“Who ran away just now?” her husband asked.

“What a strange visitor,” his wife said. “He spoke about his nerves, then ran off without another word. He acted like a man who’d just seen a ghost.”

“The spaniel frightened him,” the girl said calmly. “He told me he was terribly afraid of dogs. In India, some dogs once chased him into a graveyard. All night, he stayed in an open grave while the dogs growled above him. It was terrible.”

She was very good at making up stories quickly.
