# The Open Window

Framton sat stiffly in the drawing room, turning his letter of introduction between his fingers. His doctor had ordered rest and quiet. So far, visiting strangers in the country had only made him more nervous.

“My aunt will be down shortly,” said the fifteen-year-old girl. “Do you know much about her?”

“Nothing, really.”

“Then you won’t know about the tragedy.”

She glanced towards the French window, open to the lawn.

“Three years ago today, her husband and her two brothers went through that window to shoot. They crossed a bog, and the ground gave way. None came back. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton’s fingers stopped moving.

“My aunt still believes they’ll return. She leaves the window open every evening. Her husband had a white waterproof coat over his arm. Their little brown spaniel went too. The youngest brother kept singing, ‘I said, why do you bound?’ He knew it annoyed her.”

Her voice broke.

“Sometimes, on evenings like this, I almost feel they’ll come walking—”

She stopped. Her aunt had entered.

“Forgive me for keeping you waiting,” the aunt said brightly. “I hope you don’t mind the open window. My husband and brothers will be back from shooting soon. They always come in this way. Such muddy boots!”

Framton tried to talk about his illness.

“The doctors agree I must avoid excitement—”

“Indeed?” Her eyes moved past him. “Oh, here they are at last!”

The girl stared outside, her lips parted. Framton turned.

Three figures were crossing the darkening lawn, each carrying a gun. One had a white coat over his shoulders. A small brown dog followed.

For a moment, everything seemed silent.

Then a young voice called through the twilight:

“I said, why do you bound?”

Framton snatched his hat and stick. He fled through the hall and out of the front door. On the road, a cyclist swerved sharply to avoid him.

The men stepped through the window, bringing cold air and the smell of wet earth.

“Who was that running away?” asked the husband.

“A most extraordinary visitor,” said the aunt. “Talking about his nerves, then rushing off without a word. You’d think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” the girl said calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. Once, in India, a pack chased him into a graveyard. He spent the night in an open grave, with the dogs growling above him. A terrible experience.”

Inventing stories at short notice was her speciality.
