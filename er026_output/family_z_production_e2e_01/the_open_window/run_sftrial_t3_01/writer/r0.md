# The Open Window

Framton sat on the edge of his chair, turning his letter of introduction between nervous fingers. Country air and quiet were supposed to help him. Meeting strangers did not.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Only her name,” Framton admitted.

Vera looked towards the long French window, open to the lawn.

“Then you won’t know about her tragedy. It happened three years ago today.”

“A tragedy?”

“Her husband and her two younger brothers went shooting. They left through that window, with their little brown spaniel. Out on the moor, they stepped into a bog. The ground looked solid, but it swallowed them. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton stopped turning the letter.

“My aunt still believes they’ll come back. That’s why she leaves the window open until dark. Her husband carried a white waterproof coat over his shoulders. Her youngest brother went out singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always sang it to annoy her.”

Her voice broke.

“Sometimes, on quiet evenings, I almost feel they might—”

The door opened.

“I’m sorry to have kept you waiting,” said her aunt brightly. “I hope Vera has entertained you.”

“Very well,” Framton managed.

“You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be home soon. They’ve been shooting. Such a mess they’ll make of my carpets!”

Framton tried to speak about his health.

“The doctors insist on complete rest. No excitement, and nothing that might—”

His hostess was looking past him towards the lawn.

“Here they are at last!” she cried. “Just in time for tea!”

Framton turned to Vera. She was staring through the window, her face pale with horror.

He looked.

Three figures were crossing the lawn in the twilight. Each carried a gun. One had a white coat over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel followed at their heels.

Then a young voice called through the fading light:

“I said, Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton seized his hat. A moment later, he was through the front door and running down the drive.

The men stepped through the window, muddy and hungry.

“Who was that rushing away?” asked the husband.

“A most peculiar visitor,” said his wife. “He looked as if he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. Once, in India, a pack chased him into a cemetery. He spent the night in an open grave, with the dogs growling above him.”

Romance at short notice was her speciality.
