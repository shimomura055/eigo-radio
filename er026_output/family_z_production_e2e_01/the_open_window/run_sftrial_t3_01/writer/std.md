# The Open Window

Framton Nuttel sat forward and turned a letter in his sweaty hands. He came to the country for rest, but the strange, quiet room made him more nervous.

“My aunt will come downstairs soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “How well do you know her?”

“I don’t know her at all.”

“Then you haven’t heard about the terrible thing that happened.”

Through the open window, she watched shadows growing across the grass.

“Three years ago today, her husband and her two young brothers went shooting. Their brown spaniel went with them through that window.”

Framton’s fingers stopped moving the letter round and round.

“The ground they crossed looked safe, but it was a bog,” Vera said. “All three sank, and nobody ever found their bodies.”

“What an awful thing to happen.”

“She still expects them to come home, so she leaves the window open until dark.”

Vera started speaking in a much quieter voice.

“Her husband left with his white coat across his shoulders. The youngest brother sang as he went out: ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He often sang it to make her angry.”

Her eyes stayed on the grass outside the window.

“On an evening like this, I sometimes feel that—”

Just then, the room’s door swung open.

“Sorry I kept you waiting!” her aunt said as she hurried in. “I hope you don’t mind the window being open. My husband and brothers will return from shooting soon and leave mud all over my carpets!”

Framton tried to smile. Her cheerful voice scared him more than tears would.

“My doctors say I must rest completely, without anything exciting or anything that might—”

“Here they come, just in time for tea!” she cried.

Framton looked at Vera’s pale face as she stared through the window.

He began to turn round, very slowly.

Three men carried guns as they crossed the dark grass. A white coat lay across one man’s shoulders. Their brown spaniel followed them across the grass.

He heard a young voice sing, “Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton struggled to breathe. Those men were back from the bog!

He grabbed his hat and raced out and down the drive.

The men came in through the window, hungry and covered in mud.

“Who was that man running away?” her aunt’s husband asked.

“He was a very strange visitor,” his wife replied.

“He ran because of the spaniel,” Vera explained calmly. “He told me he was very afraid of dogs. In India, a group of dogs chased him into a graveyard. He had to stay all night in an open grave, with the dogs growling above him.”

She sounded so sure that nobody thought she was lying.

Making up stories in seconds was Vera’s special skill.
