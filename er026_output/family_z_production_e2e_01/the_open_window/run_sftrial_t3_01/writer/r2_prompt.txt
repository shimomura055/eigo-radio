# The Open Window

Framton Nuttel sat on the edge of his chair, twisting his letter of introduction. He had come to the country for peace and quiet, but waiting in a stranger’s sitting room only made him more nervous.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Only her name.”

Vera glanced towards the French window, standing open to the lawn. The evening air was growing cool.

“Then you haven’t heard about her tragedy. It happened three years ago today.”

Framton’s fingers became still.

“Her husband and her two younger brothers went shooting. They walked out through that window, with their brown spaniel. On the moor, they stepped into a bog. It looked like firm ground.” She paused. “Their bodies were never found.”

“How dreadful,” Framton whispered.

“My aunt believes they’ll return. She leaves the window open until dark, waiting for them.” Vera swallowed. “Her husband had his white coat over his shoulders. Her youngest brother was singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always did that to annoy her.”

She stared at the empty lawn.

“Sometimes, when it gets quiet, I almost feel they might walk back through—”

The door opened, and her aunt hurried in.

“I’m so sorry! I hope Vera has kept you entertained.”

“Very well,” Framton said.

“You don’t mind the window? My husband and brothers will be home soon. They’ve been shooting. Imagine what their muddy boots will do to my carpets!”

Framton looked away. Her cheerful voice was somehow worse than tears.

“My doctors advise complete rest,” he began. “Nothing exciting, nothing that might disturb—”

“Here they are!” his hostess cried. “Just in time for tea!”

Framton turned to Vera. Her face was pale, her eyes fixed on the window.

He looked outside.

Three figures were crossing the lawn in the fading light, guns in their hands. One wore a white coat over his shoulders. A brown spaniel followed close behind.

Then a young voice called:

“I said, Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton’s heart hammered. The dead men were coming towards the house.

He snatched up his hat and rushed out. Within seconds, his footsteps were racing down the drive.

The men stepped through the window, muddy and ready for tea.

“Who was that?” asked the husband.

“A peculiar visitor,” his wife replied. “He looked as though he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he’s terrified of dogs. In India, a pack chased him into a cemetery. He spent the night in an open grave while they growled above him.”

Inventing convincing stories at a moment’s notice was Vera’s speciality.
