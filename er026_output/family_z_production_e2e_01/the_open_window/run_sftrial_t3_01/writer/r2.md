# The Open Window

Framton Nuttel sat on the edge of his chair, turning his letter of introduction between damp fingers. He had come to the country to rest his nerves. So far, the silence of this unfamiliar sitting room had only made them worse.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know her well?”

“Not at all.”

“Then you won’t know about the tragedy.”

She looked towards the open French window. Beyond it, the lawn was beginning to fade into shadow.

“It happened three years ago today. Her husband and her two young brothers went shooting. They left through that window, with their brown spaniel.”

Framton stopped twisting the letter.

“They crossed a part of the moor that looked solid,” Vera continued. “But it was a bog. All three sank into it. Their bodies were never found.”

“How terrible.”

“My aunt still thinks they’ll come back. That’s why she keeps the window open until dark.”

Vera’s voice grew quieter.

“Her husband was carrying his white coat over his shoulders. The youngest brother went out singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He used to sing that just to annoy her.”

She stared across the lawn.

“On evenings like this, I sometimes feel—”

The door opened.

“So sorry to keep you waiting!” Her aunt hurried in. “I hope you don’t mind the open window. My husband and brothers will be back from shooting soon. They’ll leave mud all over my carpets!”

Framton forced a smile. Her cheerful certainty was more frightening than tears.

“My doctors say I need complete rest,” he began. “No excitement, and nothing that might—”

“Here they are!” she cried. “Just in time for tea!”

Framton glanced at Vera. She was pale, staring through the window.

Slowly, he turned.

Three figures were crossing the darkening lawn, carrying guns. One had a white coat over his shoulders. A brown spaniel trotted behind them.

A young voice called, “Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton could hardly breathe. They had come back from the bog.

He grabbed his hat and ran. A moment later, his footsteps were racing down the drive.

The men stepped through the window, muddy and hungry.

“Who was that rushing away?” asked the husband.

“A most peculiar visitor,” said his wife.

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he’s terrified of dogs. In India, a pack chased him into a cemetery. He had to spend the night in an open grave while they growled above him.”

She spoke so naturally that no one questioned her.

Inventing convincing stories at a moment’s notice was Vera’s speciality.
