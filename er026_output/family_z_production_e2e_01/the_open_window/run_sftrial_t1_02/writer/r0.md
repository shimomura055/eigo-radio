# The Open Window

Framton Nuttel sat holding his letter of introduction, wishing his hands would stop shaking. Opposite him, a girl of fifteen waited calmly for her aunt.

“Do you know much about my aunt?” the niece asked.

“Only her name. My doctor recommended the country. Rest for my nerves, you know.”

The girl glanced towards the open French window.

“Then you don’t know about her tragedy. Three years ago today.”

“Her tragedy?”

“Her husband and her two young brothers went shooting. They walked out through that window, with their little brown spaniel. They never came back. The ground was terribly wet that summer. They sank into a bog.”

Framton looked out across the lawn.

“Their bodies were never found,” she continued. “My aunt still leaves the window open for them. Her husband carried his white waterproof coat over his arm. The youngest brother went out singing, ‘Why do you bound?’ He always sang that to tease her.”

Her voice broke.

“Sometimes, on quiet evenings like this, I almost feel they might—”

She stopped as her aunt hurried into the room.

“I hope my niece has kept you company! You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be home from shooting soon. They’ll make a dreadful mess of the carpets.”

Framton struggled to answer. The poor woman kept looking towards the lawn.

“My doctors insist on complete rest,” he began. “No excitement, and nothing that might—”

“Yes, of course,” said his hostess, barely listening.

Then her face brightened.

“Here they are at last!”

Framton turned towards the niece. She was staring through the window, her lips parted in horror.

He looked too.

Three figures were crossing the darkening lawn. They carried guns. One had a white coat hanging over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel followed at their heels.

They came nearer without a word.

Then a young voice called through the twilight:

“I said, why do you bound?”

Framton seized his hat and stick. He rushed through the hall and out of the house. At the gate, a cyclist swerved into the hedge to avoid him.

The men entered through the window, muddy and cheerful.

“Who was that running down the drive?” asked the aunt’s husband.

“A most peculiar visitor,” she said. “He talked about his nerves, then suddenly fled. You’d think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” said the niece calmly. “He told me he’s terrified of dogs. In India, a pack chased him into a graveyard. He spent the night in an empty grave, with the dogs growling above him.”

Romance at short notice was her speciality.
