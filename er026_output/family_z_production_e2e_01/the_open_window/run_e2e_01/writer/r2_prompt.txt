# The Open Window

Framton Nuttel sat stiffly in the drawing room, turning his letter of introduction between his fingers. His doctor had sent him to the country for peace and quiet. Meeting strangers was hardly peaceful, but so far only a fifteen-year-old girl had appeared.

“My aunt will be down shortly,” she said. “Do you know much about her?”

“Nothing, really.”

The girl glanced towards the French window, open to the lawn.

“You may wonder why we leave that open in October.”

“It is rather warm,” Framton offered.

“Three years ago today, my aunt’s husband and her two young brothers went shooting. They left through that window.”

She paused.

“They never came back.”

Framton stopped turning the letter.

“They crossed the marsh where the ground looked firm. All three sank into a bog. Their little brown spaniel went with them. The bodies were never found.”

Outside, the lawn lay empty.

“My aunt still expects them home,” the girl continued. “She keeps the window open until dark. Her husband had his white waterproof coat over his shoulders. Her youngest brother was singing, ‘Oh, brother, why do you bound?’ He always sang it to annoy her.”

She swallowed and lowered her voice.

“Sometimes, towards evening, I almost feel they really will—”

The door opened.

The aunt hurried in, apologising for keeping Framton waiting.

“I hope my niece has entertained you. You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be back directly. They’ve been shooting. Such a mess they’ll make of my carpets!”

Framton searched for something safe to say. He explained his doctor’s orders: complete rest, no excitement. His hostess listened, but her eyes kept wandering towards the lawn.

Suddenly, she smiled.

“Here they are at last!”

Framton looked at the girl. Her lips were parted, her eyes fixed on the window.

He slowly turned.

Three men were crossing the darkening lawn, guns in their hands. One wore a white coat over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel followed.

Then a young voice called through the twilight:

“Oh, brother, why do you bound?”

Framton seized his hat and stick. He rushed through the hall and out of the gate, forcing a passing cyclist into the hedge.

The men stepped through the window.

“Who was that running away?” asked the aunt’s husband, wiping his muddy boots.

“A most peculiar visitor,” his wife replied. “You’d think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” the girl said calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. A pack once chased him into a cemetery. He spent the night in an empty grave, with them looking down at him.”

She glanced at the sleepy spaniel.

“He said they took turns.”
