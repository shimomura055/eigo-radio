# The Open Window

Framton Nuttel sat stiffly in the country-house drawing room, holding his letter of introduction. He had come for peace and quiet, on his doctor’s advice. So far, only a fifteen-year-old girl had appeared.

“My aunt will be down shortly,” she said. “Do you know much about her?”

“Nothing, really.”

The girl looked towards the large French window, standing open to the lawn.

“You may wonder why we keep that open on an October afternoon.”

“It is rather warm,” Framton suggested.

“Three years ago today, my aunt’s husband and her two young brothers went shooting. They walked out through that window. They never came back.”

Framton’s fingers tightened around the letter.

“They crossed the marsh, where the ground looked firm. All three sank into a bog. Even the little brown spaniel disappeared. Their bodies were never found.”

Her voice remained quiet.

“My aunt still believes they’ll return. She leaves the window open until dark. Her husband carried his white waterproof coat over his shoulders. Her youngest brother went out singing, ‘Oh, brother, why do you bound?’ He always sang that to annoy her.”

The girl swallowed.

“Sometimes, in the evening, I almost feel they really will—”

She stopped.

The aunt hurried in, full of apologies.

“I hope my niece has kept you entertained. You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be back directly. They’ve been shooting. Such a mess they’ll make of my carpets!”

Framton tried to discuss his treatment. Complete rest, his doctor insisted; no excitement. But his hostess kept looking past him towards the lawn.

Then her face brightened.

“Here they are at last!”

Framton turned towards the girl. She was staring through the window, her lips parted.

Slowly, he looked round.

Three figures were crossing the darkening lawn, carrying guns. One had a white coat over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel followed at their heels.

Through the twilight came a young voice:

“Oh, brother, why do you bound?”

Framton snatched his hat and stick. He rushed through the hall and out of the gate. A cyclist swerved into the hedge to avoid him.

The men stepped through the window.

“Who was that running away?” asked the aunt’s husband, wiping his muddy boots.

“A most peculiar visitor,” said his wife. “You’d think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” the girl said calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. A pack once chased him into a cemetery. He spent the night in an empty grave, with them looking down at him.”

She glanced at the sleepy spaniel.

“He said they took turns.”
