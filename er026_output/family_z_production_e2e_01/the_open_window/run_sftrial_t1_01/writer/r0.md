# The Open Window

Framton Nuttel sat stiffly in the country-house drawing room, holding his letter of introduction. He had come to the countryside to rest his nerves. So far, meeting strangers was doing them little good.

“My aunt will be down shortly,” said her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Nothing,” Framton admitted.

The girl looked towards the large French window, standing open to the lawn.

“You may wonder why we leave that open on an October evening.”

“It is quite warm,” he said.

“Three years ago today, my aunt’s husband and her two young brothers went out through that window to shoot. They never came back. They stepped into a deep bog. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton’s fingers tightened around the letter.

“The little brown spaniel went too. My aunt still thinks they’ll return. She leaves the window open until dark.” The girl paused. “Her husband carried his white waterproof coat. Her youngest brother sang, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always sang that to annoy her.”

Her voice broke.

“Sometimes, on quiet evenings like this, I almost feel they might—”

She stopped as her aunt hurried in.

“I hope my niece has kept you entertained! You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be back from shooting any minute. They’ll make a dreadful mess of the carpets.”

Framton tried to speak about his illness.

“The doctors insist on complete rest. No excitement of any kind.”

“Oh, really?” said his hostess, looking past him towards the lawn.

He turned his chair slightly away from the window.

“Here they are at last!” she cried.

Framton looked at the girl. She was staring outside, her eyes wide with horror.

He turned.

In the fading light, three figures were crossing the lawn. Each carried a gun. One had a white coat over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel followed at their heels.

They approached without speaking.

Then a young voice called through the twilight:

“I said, Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton seized his hat and stick. He rushed through the hall and out into the road, where a cyclist swerved into the hedge.

The men came in, muddy and hungry.

“Who was that running away?” asked the aunt’s husband.

“A most peculiar visitor,” she said. “You’d think he had seen a ghost.”

“The spaniel frightened him,” said the niece calmly. “He told me he was once chased by dogs in India. He spent the night hiding in an empty grave, with the dogs growling above him. He has never recovered.”

Romance at short notice was her speciality.
