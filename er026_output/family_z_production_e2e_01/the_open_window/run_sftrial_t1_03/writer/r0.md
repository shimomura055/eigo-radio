# The Open Window

Framton Nuttel sat on the edge of his chair, holding his letter of introduction. Quiet country life was supposed to help his nerves. Yet here he was, waiting in a stranger’s drawing room, wondering what to say to a fifteen-year-old girl.

“My aunt will be down soon,” the niece said. “Do you know much about her?”

“Nothing, really.”

The girl looked towards the large French window, which stood open to the lawn.

“Then you don’t know about her tragedy. It happened three years ago today.”

“Her tragedy?”

“Her husband and her two young brothers went shooting. They walked out through that window, with their little brown spaniel. On the moor, they stepped into a bog. It had been a terribly wet summer. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton’s fingers tightened around the letter.

“My aunt still thinks they’ll return. That’s why she leaves the window open until dark. Her husband carried his white waterproof coat over his arm. Her youngest brother went out singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always sang that to annoy her.”

The girl’s voice broke.

“Sometimes, on quiet evenings like this, I almost think they will—”

She stopped as her aunt hurried in.

“I’m so sorry to keep you waiting! You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be home from shooting soon. They always come in this way. I expect they’ll bring half the mud in the county with them.”

Framton tried to speak about his treatment.

“The doctors insist on complete rest. No excitement of any kind.”

“Indeed?” said his hostess, looking past him towards the lawn.

Then her face brightened.

“Here they are at last!”

Framton turned to the niece. She was staring through the window, her eyes wide.

He turned too.

In the fading light, three figures were crossing the grass. They carried guns. One had a white coat over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel followed at their heels.

A young voice called, “I said, Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton seized his hat and stick. A moment later he was racing down the drive. A cyclist swerved into the hedge to avoid him.

“Who was that?” asked the husband, stepping inside with his muddy boots.

“A most peculiar visitor,” said his wife. “He rushed off as though he’d seen a ghost.”

“The spaniel frightened him,” said the niece calmly. “He told me dogs once chased him into a graveyard in India. He spent the night in an open grave, with them growling above him. He can’t bear dogs now.”

Romance at short notice was her speciality.
