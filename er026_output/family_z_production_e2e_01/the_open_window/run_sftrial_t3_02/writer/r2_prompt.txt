# The Open Window

Framton Nuttel sat stiffly in the drawing room, gripping his letter of introduction. He had come to the country to rest his nerves. So far, meeting strangers was only making them worse.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Almost nothing.”

Vera looked towards the French window, standing open to the lawn.

“Three years ago today, my aunt’s husband and her two young brothers went shooting. They walked out through that window with their brown spaniel. The summer had been terribly wet. On the moor, they stepped into a bog. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton’s fingers tightened around the letter.

“My aunt still expects them back. Every evening, she keeps the window open until dark. Her husband carried his white coat over his arm that day. Her youngest brother was singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always sang it to annoy her.”

Vera’s voice trembled.

“Sometimes, in the evening, I almost expect—”

She stopped. In the silence, Framton heard the clock ticking. He was relieved when her aunt hurried in.

“I hope Vera has kept you company! You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be back soon. They’ve been shooting. Just imagine the mud they’ll bring onto my carpets!”

Framton stared at her cheerful face. He tried to explain his doctor’s orders: complete rest, no excitement. But she kept watching the lawn.

Suddenly, she smiled.

“Here they are at last!”

Vera’s eyes widened in horror. Framton turned to follow her gaze.

Three figures were crossing the grass in the fading light, guns in their hands. A white coat hung over one man’s shoulders. Behind them came a tired brown spaniel.

They drew closer.

Then a young voice called, “I said, Bertie, why do you bound?”

Dead men were walking towards him.

Framton could not breathe. He seized his hat and rushed from the room. A moment later, he was racing down the drive.

The men stepped through the window, muddy and quite ordinary.

“Who was that running away?” asked the aunt’s husband.

“A peculiar visitor,” she replied. “He talked about his nerves, then ran off. You’d think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. Once, in India, a pack chased him into a graveyard. He spent the night in an empty grave, with the dogs standing above him, growling.”

Inventing convincing stories at a moment’s notice was Vera’s special talent.
