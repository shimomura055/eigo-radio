# The Open Window

Framton Nuttel sat on the edge of his chair, turning his letter of introduction between damp fingers. He had come to the country to rest his nerves, but this quiet drawing room offered little comfort.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know her well?”

“Hardly at all.”

Vera looked towards the open French window.

“Three years ago today, her husband and her two young brothers went shooting. They left through that window, with their brown spaniel. It had been a terribly wet summer. Out on the moor, they sank into a bog. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton stopped turning the letter.

“My aunt still thinks they’ll return. She keeps the window open every evening until dark. Her husband had his white coat over his arm. Her youngest brother was singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He used to sing it just to annoy her.”

Her voice shook. “Sometimes, when the light begins to fade, I almost expect—”

She broke off. The clock ticked loudly in the silence.

Then her aunt hurried in.

“I hope Vera has kept you company! You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be back soon. They’ve been shooting. They’ll bring half the countryside in on their boots!”

Her cheerful voice made Framton feel cold. So she really was still waiting. He began explaining his doctor’s orders: complete rest, no excitement. But she hardly listened. Her eyes kept returning to the lawn.

Suddenly she smiled. “Here they are at last!”

Vera stared outside, her face full of horror.

Framton turned. Three figures were crossing the lawn in the fading light, carrying guns. One had a white coat over his arm. A tired brown spaniel followed them.

Then a young voice called, “I said, Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton could not draw breath. The men from the bog were coming towards him.

He grabbed his hat and stumbled out. A moment later, he was racing down the drive.

The men stepped through the window, muddy and quite ordinary.

“Who was that running away?” asked the aunt’s husband.

“A peculiar visitor. He talked about his nerves, then suddenly ran. You’d think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. Once, in India, a pack chased him into a graveyard. He spent the whole night in an empty grave, with the dogs above him, growling.”

Making up convincing stories on the spot was Vera’s special talent.
