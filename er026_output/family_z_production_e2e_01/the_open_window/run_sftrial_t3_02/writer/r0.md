# The Open Window

Framton sat stiffly in the country-house drawing room, holding his letter of introduction. He had come to the country to rest his nerves, but visiting strangers was hardly restful.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Almost nothing.”

Vera looked towards the tall French window, open to the lawn.

“You may wonder why we leave that open on an October evening.”

“It is rather warm,” Framton suggested.

“Three years ago today, my aunt’s husband and her two young brothers went out through that window to shoot. They took their little brown spaniel. On the moor, they stepped into a bog. It had been a terribly wet summer. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton’s fingers tightened around the letter.

“My aunt still believes they’ll come back. Every evening she leaves the window open until dark. Her husband was carrying his white waterproof coat. Her youngest brother was singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always sang it to annoy her.”

Vera’s voice broke.

“Sometimes, when it gets quiet like this, I almost expect—”

She stopped. Framton was grateful when her aunt hurried into the room.

“I hope Vera has kept you company! You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be home soon. They’ve been shooting, and they’ll make a dreadful mess of the carpets.”

Framton tried to speak about his doctor’s orders: complete rest, no excitement. But his hostess kept looking past him towards the lawn.

Then her face brightened.

“Here they are at last!”

Vera stared through the window, her eyes wide with horror.

Framton turned.

In the fading light, three figures were crossing the grass. Each carried a gun. A white coat hung over one man’s shoulders, and a tired brown spaniel followed at their heels.

They came nearer without a word.

Then a young voice called, “I said, Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton seized his hat. A moment later, he was racing down the drive.

The men stepped through the window, muddy and quite ordinary.

“Who was that running away?” asked the aunt’s husband.

“A most peculiar visitor,” his wife said. “He talked about his nerves, then rushed off. You’d think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. Once, in India, a pack chased him into a graveyard. He spent the night in an empty grave while they stood above him, growling.”

Romance at short notice was her speciality.
