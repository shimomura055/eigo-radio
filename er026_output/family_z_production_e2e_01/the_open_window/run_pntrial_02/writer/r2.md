# The Open Window

Framton sat with his letter of introduction pressed against his knee. His doctor had ordered rest in the country to help his nerves. So far, calling on strangers had only made them worse.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Only her address.”

Vera glanced towards the French window, open to the lawn despite the October chill.

“Her great tragedy happened three years ago today.”

Framton looked up.

“Her husband and two brothers went shooting. They left through that window and never came back. They sank into a bog beyond the fields. Their bodies were never found.”

The room suddenly seemed colder.

“My aunt still thinks they’ll return,” Vera continued. “She keeps the window open until dark. Her husband had his white waterproof coat over his shoulders. Her youngest brother was singing, ‘I said, why do you bound?’ He always sang it to annoy her. Their little brown spaniel went too.”

Her voice trembled.

“On quiet evenings like this, I almost think—”

The door opened, and her aunt came in smiling.

“I hope Vera has been entertaining you. You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be home soon. They always come in this way. Such muddy boots!”

Framton tried to smile. The poor woman was still waiting for the dead.

“The doctors say I must avoid excitement,” he began. “Complete rest, and no worry.”

“Indeed?” She was watching the lawn, barely listening as he described his treatment.

Then she leaned forward.

“Here they are at last!”

Framton turned to Vera. Her eyes were wide with horror, fixed on the window.

He looked round.

Three figures were crossing the darkening lawn, guns under their arms. One had a white coat over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel followed close behind.

They came towards the window in silence.

Then a young voice sang, “I said, why do you bound?”

Framton seized his hat and stick. He rushed through the hall and down the drive. At the gate, a cyclist swerved into the hedge to avoid him.

“Who was that?” asked the aunt’s husband, stepping through the window. “He left rather quickly.”

“A most peculiar visitor,” his wife replied. “Anyone would think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me wild dogs chased him abroad once. He spent the night in an empty grave while they barked above him.”

She watched the dog settle beside the fire.

“He said he preferred cats.”
