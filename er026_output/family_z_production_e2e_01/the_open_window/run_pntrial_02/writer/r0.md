# The Open Window

Framton held his letter of introduction on his knee. His doctor had ordered rest in the country, but visiting strangers was hardly restful.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Only her address.”

Vera glanced towards the tall French window, open to the lawn despite the cool October air.

“Her great tragedy happened three years ago today.”

“Her tragedy?”

“Her husband and her two brothers went shooting. They walked out through that window and never came back. They sank into a bog beyond the fields. The bodies were never found.”

Framton looked at the empty lawn.

“My aunt believes they’ll return,” Vera continued. “So she keeps the window open until dark. Her husband carried his white waterproof coat over his shoulders. Her youngest brother went out singing, ‘I said, why do you bound?’ He always sang that to annoy her. Their little brown spaniel followed them.”

Her voice suddenly broke.

“Sometimes, on quiet evenings like this, I almost think—”

She stopped as her aunt entered.

“I hope Vera has been entertaining you,” she said brightly. “You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be home soon. They always come in this way. Such muddy boots!”

Framton tried to smile. It was horrible to hear her speak so cheerfully.

“The doctors insist I avoid excitement,” he began. “Complete rest, and no worry of any kind.”

“Indeed?” said his hostess, looking past him towards the lawn.

He explained his treatment in greater detail. She scarcely seemed to listen.

Then her face brightened.

“Here they are at last!”

Framton turned towards Vera. She was staring through the window, her eyes wide.

He looked round.

Three figures were crossing the darkening lawn, guns under their arms. One had a white coat over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel followed at their heels.

They came closer without a word.

Then a young voice sang, “I said, why do you bound?”

Framton seized his hat and stick. The hall, the drive and the gate passed in a blur. A cyclist swerved into the hedge to avoid him.

“Who was that?” asked the aunt’s husband, stepping through the window. “He left rather quickly.”

“A most peculiar visitor,” said his wife. “Anyone would think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he was once chased by wild dogs abroad. He spent the night in an empty grave while they barked above him.”

She watched the spaniel settle beside the fire.

“He said he preferred cats.”
