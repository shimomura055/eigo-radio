# The Open Window

Framton pressed his letter of introduction against his knee. His doctor had ordered rest in the countryside for his nerves. But visiting strangers had only made him feel worse.

“My aunt is coming,” said her fifteen-year-old niece, Vera. “How well do you know her?”

“I just have her address.”

Vera looked at the French window, open to the lawn even in the October cold.

“Something terrible happened to her three years ago today.”

Framton raised his head.

“Her husband and two brothers left through that window to go shooting. They sank into soft, wet ground beyond the fields. They never returned, and nobody found their bodies.”

Suddenly, the room felt colder to him.

“She still expects them back and leaves that window open until dark,” Vera said. “Her husband wore a white raincoat over his shoulders. Her youngest brother sang, ‘I said, why do you jump?’ He always sang that to upset her. Their little brown spaniel went with them.”

Her voice began to shake.

“When an evening is this quiet, I almost think—”

Her aunt opened the door and entered with a smile.

“I hope you enjoyed talking to Vera, and the open window doesn’t bother you. My husband and brothers will soon be home. They always enter here, with such muddy boots!”

Framton tried to smile. This poor woman still expected the dead to return.

“My doctors say I need complete rest without excitement or worry,” he began.

“Really?” She watched the lawn, hardly listening to his talk about treatment.

Suddenly, she moved forward in her chair.

“They’re back at last!”

Framton looked at Vera, who watched the window with wide, frightened eyes.

He turned to look outside.

Three men crossed the darkening lawn with guns under their arms. One wore a white coat over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel walked just behind them.

Without a word, they walked towards the open window.

A young man began singing, “I said, why do you jump?”

Framton grabbed his hat and walking stick. He ran through the hall and down the drive. At the gate, a cyclist turned sharply into the hedge to avoid him.

“Who was that visitor?” her husband asked, stepping through the window. “He was in a hurry.”

“A very strange guest,” his wife answered. “He looked like someone who had seen a ghost.”

“The spaniel frightened him,” Vera said calmly. “Wild dogs once chased him in another country, he told me. He spent that night in an empty grave, with the dogs barking above him.”

She watched the dog lie down beside the fire.

“He told me he liked cats better.”
