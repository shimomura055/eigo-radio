# The Open Window

Framton held his letter of introduction tightly on his knee. His doctor had ordered rest in the country, but visiting strangers only made him more nervous.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Only her address.”

Vera glanced at the French window, standing open to the lawn in the cool October air.

“Her great tragedy happened three years ago today.”

“Her tragedy?”

“Her husband and two brothers went shooting. They left through that window and never returned. They sank into a bog beyond the fields. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton looked uneasily towards the empty lawn.

“My aunt still believes they’ll come back. That’s why she keeps it open until dark. Her husband carried his white waterproof coat over his shoulders. Her youngest brother was singing, ‘I said, why do you bound?’ He always sang that to annoy her. Their little brown spaniel followed them.”

Her voice broke.

“Sometimes, on quiet evenings like this, I almost think—”

She stopped as her aunt entered.

“I hope Vera has been entertaining you,” she said brightly. “You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be home soon. They always come in this way. Such muddy boots!”

Framton forced a smile. How could she speak so cheerfully about people who were dead?

“The doctors insist I avoid excitement,” he began. “Complete rest, and no worry of any kind.”

“Indeed?” His hostess was looking past him towards the lawn. As he explained his treatment, she scarcely listened.

Suddenly her face brightened.

“Here they are at last!”

Framton turned to Vera. Her eyes were wide, fixed on the window.

He looked round.

Three figures crossed the darkening lawn, guns under their arms. One had a white coat over his shoulders. Behind them came a tired brown spaniel.

They approached without a word.

Then a young voice sang, “I said, why do you bound?”

Framton grabbed his hat and stick. The hall, drive and gate rushed past in a blur. A cyclist swerved into the hedge to avoid him.

“Who was that?” asked the aunt’s husband, stepping through the window. “He left rather quickly.”

“A most peculiar visitor,” said his wife. “Anyone would think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he was once chased by wild dogs abroad. He spent the night in an empty grave while they barked above him.”

She watched the spaniel settle beside the fire.

“He said he preferred cats.”
