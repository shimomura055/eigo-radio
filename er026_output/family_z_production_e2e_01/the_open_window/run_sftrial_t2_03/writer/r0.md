# The Open Window

Framton sat on the edge of his chair, turning his letter of introduction between his fingers. The country was supposed to help his nerves. So far, visiting strangers had only made them worse.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know anyone here?”

“No. Nothing about the place, really.”

Vera looked towards the long French window, open to the lawn.

“Then you won’t know about her tragedy. Three years ago today.”

“Her tragedy?”

“Her husband and her two young brothers went out through that window to shoot. They took the little brown spaniel. Beyond the woods, they stepped into a bog. The ground looked safe, but it swallowed them. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton’s fingers stopped moving.

“My aunt believes they’ll come back. That’s why she leaves the window open until dark. Her husband had his white waterproof coat over his arm. Her youngest brother went out singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always sang it to annoy her.”

Vera’s voice broke.

“Sometimes, on quiet evenings like this, I almost feel they might—”

She stopped as her aunt hurried in.

“I’m so sorry to keep you waiting! You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be home soon. They always come in this way, bringing half the countryside on their boots.”

Framton tried to smile. He began explaining that his doctors had ordered complete rest, but her eyes kept moving past him towards the lawn.

Then her face brightened.

“Here they are at last!”

Framton felt cold. He glanced at Vera. She was staring through the window without a word.

He turned.

Three figures were crossing the lawn in the fading light, guns under their arms. One carried a white coat over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel followed at their heels.

Then a young voice called through the twilight:

“I said, Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton snatched his hat and stick. A moment later he was racing down the drive.

The men stepped through the window, muddy and cheerful.

“Who was that running away?” asked the aunt’s husband.

“A very nervous visitor,” she said. “You’d think he’d seen a ghost.”

“The spaniel frightened him,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he was terribly afraid of dogs. Once in India, a pack chased him into a graveyard. He had to spend the night in a newly dug grave, with the dogs growling above him. He said their teeth shone in the moonlight.”

Romance at short notice was her speciality.
