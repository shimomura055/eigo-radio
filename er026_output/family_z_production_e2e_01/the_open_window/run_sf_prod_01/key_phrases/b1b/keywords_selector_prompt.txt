【記事本文】
Framton sat on the edge of his chair, turning his letter of introduction between his fingers. His doctor had recommended peace and country air, but visiting strangers was hardly peaceful.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Almost nothing,” he admitted.

Vera glanced towards the French window, which stood wide open to the lawn.

“Her great tragedy happened three years ago today.”

Framton stopped turning the letter.

“Her husband and her two young brothers went out shooting through that window. Their little brown spaniel went with them. They tried to cross a bog, but the ground gave way beneath them. They all disappeared. Their bodies were never found.”

“How terrible,” Framton murmured.

“My aunt still thinks they’ll come back. She keeps the window open until dark, waiting for them.” Vera’s voice grew quieter. “Her husband had his white coat over his arm. Her youngest brother was singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always sang it to tease her.”

She looked out across the empty lawn.

“Sometimes, on evenings like this, I almost expect to see them.”

Before Framton could reply, her aunt hurried into the room.

“I hope Vera has kept you company! You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be back soon. They’ve been shooting. Their boots will be covered in mud, of course!”

Framton felt a chill. So she really was waiting.

He began describing his illness, trying to keep his eyes away from the window. His hostess listened with only half her attention. Again and again, she looked past him towards the lawn.

Then her face brightened.

“Here they are at last!”

Vera stared outside, her eyes wide with horror.

Framton turned.

Three figures were crossing the lawn in the fading light, carrying guns. A white coat hung over one man’s arm. A brown spaniel followed close behind.

Across the quiet garden came a young voice:

“Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton could scarcely breathe. The dead were coming towards the house.

He grabbed his hat and stick and rushed out. Within seconds, he was running down the drive.

The men stepped through the window, muddy and hungry.

“Who was that?” asked the husband.

“A most peculiar visitor,” his wife said. “He talked about his nerves, then suddenly ran away.”

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. In India, a pack chased him into an empty grave. He spent the whole night there, with them snarling above him.”

Inventing convincing stories at a moment’s notice was Vera’s speciality.
