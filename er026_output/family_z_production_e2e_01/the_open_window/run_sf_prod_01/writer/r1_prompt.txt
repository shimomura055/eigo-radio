# The Open Window

Framton sat on the edge of his chair, holding his letter of introduction. His doctor had ordered country air and complete rest, but visiting strangers did little to calm his nerves.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know anything about her?”

“Almost nothing.”

Vera glanced towards the tall French window, which stood open to the lawn.

“Her great tragedy happened three years ago today.”

“Her tragedy?”

“Her husband and her two young brothers went shooting. They walked out through that window, with their little brown spaniel. They never came back. A bog swallowed them. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton shifted in his chair.

“She keeps the window open until dark,” Vera continued. “She believes they’ll return. Her husband had his white waterproof coat over his arm. Her youngest brother went out singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always sang it to tease her.”

Her voice broke.

“Sometimes, on quiet evenings like this, I almost feel they might—”

She stopped. Framton was relieved when her aunt hurried into the room.

“I hope Vera has kept you company! You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be home soon. They’ve been shooting, and they’ll make a terrible mess of the carpets.”

Framton searched for something safe to say. He began explaining his illness and the need to avoid excitement.

Her aunt listened with half an ear, her eyes moving repeatedly towards the lawn.

Then her face brightened.

“Here they are at last!”

Framton turned to Vera. She was staring through the window, her lips parted.

He turned too.

Three figures were crossing the lawn in the fading light. Each carried a gun. One had a white coat hanging over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel followed at their heels.

They approached without a word.

Then a young voice called through the twilight:

“I said, Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton snatched his hat and stick. A moment later he was racing down the drive.

The men stepped through the window, muddy and hungry.

“Who was that running away?” her husband asked.

“A most peculiar visitor,” said her aunt. “He talked about his nerves, then rushed off without a word. Anyone would think he’d seen a ghost.”

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. In India, a pack chased him into an empty grave. He spent the night there, with the dogs snarling above him. A rather unpleasant cure for sleeplessness.”

Romance at short notice was her speciality.
