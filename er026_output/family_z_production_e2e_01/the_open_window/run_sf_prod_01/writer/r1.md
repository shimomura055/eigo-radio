# The Open Window

Framton sat stiffly on the edge of his chair, turning his letter of introduction between his fingers. His doctor had ordered rest and country air. So far, meeting strangers had only made him more nervous.

“My aunt will be down soon,” said Vera, her fifteen-year-old niece. “Do you know much about her?”

“Almost nothing.”

Vera looked towards the French window, standing wide open to the lawn.

“Her great tragedy happened three years ago today.”

“Her tragedy?”

“Her husband and her two young brothers went out shooting through that window. Their little brown spaniel followed them. They crossed a bog, but the ground gave way. All of them disappeared. Their bodies were never found.”

Framton’s fingers tightened around the letter.

“My aunt still believes they’ll return. That’s why she leaves the window open until dark. Her husband was carrying his white coat over his arm. Her youngest brother was singing, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He always sang that to tease her.”

Vera’s voice trembled.

“On evenings like this, I sometimes feel they might really come back.”

Before Framton could answer, her aunt hurried in.

“I hope Vera has kept you company! You don’t mind the open window? My husband and brothers will be home soon. They’ve been shooting. Just think what their muddy boots will do to my carpets!”

Framton tried not to look at Vera. He began explaining his illness, grateful for a subject that had nothing to do with the dead.

His hostess barely listened. Her eyes kept returning to the lawn.

Suddenly, she smiled.

“Here they are at last!”

Vera stared through the window, her mouth open in horror.

Framton turned.

Three figures were crossing the darkening lawn, guns in their hands. A white coat hung over one man’s arm. Behind them came a tired brown spaniel.

Then a young voice called:

“I said, Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton’s blood ran cold. They had come back.

He seized his hat and stick and rushed from the room. Moments later, he was racing down the drive.

The men stepped through the window, muddy and hungry.

“Who was that?” asked the husband.

“A peculiar visitor,” his wife replied. “He talked about his nerves, then ran away.”

“It was the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he was terrified of dogs. In India, a pack chased him into an empty grave. He spent the whole night there while they snarled above him. He couldn’t bear the sight of another dog.”

Inventing convincing stories at a moment’s notice was Vera’s speciality.
