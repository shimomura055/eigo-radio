[Story Seed -- inspired by a real, rights-cleared source, freely reimagined;
this is NOT a summary to reproduce, just raw material]
- On Christmas Eve Della counts her savings again and again: one dollar and eighty-seven cents, much of it in pennies, and nothing near enough to buy Jim a present; she cries on the shabby couch, then looks around the cheap furnished flat she shares with Jim, whose pay has shrunk from thirty to twenty dollars a week.
- The young couple have only two things they are truly proud of: Jim's gold watch that was his father's and grandfather's, and Della's long, shining brown hair that falls below her knees.
- Suddenly Della decides: she pulls down her hair, puts on her old brown jacket and hat, and hurries to Madame Sofronie's shop, where Madame buys her hair for twenty dollars and Della asks for the money quickly.
- Della searches the shops for two hours and finds the perfect gift for Jim, a simple, quiet platinum chain for his watch (twenty-one dollars), which she buys, knowing the watch hangs on an old leather strap because Jim cannot afford a chain.
- At home Della curls what is left of her hair, looks like a schoolboy, and prays quietly that Jim will still think she is pretty; at seven o'clock, with the dinner nearly ready, she hears his step on the stairs.
- Jim comes in, thin and serious, in an old overcoat and without gloves, and stops dead, staring at her with an expression she cannot read; Della runs to him, says she sold her hair because she could not get through Christmas without a present for him, and that nobody could ever count her love.
- Jim holds her, then takes a package from his pocket: the set of tortoise-shell combs Della had longed for in a Broadway shop window. She cries with joy and then with sorrow because her hair is gone, and then holds out the chain; Jim sits down with a smile and says he sold the watch to buy the combs.
- The narrator ends by saying that the magi, the wise men who brought gifts to the baby in the manger, invented the art of giving Christmas presents, and that these two foolish children, who gave up their greatest treasures for each other, were the wisest of all who give gifts: they are the magi.

[How to convert it]
Keep the New York flat on Christmas Eve and the original order of events; do not modernize the setting. Write it mainly in natural third-person English at about 400 words, with Della as the main character, her husband Jim as the second main character, and the hair-goods shop owner described by her role (Madame, the hair buyer) or by her name only once. Preserve the central story, the loving relationship between the couple, the cause-and-effect chain and the ending: Della has only $1.87; the two treasures are Della's hair and Jim's gold watch; Della sells her hair and buys a platinum chain for the watch; Jim stares at her short hair with an expression she cannot read; Jim gives her the tortoise-shell combs; Della shows the chain; Jim tells her he sold the watch to buy the combs. Keep the bittersweet irony that each gift is now useless because of what the other gave up, and the warmth behind it. The title of the story, 'The Gift of the Magi', must be explained in the closing paragraph in the narrator's own words, as in the original: the magi were the wise men who brought gifts to the Babe in the manger and invented the art of giving Christmas presents; the two foolish young people, who gave up their greatest treasures for each other, were the wisest of all who give gifts; they are the magi. Do not explain the irony or hint at the watch, the combs or the sale of the watch before Jim reveals them, and do not add a moral of your own. The original's own closing reflection about the magi is not such an addition: it is the closing paragraph of the story and must be kept in short form. Compress by trimming the narrator's jokes, the description of the flat and the repeated small talk, not by cutting the foreshadowing in the description of the two treasures.
Level and length override: write natural upper-intermediate (B1-B2) English, about 400 words (roughly 330-470); ignore any earlier A2 / word-count instruction.

[Must-keep -- story essentials]

- Ending (required): Each of the two has sold their greatest treasure to buy a gift for the other, and each gift is now useless: Della's hair is gone, so the combs are useless, and Jim's watch is sold, so the chain is useless. Yet the love behind the sacrifices makes the two foolish young people the wisest of all gift-givers, the magi, which explains the title.
