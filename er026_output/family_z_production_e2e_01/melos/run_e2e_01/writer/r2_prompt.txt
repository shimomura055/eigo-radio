# Before Sunset

“You kill people without a fair trial,” Melos said. “A man only has to frighten you, and you order his death.”

The shepherd stood before the ruler, dust covering his sandals. Around him, the guards went still. The ruler’s eyes narrowed.

“Then you shall die as well.”

Melos felt his stomach tighten, but he kept his voice steady.

“Give me three days first. My sister is getting married. Let me attend her wedding, and I will return.”

“Return to your own execution?” The ruler laughed.

“My friend will stay here in my place.”

His friend stepped forward without a word.

The ruler looked from one man to the other. At last, he agreed.

“Before sunset on the third day. If you are late, your friend dies.”

His friend took Melos’s hand. His fingers were warm and steady.

“Go,” he said. “I’ll wait.”

At the wedding, Melos smiled whenever his sister smiled. He watched her join hands with her husband. Yet every time a shadow crossed the floor, his eyes moved toward the door.

Before dawn on the third day, he set out for the city.

At the river, he stopped.

The bridge was gone. Below the empty crossing, brown water rushed past, carrying branches in its current. Rain had turned the river into a flood.

Melos searched the banks. There was no other crossing.

He stepped into the water.

The current swept his feet away. He fought toward the far bank, choking on muddy water. A branch came within reach. He caught it, held on, and dragged himself ashore.

Shaking, he ran on.

Beyond the river, attackers blocked the road. One seized his arm while another swung a stick. Melos struck out, broke free, and stumbled past them. Their shouts followed him. He did not look back.

By afternoon, every step hurt.

Then his legs gave way.

He lay beside the road, his face pressed against the earth. The city was still far away.

*I cannot do it.*

He could stay here. No one would find him before sunset. His friend would die, but Melos would live.

For one terrible moment, he felt relief.

Then he remembered the hand holding his own.

“I’ll wait.”

His friend was waiting now, listening for footsteps, trusting him to come.

Melos lifted his head.

A little water flowed between nearby stones. He drank, pushed himself to his knees, and stood.

He began to run again.

The sun was sinking when he reached the city. He forced his way through the crowd.

His friend stood with his wrists tied. Above him, the executioner raised his sword.

“Wait!”

Melos fell at his friend’s feet.

“I’m here.”

The sword stopped.

His friend bent over him, laughing and crying. Melos reached up and held him tightly.

“I nearly gave up,” he whispered.

“But you came.”

The ruler stared at their joined hands. Then he ordered the guards to release them both.

“I believed every man would save himself,” he said.

Neither friend let go.

For the first time, the ruler spoke without giving an order.

“Could you teach me to trust?”
