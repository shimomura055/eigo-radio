# Before Sunset

“You kill people without a fair trial,” Melos said. “A man only has to frighten you, and you order his death.”

The shepherd stood before the ruler with dust on his sandals. Around him, the guards went still. No one spoke to the ruler like that.

“Then you shall die as well,” the ruler replied.

Melos felt his stomach tighten.

“Give me three days first. My sister is getting married. Let me attend her wedding, and I will return.”

The ruler laughed. “Return to your own execution?”

“My friend will stay here in my place.”

His friend stepped forward.

At last, the ruler agreed. “Before sunset on the third day. If you are late, your friend dies.”

His friend took Melos’s hand. His fingers were warm and steady.

“Go,” he said. “I’ll wait.”

At the wedding, Melos smiled when his sister smiled. He watched her join hands with her husband, but whenever a shadow moved across the floor, he looked toward the door.

Before dawn on the third day, he began his journey back.

He reached the river and stopped. Rain had turned it into a roaring brown flood. The bridge was gone.

For a moment, he searched the empty banks for another crossing. Then he stepped into the water.

The current lifted him off his feet. He fought toward the far bank, swallowed muddy water, and caught a branch. He pulled himself ashore, shaking.

He ran on.

Beyond the river, attackers blocked the road. One grabbed his arm; another swung a stick. Melos struck out, broke free, and stumbled past them. Behind him, they shouted. He did not look back.

By afternoon, his legs would barely carry him.

He fell beside the road. The city was still far away.

*I cannot do it.*

He pressed his face against the earth. He could stay here. No one would find him before sunset. His friend would die, and Melos would live.

The thought brought a terrible moment of relief.

Then he remembered the hand that had held his.

“I’ll wait.”

His friend was waiting even now, listening for footsteps. He had trusted Melos with his life.

Melos lifted his head.

A little water flowed between the stones nearby. He drank, pushed himself to his knees, and stood.

Then he ran again.

The sun was sinking when he reached the city. He forced his way through the crowd toward the place of execution.

His friend stood with his wrists tied. The executioner raised his sword.

“Wait!”

Melos fell at his friend’s feet.

“I’m here.”

The sword stopped.

His friend bent over him, laughing and crying. Melos held him tightly.

“I nearly gave up,” he whispered.

“But you came.”

The ruler stared at their joined hands. Then he ordered the guards to release them both.

“I believed every man would save himself,” he said.

Neither friend let go.

For the first time, the ruler spoke without giving an order.

“Could you teach me to trust?”
