# Before the Last Light

“You kill men because you fear them,” Melos told the ruler. “You do not even let them speak.”

The guards seized the young shepherd. The ruler sentenced him to death.

“Give me three days,” Melos said. “My sister is getting married. Let me see her wedding, and I will return.”

The ruler smiled. “Leave your friend here. If you are late, he dies instead.”

Melos turned to his friend, but before he could speak, his friend held out his hands for the chains.

“Go,” he said. “I know you’ll come back.”

At the wedding, Melos watched his sister laugh. He tried to remember the sound. Before dawn on the third day, he started toward the city.

Then he reached the river.

Rain had swept away the bridge. Brown water rushed between the broken stones. Melos stepped back, ran, and threw himself into it.

The river rolled him under. He fought toward daylight, caught a branch, and dragged himself onto the far bank.

Farther along the road, three men blocked his path. One raised a knife.

Melos struck him with his walking stick and ran. Behind him came angry shouts. Ahead, the road climbed into the hills.

By afternoon, his feet were bleeding. He fell beside a rock and could not make himself rise.

He could stay here. Someone might find him. He could go home.

His friend would die.

For one terrible moment, that seemed easier than taking another step.

Then he remembered those hands held out for the chains. His friend had not even asked him to promise again.

Water dripped from a crack in the rock. Melos drank, pushed himself onto his knees, and stood.

One step. Then another.

At the city gates, he heard the execution bell.

He ran toward the square. The sword was already raised.

“Stop!” he cried. “I am here!”

His friend turned. Melos fell against him.

“I nearly abandoned you,” Melos whispered.

“I nearly stopped believing,” his friend answered.

They held each other while the last sunlight touched the walls.

The ruler stared at them.

“Lower the sword,” he said at last. “Free them both.”

Then, more quietly, he asked, “How does a man earn such trust?”

For the first time, he waited for an answer instead of ordering one.
