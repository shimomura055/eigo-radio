# Before the Last Light

“You kill men because you fear them,” Melos said. “You won’t even let them speak.”

For a moment, the hall was silent. Then the guards seized the young shepherd, and the ruler sentenced him to death.

“Give me three days. My sister is getting married. Let me see her wedding, and I will return.”

The ruler smiled. “Your friend stays here. If you are late, he dies instead.”

Melos turned toward his friend. Before he could speak, his friend held out his hands for the chains.

“Go. I know you’ll come back.”

At the wedding, his sister’s laughter rose above the voices around her. Melos listened, trying to fix that sound in his memory.

Before dawn on the third day, he set out for the city.

At the river, he stopped. Rain had swept away the bridge. Brown water crashed through the broken stones. He stepped back, ran, and threw himself into the flood.

The current dragged him under. He struggled toward the light, caught a branch, and pulled himself onto the far bank, gasping.

Farther along the road, three men blocked his way. One raised a knife. Melos struck him with his walking stick and ran. Their angry shouts followed him as the road climbed into the hills.

By afternoon, his feet were bleeding. He fell beside a rock. His legs would not lift him.

He could stay. Someone might find him. He could go home.

His friend would die.

For one terrible moment, that seemed easier than standing.

Then he saw, in his mind, those hands held out for the chains. His friend had not asked for another promise.

Water dripped from a crack in the rock. Melos drank, pushed himself onto his knees, and stood.

One step. Another.

At the city gates, the execution bell rang.

Melos ran toward the square. The sword was already raised.

“Stop! I am here!”

His friend turned. Melos fell against him.

“I nearly abandoned you,” Melos whispered.

“I nearly stopped believing,” his friend answered.

They held each other as the last sunlight touched the walls.

The ruler stared. At last, he spoke.

“Lower the sword. Free them both.”

Then his voice grew quieter.

“How does a man earn such trust?”

For the first time, he waited for an answer instead of ordering one.
