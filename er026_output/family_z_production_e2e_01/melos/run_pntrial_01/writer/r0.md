# Before Sunset

His friend held out his hands before the guards asked.

That was what Melos would remember: those steady hands, waiting for the rope.

Only an hour earlier, Melos had stood before the ruler and shouted, “You kill people because you fear them. You never even let them answer!”

The ruler had sentenced the shepherd to death.

Melos had asked for three days to attend his sister’s wedding. Now his friend would remain as a prisoner in his place.

“Return before sunset on the third day,” the ruler said. “Or he dies instead.”

“I’ll return,” Melos promised.

His friend nodded.

At the wedding, Melos smiled when his sister smiled. He ate almost nothing. Before dawn, he began the journey back.

By midday, he reached the river.

The bridge was gone.

Brown water rushed between the broken posts, carrying branches downstream. Melos ran along the bank, searching for a crossing. There was none.

He stepped into the water.

The current knocked him sideways. He swallowed muddy water and reached for a branch that broke in his hand. At last, his fingers caught a root on the opposite bank.

He pulled himself onto the mud, coughing.

Farther along the road, men with sticks blocked his way.

“Your money.”

“I have none.”

They came at him anyway. He pushed one aside and ran, but a stick struck his shoulder. Pain spread down his arm.

By late afternoon, he could barely walk.

He fell beside the road.

For a while, he watched an ant climb over his finger.

*You have tried,* he thought. *No one could ask for more.*

His village was behind him. He could return there. Hide. Live.

His friend would die.

Melos closed his eyes.

Then he saw those hands again, held out willingly.

Nearby, water trickled over stones. He dragged himself towards it and drank. He washed his face.

His legs still shook when he stood.

He ran anyway.

At the city gate, people were hurrying towards the square. Above their heads, Melos saw a wooden platform—and his friend beneath a hanging rope.

“Stop!”

His voice disappeared in the crowd.

He forced his way forward.

“I’m here!”

A guard’s hand stopped at the knot.

Melos fell against his friend.

“I almost gave up,” he whispered.

His friend gripped his injured shoulder, then quickly loosened his hold.

“But you came.”

The ruler stared at them.

At last, he ordered the guards to remove the rope.

“I thought every man would save himself,” he said.

Neither friend answered.

The ruler stepped down from his chair, away from his guards.

“Could you ever trust me?”

For the first time, Melos heard fear in his voice.
