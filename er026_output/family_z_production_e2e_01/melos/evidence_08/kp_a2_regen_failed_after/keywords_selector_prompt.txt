The guards stopped moving as Melos began to speak.

“You give people no fair trial before killing them,” he told the ruler. “If someone scares you, you order his death.”

The shepherd faced the ruler alone in dusty sandals, trying to hide his fear.

The ruler narrowed his eyes. “You will die too.”

His stomach felt tight.

“May I have three days for my sister’s wedding?” he asked. “Then I'll come back.”

Laughing, the ruler asked, “Back to die?”

“My friend can take my place here.”

His friend silently stepped up. The ruler watched both as if looking for a trick.

“Return before sunset on day three,” he finally said. “Your friend dies if you're late.”

His friend’s warm, steady fingers closed around Melos’s hand.

“Leave now. I'll wait for you.”

His sister’s smiles made Melos smile at her wedding. He watched her take her husband’s hands. But he looked at the door whenever a shadow crossed the floor. Time moved on.

He left for the city before dawn on day three.

He stopped walking at the river.

There was no bridge left.

Brown water rushed through the crossing with broken branches. Rain had flooded the river. He searched the banks but found no other crossing.

Melos took a step into the river.

The current knocked him down, filling his mouth with muddy water. He fought towards the far bank, reaching for a passing branch. He caught it, held tight, and pulled himself ashore.

Shaking, he got up and ran on.

Past the river, attackers blocked his way. One grabbed his arm; another swung a stick. Melos hit back, escaped, and stumbled past. They shouted angrily behind him, but he never looked back.

Every step hurt by that afternoon.

His legs suddenly failed him.

On the ground, face down, he could not even stand. The distant city was still beyond reach.

Perhaps he could stay here.

Until sunset, nobody would find him. He would live, although his friend would die.

For one terrible moment, that idea made him feel better.

Then he remembered his friend’s warm fingers holding his hand.

“I'll wait for you.”

Now his friend listened for footsteps as he waited.

Melos raised his head.

A little water ran between nearby stones. He drank, rose to his knees, then stood with shaking legs.

Once more, he started to run.

With the sun sinking, he reached the city, pushing through the crowd.

His friend stood, wrists tied. The executioner lifted his sword.

“Wait, please!”

He fell down at his friend’s feet.

“I've come back!”

The sword moved no further.

Laughing and crying, his friend bent over Melos. Reaching up, Melos held him tightly.

“I almost stopped trying,” he whispered.

“You came back, though.”

Watching their joined hands, the ruler finally ordered the guards to free both men.

“I thought everyone would save himself,” he said.

Their hands stayed together.

For the first time, the ruler’s words were not an order.

“Can you show me how to trust?”
