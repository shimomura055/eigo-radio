# The Last Wish

Wind shook the windows, but a bright fire warmed the room. Mr. White lost at chess to his grown son, Herbert. Mrs. White smiled as she knitted beside them.

An old friend visited them, a retired sergeant-major from India. He showed them a small, dry monkey’s paw.

“The paw gives three wishes,” he said. “But trying to change your fate brings sadness.”

“Did you make any wishes?” Mr. White asked.

“I’ve already used all three wishes.”

The soldier suddenly threw the paw into the fire. Mr. White quickly pulled it out.

“Burn it, or don’t blame me for what happens,” his friend warned.

After their guest left, Herbert joked, “Ask for two hundred pounds, Father! That would pay for the house.”

Mr. White smiled as he raised the paw. “I wish to have two hundred pounds.”

The paw twisted in his hand, and he dropped it with a cry. They watched the floor, but nothing happened.

They laughed about it again over breakfast before Herbert left for work. Mrs. White then cleared the table.

Later, a man from Herbert’s company came to visit.

“Your son died after a machine caught him at work,” the man said.

Mr. White took hold of his wife’s hand.

“The company says it isn’t to blame, but offers two hundred pounds for your loss.”

Mr. White fell off his chair.

A week after the funeral, Mrs. White woke her husband with tears on her face.

“There are still two wishes in the paw! Bring our son back to life!”

He looked away. “You didn’t see him, and I’m afraid of what might return.”

“But he’s our own son!”

Mr. White finally went downstairs and found the paw. His wife followed, and his voice was shaking.

“I wish for Herbert to live again.”

It slipped from his hand as they waited in darkness, listening to the clock.

A quiet knock broke the silence.

“It’s Herbert!” She ran towards the door, pulling free when Mr. White caught her arm.

The knocking became louder as she dragged a chair over. She climbed onto it to reach the high door lock.

Mr. White searched on his knees, feeling across the floorboards. The door shook with each heavy knock.

“Please wait!”

The chair scraped, and the last lock began to slide. Just as she reached to open the door, he grabbed the paw and made his third and last wish.

Suddenly, the terrible knocking ended.

She opened the door, but no one stood outside. Cold wind blew in as her long cry of loss rose into the night.

Across the street, the lamp shone on an empty, silent road.
