# The Last Wish

Wind shook the windows of the lonely country house. By the fire, Mr. White stared at the chessboard.

“You’ve beaten me again.”

Herbert laughed and rested a hand on his father’s shoulder. Mrs. White smiled over her knitting.

Then their old friend arrived, bringing rain and cold air into the room.

The retired sergeant-major had returned from India. Beside the fire, he showed them a small, dried monkey’s paw.

“It grants three wishes,” he said. “But those who interfere with fate find only sorrow. Don’t use it.”

“Have you tried?” Mr. White asked.

The old soldier looked into his glass.

“I’ve had my three.”

He threw the paw into the fire. Mr. White bent quickly and pulled it out.

“Burn it,” his friend said. “And don’t blame me if you keep it.”

After he left, Herbert examined the paw.

“Ask for two hundred pounds, Father. Enough to pay off the house.”

Mr. White laughed and raised it.

“I wish for two hundred pounds.”

The paw twisted in his hand. He cried out and dropped it.

But nothing else happened.

By morning, they could laugh about it. Herbert went to work, and Mrs. White cleared the breakfast things.

Later, a stranger stood at their gate. Mr. White watched him hesitate before approaching the house.

“I’m from the firm where your son works,” he said when they let him in.

“Is something wrong?” Mrs. White asked.

The man lowered his eyes.

“He was caught in the machinery. He is dead.”

Mr. White reached for his wife’s hand.

“The firm accepts no responsibility,” the stranger continued. “But they are offering two hundred pounds.”

Mr. White fell from his chair.

A week after the burial, his wife woke him in the night.

“The paw,” she said. “We have two wishes left.”

“No.”

“Wish him alive again.”

He drew away from her.

“You didn’t see him. I’m afraid of what might come back.”

“He is my son.”

At last, Mr. White went downstairs. In the dark, he raised the paw.

“I wish Herbert alive again.”

His hand fell to his side. The paw slipped to the floor.

They waited in silence.

Then came a slow, quiet knock.

Mrs. White ran towards the door.

“Herbert!”

Mr. White caught her arm, but she pulled free. A chair scraped as she dragged it beneath the high bolt.

The knocking grew louder.

He searched the floor on his knees.

“Wait,” he whispered.

As she drew back the last bolt, he found the paw and made his third and last wish.

The knocking stopped.

The door opened. Cold wind entered, and Mrs. White gave a long cry of loss.

The street lamp opposite shone on a quiet, deserted road.
