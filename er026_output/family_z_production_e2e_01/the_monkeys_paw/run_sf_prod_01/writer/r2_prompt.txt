# The Last Wish

Wind rattled the windows, but the fire kept the room warm. Herbert grinned across the chessboard as Mr. White pushed back his chair. Mrs. White smiled over her knitting.

A knock announced their old friend, a retired sergeant-major, home from India. Beside the fire, he took out a small, dried monkey’s paw.

“Three wishes,” he said. “But it brings sorrow to anyone who interferes with fate.”

“Have you tried it?” Mr. White asked.

“I’ve had my three.”

Suddenly, the old soldier threw it into the fire. Mr. White snatched it out.

“Burn it,” his friend warned. “Don’t blame me if you keep it.”

After their visitor left, Herbert laughed.

“Wish for two hundred pounds, Father. We could pay off the house.”

“Is that all?” Mr. White said with a smile. He raised the paw. “I wish for two hundred pounds.”

It twisted in his hand. He cried out and dropped it.

They stared. Nothing happened.

By breakfast, they were laughing about it. Herbert left for work, and Mrs. White cleared the table.

Later, a stranger from the firm came to the house.

“Your son was caught in the machinery,” he said quietly. “He is dead.”

Mr. White reached for his wife’s hand.

“The firm accepts no responsibility, but it is offering you two hundred pounds.”

Mr. White fell from his chair.

A week after the burial, Mrs. White woke her husband.

“The paw! We still have two wishes.”

He turned away.

“Wish him alive again,” she begged.

“You didn’t see him,” he whispered. “I’m afraid of what might come back.”

“He’s our son!”

At last, Mr. White went downstairs and found the paw.

“I wish Herbert alive again.”

The paw fell from his hand. They waited in the darkness.

Then a soft knock broke the silence.

Mrs. White rushed downstairs. “Herbert!”

Mr. White caught her arm, but she pulled free.

The knocking grew louder. She dragged a chair to the door and climbed up to reach the high bolt.

He dropped to his knees, searching the floor. His fingers swept across the boards.

“Wait!”

The chair scraped. The last bolt began to slide.

His hand closed around the paw. Just as Mrs. White was about to open the door, he made his third and last wish.

The terrible knocking stopped.

She pulled the door open. Cold wind swept into the house, and her long wail of loss rose into the night.

The street lamp opposite shone on a quiet, deserted road.
