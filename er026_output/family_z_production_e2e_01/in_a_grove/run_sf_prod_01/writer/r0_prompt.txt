[Story Seed -- inspired by a real, rights-cleared source, freely reimagined;
this is NOT a summary to reproduce, just raw material]
- The story is a set of seven separate spoken testimonies about one death in a thicket, with no narrator who knows the truth and no verdict: the first four speakers (a woodcutter, a traveling monk, a constable and an old woman) answer a police magistrate about what they saw and know, and the last three (the bandit Tajomaru, the wife Masago and the dead husband Takehiro) each speak about what happened in the thicket. Nobody says which account is true.
- The woodcutter says he found the body that morning in a bamboo and cedar thicket away from the road, stabbed once in the chest, the blood already dry; there was no sword or bow, only a length of rope at the foot of a cedar and a comb, and the ground was trampled as if the man had fought hard before he died; no horse could have come in.
- The monk says he met the dead man the day before at about noon, walking with a woman on a horse (her face hidden by a veil, her robes pink-and-green), the man carrying a sword and a bow with many arrows; he never imagined the man would die, and thinks of how short human life is.
- The constable says he caught Tajomaru, a famous bandit, groaning beside a stone bridge after falling from a horse, carrying the dead man's bow and arrows; he is sure Tajomaru is the killer, says Tajomaru is a notorious chaser of women, and asks the magistrate to find the woman, who has disappeared.
- The old woman says the dead man, Takehiro, a gentle twenty-six-year-old samurai from Wakasa, was her daughter's husband; her daughter Masago, nineteen, is proud and strong-willed and has known no other man; the couple left for Wakasa the day before, and she begs the magistrate to find her daughter and curses the bandit, weeping.
- Tajomaru confesses with pride that he killed the man: a breeze lifted the wife's veil and her face seemed to him like a goddess's; he tricked the husband into the thicket with a story of buried treasure and tied him to a cedar, then brought the wife; she drew a dagger and fought like no woman he had known, but he overpowered her; as he turned to go, she clung to him and cried that one of the two men must die, and that she would go with whichever survived; he untied the husband, fought him fairly and killed him on the twenty-third stroke (no one else had lasted twenty), but the wife had vanished, so he took the sword and bow and left.
- Masago, giving her account at Kiyomizu temple, says that after the bandit had taken her by force and left, the cold, contemptuous look in her bound husband's eyes hurt her more than the blow; she begged him to die with her, took up the dagger when she could not endure the look, and stabbed him when his lips silently said 'Kill me'; then she tried to drown or stab herself, but could not die, and she asks, weeping, what a woman like her should do.
- Takehiro's spirit, speaking through a medium, says the bandit sat down beside his wife and comforted her; he, bound and unable to speak, tried to warn her with his eyes; but she looked up and said 'Take me anywhere', then pointed at him and cried 'Kill that man'; the bandit was shocked, kicked her down and asked him whether to kill her or let her live; she ran into the thicket and the bandit left, cutting one rope; alone, he took up the dagger his wife had dropped and stabbed himself in the chest.
- The story ends with the dead man's last words: the thicket falls quiet, someone creeps up to him on soft feet, an unseen hand quietly draws the dagger out of his chest, blood fills his mouth once more, and he sinks for ever into the darkness; nothing is added after it.

[How to convert it]
Keep the original's frame of seven separate spoken testimonies about one death in a thicket, in the original order (the woodcutter, the monk, the constable, the old woman, Tajomaru, Masago, Takehiro's spirit), the original period (old Japan, a mountain road near Kyoto) and the original's refusal to say what really happened; do not modernize the setting, and do not add a narrator who knows the truth, a detective, a judge's verdict, a new witness, a new piece of evidence or a clear culprit. Write it in natural English at about 400 words as a chain of short testimonies: each witness speaks in the first person ('I') in quoted speech with a plain speech tag that names the speaker (for example '"...," said the woodcutter.'), so that every testimony has its own voice and its own purpose; an outside narrator may only say in a few words who is speaking (for example 'The woodcutter was questioned first.') and never comments on whom to believe; the magistrate's questions are only implied by the answers, so do not write quoted lines for the magistrate. Make each witness sound different through what they care about, not through gimmicks: the woodcutter plain and practical, the monk vague and regretful, the constable eager to blame the bandit, the old woman worried about her daughter, Tajomaru proud and boastful, Masago ashamed and broken, Takehiro bitter and wounded. Use the names Tajomaru, Takehiro and Masago (these three only) and role names for everyone else (the woodcutter, the monk, the constable, the old woman). Preserve the contradiction as the heart of the story: after the four outside witnesses set the scene, all three people who were in the thicket each claim the death for themselves, with their own reasons: Tajomaru says he killed the man fairly in a sword fight, after the woman begged one of the two men to die; Masago says she herself killed her helpless husband with the dagger because of the cold contempt in his eyes; Takehiro says he killed himself with the dagger after his wife asked the bandit to kill him, and that an unseen hand then drew the dagger out of his chest. Keep each of these accounts convincing from the inside, and keep the details that clash (who did what with the dagger, what the wife said and did, how the man died). Do not reconcile the accounts, do not decide which one is true, do not call anyone a liar, and do not write a line that explains the differences. Keep the sexual violence off the page and non-graphic: refer to it with one plain phrase (for example 'he took her by force'), and describe no body or wound in detail. Keep the ending exactly as the original leaves it: it ends on the dead man's last words as he sinks into the darkness, with no verdict, no explanation of who took the dagger, no moral and no comment of your own. Do not reveal in an earlier testimony, in the introductions or anywhere before its turn what a later testimony will say. Compress by trimming the witnesses' small physical details, the bandit's long speeches and the old woman's description of her daughter, and by keeping the first four witnesses brief; do not cut any of the three main accounts, their claims, or the final scene.
Level and length override: write natural upper-intermediate (B1-B2) English, about 400 words (roughly 330-470); ignore any earlier A2 / word-count instruction.
