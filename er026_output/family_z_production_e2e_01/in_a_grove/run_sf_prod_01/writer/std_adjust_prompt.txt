# The Thicket

“One stab in the chest. That’s what I saw,” said the woodcutter. “I found him this morning, in a thicket off the mountain road. The blood was dry. No sword or bow, sir—only rope beneath a cedar, and a comb. The broken bamboo and trampled earth showed a struggle.”

“I passed them yesterday at noon,” said the monk. “He carried a sword and bow. His wife rode beside him, her face veiled. I hardly looked at them. Now I wish I had.”

“I caught Tajomaru, the famous bandit, by the stone bridge,” said the constable. “He’d fallen from a horse, but he still had the dead man’s bow. Find the woman. She’ll tell you what he did.”

“Takehiro was my daughter’s husband,” said the old woman. “A gentle samurai. Masago is proud, yes, but she’s only nineteen. Please find her. Don’t send me home without news.”

“Yes, I killed him,” said Tajomaru. “But listen before you call me a coward! A breeze lifted her veil, and I wanted her. I promised him buried treasure, led him into the thicket and tied him to the cedar. She fought with her dagger, but I took her by force.

“Then she seized my arm. One of us must die, she said. She would go with the survivor. So I freed him. We fought fairly, sword against sword. Twenty-three strokes! No other man had lasted twenty. When he fell, she was gone. I took his weapons and left.”

“I crawled towards my husband after the bandit left,” said Masago at the temple. “He was still tied up. I begged him to die with me. But his eyes held nothing except cold contempt. For me. His own wife.

“I held my dagger. His lips moved: ‘Kill me.’ So I stabbed him. How could I bear those eyes?

“Afterwards, I tried to drown myself. I couldn’t even do that. Where can I go now?”

“I heard the bandit comforting her,” said Takehiro’s spirit through the medium. “My wife said, ‘Take me anywhere.’ Then she pointed at me. ‘Kill that man.’
