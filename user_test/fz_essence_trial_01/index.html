『告げ口心臓』Advanced 語り口改善Trial 比較試聴

状態: Trial(Production採用ではありません)。公開済みの『告げ口心臓』ページ・音声は変更していません。ユーザーNGだった現行Advanced(三人称)と、語り口を原作どおり一人称で保つ上流改善を適用したTrial Advanced(一人称、1本)を並べました。聴いていただきたい観点: (1)一人称の語り手が自分の冷静さを聞き手に語りかける口調が維持されているか (2)異常な心理・自信過剰が伝わるか (3)緊張の積み重ね・心臓音・自白の展開 (4)聞きやすさ・テンポ。

A. 現行Advanced(三人称・ユーザーNGの版)

本文を見る

He was not mad, the old man’s housemate insisted. Only nervous. His illness had sharpened his senses, especially his hearing. He heard everything in heaven and earth. Surely that proved he was sane. His listener should judge how calmly he told the story.

He loved the old man, who had never hurt him. He wanted no money. But that pale blue eye, covered by a thin film like a vulture’s, made his blood turn cold. He would kill the old man and close it forever.

For seven nights, at midnight, he eased open the bedroom door. Slowly, he slipped his head inside with a covered lantern. He let one thin ray fall across the eye.

Always closed.

He could do nothing. He hated the eye, not the man. Each morning, he greeted his housemate warmly.

On the eighth night, his thumb slipped on the lantern’s metal fastening.

“Who’s there?” the old man cried.

For an hour, he stood perfectly still while the old man sat upright, listening. From the bed came a small groan of terror.

At last, he opened the lantern a little. The narrow beam struck the eye.

Wide open.

Then he heard it: a low, quick beating, like a watch wrapped in cotton. The old man’s heart. Faster it beat, and louder. What if a neighbour heard?

He rushed forward with a yell. The old man shrieked once. He dragged him to the floor and pulled the heavy bed over him. For a while, the beating continued.

Then silence.

He hid the body beneath three floorboards and carefully fitted them back. No mark remained.

At four in the morning came a knock. Three police officers stood outside; a neighbour had heard a shriek.

He smiled and welcomed them. The cry had been his own, he explained—a bad dream. The old man was away in the country.

He showed them the house, then brought chairs into the bedroom. Calm and confident, he sat directly above the hidden body. The officers settled down and chatted.

A ringing troubled his ears. He spoke louder, but now the sound was clear: low, quick, like a watch wrapped in cotton.

He stood and paced. He scraped his chair across the boards. Still the beating grew louder.

The officers kept smiling and talking. They seemed to hear nothing.

But they knew. They must know. Their pleasant smiles were mocking him. How could they sit there? How could he bear another second?

“Stop pretending!” he screamed. “I did it! Tear up the planks! Here, beneath my feet! It is the beating of his hideous heart!”

B. Trial Advanced(一人称・語り口改善版)

本文を見る

You call me mad? I am perfectly sane. My illness sharpened my senses; it did not weaken my mind. I hear everything in heaven and earth. Listen, and judge how calmly I tell you what happened.

The old man shared my house. I loved him; he had never hurt me, and I wanted none of his money. Only his eye troubled me: pale blue, with a cloudy film. A vulture’s eye. You understand? That eye had to go.

For seven nights, at midnight, I opened his bedroom door so slowly that it made no sound. Then I let a thin ray from my lantern touch his face. Always, the eye was closed. What could I do? I hated the eye, not the man.

On the eighth night, I smiled at my own patience. Then my thumb slipped against the lantern fastening.

The old man sat up. “Who’s there?”

I stood still for an hour. Imagine such control! He remained awake, listening. Once, he gave a small groan of terror. I knew that sound from my own sleepless nights.

At last, I opened the lantern a little. The ray fell upon the eye.

Wide open.

Then I heard his heart—a low, quick beat, like a watch wrapped in cotton. It grew faster. Louder. Surely a neighbour would hear!

I rushed in. He shrieked once before I dragged him down and pulled the heavy bed over him. Soon the beating stopped. I checked. He was dead.

I hid his body beneath three floorboards and replaced them perfectly. No stain, no mark. Would a madman have worked so neatly?

At four, three police officers knocked. A neighbour had heard a shriek. I welcomed them. The cry was mine, I explained: a bad dream. The old man was away in the country.

I showed them every room, then brought chairs into his bedroom. My own chair stood directly above the body. You see how little I feared?

They sat chatting. Soon my ears began to ring. Beneath their voices came a low, quick beating.

I talked louder. I stood and paced, scraping my chair across the boards. Still it grew louder. Louder!

The officers went on smiling and talking. Not one seemed to hear. Yet they must hear! They knew. Their easy smiles were mocking me. How could you expect me to sit there and bear it?

“I did it!” I screamed. “Tear up the planks! Here, beneath my chair! It is the beating of his hideous heart!”
