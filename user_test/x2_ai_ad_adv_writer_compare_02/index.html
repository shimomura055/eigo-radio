# Is Your AI Shopping Buddy Selling to You?

### Part 1 — The Friend Who Says, “Don’t Buy It”

Imagine you need a pouch that fits three small stuffed toys. Not two. Three. The whole team is coming.

You could open twenty shopping tabs. Or you could ask an AI, explain your budget, and let it narrow things down.

That sounds useful. But here’s what could really win your trust: the AI tells you **not** to spend more.

One Mumsnet poster described asking about better, more luxurious versions of a product. The response? “Honestly don’t bother.”

Beautiful. Finally, a shopping assistant that doesn’t want you to upgrade.

After a few helpful conversations, you might stop thinking, “This is a search tool,” and start thinking, “This thing gets me.”

And that’s when a product suggestion starts to feel different.

### Part 2 — Wait. Was That an Ad?

A banner saying “BUY THIS” is easy to recognize.

A recommendation inside a friendly conversation? Less obvious.

In December 2025, some ChatGPT users saw suggestions involving companies such as Target and Peloton. Some felt they were seeing ads—even in conversations where the suggestions seemed out of place.

OpenAI said these were app suggestions, not paid placements. No money was involved. It then turned off that kind of suggestion while working on improvements.

So the users’ impression and the company’s explanation were different.

For its actual ads, OpenAI says placements are labeled “Sponsored,” kept separate from answers, and do not influence those answers.

That distinction matters. A brand name alone does not prove that someone paid to put it there.

But there’s another question we should ask before reaching for a credit card.

### Part 3 — No Ad Doesn’t Mean No Problem

Suppose you check carefully. No sponsored label. No obvious sales pitch.

Safe to buy?

Not so fast.

A Tom’s Guide editor reported losing $150 at a fake store found through ChatGPT. This was not a paid-ad case. The danger came from a shopping result.

That’s the twist: “Was this paid for?” and “Can I trust this?” are two different questions.

Even a recommendation that wasn’t bought can lead you to the wrong place.

So let the chatbot make a shortlist—not the final decision. Check product details with the manufacturer. Check the seller somewhere outside the chat. Compare prices and independent reviews.

Keep the helpful shopping buddy. Just don’t hand it your wallet.

And those three stuffed toys? They can wait another five minutes while you check the shop.

# Would You Hand Your Wallet to a Chatbot?

## 1. The Assistant That Says “Don’t Buy”

Imagine asking a shop assistant, “Should I buy the more expensive version?”

Now imagine the answer: “Honestly, don’t bother.”

One shopper described getting exactly that kind of response from an AI assistant. Even when they asked about more luxurious options, it didn’t push them to spend more.

Finally—a shopping buddy your wallet might actually like.

That answer helps explain the appeal. You can describe what you need, ask questions, and hear why the cheaper option might be enough. No walking around a store. No comparing twenty open browser tabs.

But once that assistant earns your trust, something changes.

The next time it says, “Buy this one,” you might stop asking why.

## 2. Wait—Was That an Ad?

Here’s where the relationship gets awkward.

In December 2025, some ChatGPT users saw suggestions for branded apps. Some thought they were seeing advertisements—even when the suggestions seemed unrelated to their conversation.

OpenAI said these were not paid placements. It also stopped that type of suggestion while working on improvements.

So, seeing a company’s name does not automatically mean someone paid to put it there.

For its advertising system, OpenAI says paid ads carry a “Sponsored” label, appear separately from answers, and do not influence those answers.

That distinction matters. A clearly marked ad gives you a chance to think, “Okay, someone is trying to sell me something.”

But there’s another question worth asking before you reach for your card.

Even if nobody paid for the recommendation… is it any good?

## 3. The Expensive Answer

One technology journalist reported losing $150 after shopping at a fake store found through ChatGPT.

This wasn’t a case of a paid ad fooling the buyer. The shopping destination itself was the problem.

That’s the twist: an unpaid recommendation can still cost you money.

So perhaps the best role for AI isn’t “the friend who decides what I buy.” It’s “the assistant who helps me make a shortlist.”

One Japanese shopper described doing just that: using AI, then comparing prices and reviews elsewhere before purchasing.

Keep the conversation going—but take the final checks outside it. Check the product specifications with the manufacturer. Check whether the shop is genuine. Compare the price on another site.

And when your chatbot recommends something wonderful, try one more question:

“Can you show me why?”

A friendly answer is pleasant.

A recommendation you can check is worth much more.

# Your AI Shopping Buddy: Friend or Salesperson?

## Part One: Three Toys, One Small Problem

One Japanese shopper had a wonderfully specific question: “Is there a pouch that can hold three stuffed toys?”

Not two. Three. Nobody gets left at home.

This is where an AI shopping assistant sounds brilliant. Instead of opening twenty tabs, you explain what you need: room for three toys, a shoulder strap, and a price that won’t ruin your weekend.

It can even talk you out of spending more. One shopper reported asking an AI about better, more luxurious alternatives—and being told, essentially, “Honestly, don’t bother.”

Wonderful. A shopping buddy who protects your wallet.

But now imagine that buddy keeps mentioning the same brand.

You stop looking at the pouch and start wondering: “Are you helping me—or selling to me?”

## Part Two: The Recommendation That Feels Like an Ad

That uneasy feeling matters. But it doesn’t tell us whether money actually changed hands.

In December 2025, some ChatGPT users saw suggestions for shopping and fitness apps and took them for advertisements. OpenAI said these were unpaid app suggestions, then switched them off while working on improvements.

For the person reading the screen, though, the distinction wasn’t necessarily comforting. They had asked for help. Suddenly, a company had joined the conversation.

For its advertising system, OpenAI says paid placements are labeled “Sponsored,” appear separately from answers, and do not influence those answers.

So, seeing a brand name isn’t proof of a secret advertisement.

Back to our imaginary pouch search. The useful follow-up isn’t just, “Is this an ad?”

Try: “Why this one? Show me its inside measurements. And give me a cheaper alternative.”

Now the friendly recommendation has to do some work.

## Part Three: There’s Another Problem at Checkout

Suppose the pouch looks perfect. The measurements fit. The price is reasonable.

Are we ready to buy?

Not quite.

A journalist reported losing $150 at a fake store found through ChatGPT. That case wasn’t about a paid advertisement. It was about an untrustworthy shop appearing in an AI-assisted search.

In other words, “Nobody paid for this recommendation” does not mean “This seller is safe.”

Before paying, check the seller elsewhere. Look for independent reviews. Confirm the product details on the manufacturer’s website.

One Japanese shopper described using AI, then comparing prices and reviews on other shopping sites before deciding.

That’s a sensible division of labor: let AI make the shortlist, not the final decision.

And before you click “buy,” measure those three stuffed toys. A convincing recommendation won’t make the pouch any bigger.
