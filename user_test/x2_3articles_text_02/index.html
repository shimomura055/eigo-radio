②恋愛は、数学の式で長続きするか予測できる？

Standard: (境界NGのため未抽出)(本文391語 = Part別 111 / 159 / 121)

Part 1 (111語)

Your phone lights up. A message from your partner.

“OK.”

That is all. No heart. No smile.

Are they angry? Are they busy? Or are they simply buying milk?

You read those two letters five times. Suddenly, your love life feels like a difficult math test.

But wait. Could math actually help?

Some researchers think it can help us understand relationships. Not by finding your perfect partner. Not by giving your love a score out of 100.

They are asking a different question: When life gets hard, why do some couples stay close while others move apart?

The interesting part may not be that first “OK.” It may be what happens next.

Part 2 (159語)

You feel worried. You send another message.

“Are you angry?”

No answer. You send three more.

Your partner feels pushed and stops replying.

This is an imagined example. But notice the pattern. Your feelings change your actions. Your actions may then change your partner’s feelings.

Researchers in France are building math models of love. A model is a simple way to show how something works. Their models include how feelings fade and how people react to each other.

The aim is not to predict whom you will love. It is to understand why some couples stay stable under stress.

And numbers are not the only useful clues.

In one study, a computer used married couples’ voices to predict whether their relationships would get better or worse. It was right about 79 percent of the time in that study.

Try saying, “You’re home,” with a warm voice.

Now say it with an angry voice.

Same words. Very different welcome.

Part 3 (121語)

So, should you ask a computer whether to break up?

Not so fast.

That 79 percent does not mean a computer knows your future. A result from one study is not a promise about your relationship.

A math model is also simpler than real life. It cannot include every bad day, kind act, or private joke.

Still, this research offers an interesting way to look at love: watch what happens between two people, not just inside one person’s heart.

Back to that “OK.”

Instead of guessing, you could ask, “Are you upset, or just busy?”

Perhaps the answer is, “Sorry. Buying milk.”

Math cannot write your love story. But it may help you notice the pattern—and choose your next line.

Advanced: (境界NGのため未抽出)(本文391語 = Part別 108 / 148 / 135)

Part 1 (108語)

“I’m fine,” said with a smile.

“I’m fine,” said through clenched teeth.

Same words. Very different dinner.

Picture a couple sitting across from each other. The food is getting cold. One person asks what’s wrong. The other says, “Nothing.”

Could a computer hear trouble before either person admits it?

In one study, researchers used couples’ tone of voice to predict whether their relationships would improve or worsen. The algorithm reached about 79 percent accuracy.

That sounds like a weather forecast for love.

But before you ask your phone, “Should we break up?” there is a bigger question: What can mathematics actually tell us about a relationship?

Part 2 (148語)

Let’s return to that dinner.

One partner wants to talk now. The other needs time to calm down.

“Why won’t you answer?”

“Because I need a minute!”

One pushes harder. The other pulls away.

To explore patterns like these, researchers build mathematical models. These describe how each person’s feelings change in response to the other person.

Some models include a delay: feelings do not always respond immediately.

Within these models, changing that delay can turn a steady relationship into repeated emotional ups and downs.

Think of two people adjusting the shower temperature. One turns it hotter. Before the warm water arrives, the other turns it hotter again. Suddenly, both are jumping away from the spray.

The problem is not simply what each person does. Timing matters too.

But this is a model—not proof that replying late to a message will ruin your love life.

Part 3 (135語)

Here is where the calculator needs a warning label.

A model can look brilliant when tested on the same data used to build it. The harder test is whether it works with new people.

In a separate analysis of relationship predictions, accuracy dropped from 90 percent on the original data to 69 percent on different data.

It is like memorizing a practice test, then opening the real exam.

So perhaps the most useful question is not, “Can an equation tell us whether to stay together?”

It is, “Can it help us notice the pattern we keep repeating?”

Back at dinner, nobody needs to announce, “Our emotional system is unstable.”

Something simpler might help:

“I do want to talk. I just need ten minutes.”

Math can describe a pattern. The next line in the conversation is still yours.

⑦週末の寝坊が、平日の体調に影響する？

Standard: More Sleep, More Tired?(本文389語 = Part別 100 / 144 / 145)

Part 1 (100語)

Saturday morning. Your alarm rings at seven.

“Not today,” you say.

You turn it off. At eleven, you finally get up. More sleep. No work. A perfect plan.

Then Monday comes.

You sit on the train. Your eyes keep closing. You slept longer at the weekend. So why do you feel so tired?

One blogger described this strange problem: “My worst Mondays came after my longest weekend sleeps, not my shortest.”

That is one person’s experience, not proof. But here is an interesting question:

Is sleep only about how long we sleep? Or does when we sleep matter, too?

Part 2 (144語)

Your body has its own clock. It helps you feel awake or sleepy.

But it does not know that Saturday is your day off.

When you go to bed and get up much later, your body clock can move later, too.

Now it is Sunday night. You need to sleep.

You check the time. Eleven. Twelve.

“Come on. I have work tomorrow.”

But your body is not ready.

Then Monday’s alarm rings.

Your alarm says, “Good morning!”

Your body says, “Absolutely not.”

So, is extra sleep bad? No. It can help. But feeling better does not always mean everything has recovered.

In one small study of 30 people, extra sleep after several short nights brought feelings of sleepiness back to normal. But scores on attention tests did not improve.

The lesson is not “sleep less.” It is that one weekend may not fix everything.

Part 3 (145語)

What could a different weekend look like?

Perhaps you get up closer to your usual time. You open the curtains, eat breakfast, and enjoy a slow morning—not necessarily an early, busy one.

One Japanese blogger tried keeping weekday wake-up times on days off, with a short daytime nap. They reported much easier Monday mornings.

That is another personal story, not a promise. But it offers a useful idea: change the pattern, not just the number of hours.

Most importantly, do not cut the sleep you need just to keep an early alarm. Getting enough sleep during the week matters, too.

The goal is not to become a perfect morning person.

It is to stop asking Saturday and Sunday to repair five tired weekdays.

Maybe a better Monday starts before Monday—with a weekend that does not move your mornings quite so far away.

Advanced: (境界NGのため未抽出)(本文393語 = Part別 98 / 181 / 114)

Part 1 (98語)

Saturday. Seven in the morning.

No train to catch. No meeting. You pull the blanket over your head.

“Two more hours. I’ve earned them.”

By Sunday, breakfast has become lunch. Wonderful.

Then Monday arrives.

You’re on the train, reading the same message three times. Wasn’t all that sleep supposed to help?

One blogger described the puzzle like this: “My worst Mondays came after my longest weekend sleeps, not my shortest.”

So, what happened? Did your weekend rest somehow turn against you?

The answer may be less about how much you slept—and more about when.

Part 2 (181語)

Sleep needs to do two things: give you enough rest and fit your body’s daily rhythm.

Think of a small restaurant. It needs enough food, but it also needs regular opening hours. Delivering more food does not automatically fix a confused schedule.

Your body has a schedule, too.

In a small experiment with ten young adults, researchers moved bedtime and waking time two hours later on Friday and Saturday. A signal of their body clock shifted later by about half an hour.

Now return to Sunday night.

The room is dark. Tomorrow’s clothes are ready. You want to sleep.

Your body’s response?

“Already?”

This mismatch between workday and free-day sleep schedules is called social jet lag. No airport required.

But here’s the twist: extra sleep can still help.

In another experiment involving thirty people, recovery sleep brought sleepiness back to normal after sleep restriction. Yet attention-test scores did not improve.

Feeling refreshed and being fully recovered are not always the same thing.

That lovely Sunday morning feeling can be real—without being a complete reset.

Part 3 (114語)

So, should you drag yourself out of bed after too little sleep?

That misses the point.

The goal is not less sleep. It is enough sleep with less weekend change.

Try keeping your weekend wake-up time closer to your weekday routine. Make room for more sleep during the week, rather than asking Saturday to repair everything.

And watch the whole story: not just how Sunday morning feels, but how Sunday night and Monday morning go.

If strong daytime sleepiness continues despite enough sleep, seek medical advice.

Your weekend is not a charging cable. Two long nights cannot promise to erase five short ones.

The best gift for Monday might begin on Tuesday night.

⑳写真は映えなくていい。2秒だけ撮るSNSが人気に？

Standard: Why Would Anyone Watch Your Boring Day?(本文383語 = Part別 117 / 135 / 131)

Part 1 (117語)

You take a photo of your lunch. Rice, an egg, and yesterday’s vegetables.

You look at it.

“No. Nobody needs to see this.”

You close the app.

But what if that very ordinary lunch was exactly what your friends wanted to see?

That is the idea behind Setlog. You can record about two seconds of video each hour and share it with a small group. At the end of the day, the app puts the videos together into one video diary.

No big event needed. No beautiful beach needed.

One blogger joked that his video was full of pictures of a confused old man: himself.

Not quite a movie star. But perhaps that is the point.

Part 2 (135語)

Picture this. You and two old friends now live in different cities.

At lunchtime, you film your rice and egg. One friend films a rainy street. The other shows a desk covered in papers.

“Oh. Still busy today?”

Later, you watch the day’s videos together on a split screen.

You were all in different places. But now you can see what was happening at the same time.

Normally, you might ask, “Anything new?”

And your friend might answer, “Not really.”

Yet there was a whole day inside that “not really.”

Some users say this is what they enjoy: seeing the small things their friends would never mention in a message.

Moving clouds. A train ride home. A tired face.

The videos are not exciting news. They are little signs that say, “I’m here.”

Part 3 (131語)

Still, even a simple app can start to feel like work.

One user told a newspaper that, after a few weeks, posting sometimes felt like something they had to do.

You can almost hear the thought:

“Oh, I haven’t filmed anything. Quick! Find something interesting!”

And suddenly, the old problem is back.

The good news? You can still watch your friends’ videos without posting your own. You do not have to turn every hour into a show.

Maybe that is the real test for Setlog.

Can it help friends feel closer without giving them another job?

Because the best moment may not be a sunset or a wonderful dinner.

It may be your friend watching your lunch and saying:

“Rice and egg? Same here.”

Two boring lunches. One small connection.

Advanced: What If Boring Is the Point?(本文394語 = Part別 109 / 144 / 141)

Part 1 (109語)

Your lunch is a convenience-store rice ball. You lift your phone, look at the screen, and lower it again.

“Nobody needs to see this.”

A beach sunset deserves a post. Tuesday lunch? Probably not.

But what if Tuesday lunch is exactly what your friends want to see?

That is the appeal of Setlog. The app lets friends share roughly two-second videos each hour within a group. It then automatically combines the day’s clips into a video diary.

No big event required. Your rice ball can make the cut.

Because the interesting part might not be your lunch. It might simply be knowing what you are doing.

Part 2 (144語)

At noon, a group’s video might look like this:

One friend unwraps lunch. Another waits for a train. A third points the camera at a dog refusing to walk.

Nothing amazing happens. Yet together, these tiny scenes tell a story: this is where everyone was while the day moved along.

Group videos can place friends’ clips side by side. Instead of seeing only your own afternoon, you get several views of the same hours.

That changes the question from “Is my life interesting?” to “What are we all doing?”

One blogger described sharing days without makeup and moments caught with a silly face. Looking back, those clips made them smile.

The things that seemed too small to post had become things worth remembering.

A polished holiday photo says, “Look where I went.”

These little clips can say, “You were part of my day.”

Part 3 (141語)

But there is a trap hiding inside this relaxed idea.

“I haven’t posted this hour. Quick—find something!”

Suddenly, even being ordinary becomes a task.

One user interviewed by a newspaper said that, after a few weeks, posting sometimes felt like an obligation. That is one person’s experience, but it points to an important question: can an app help us relax if we feel we must keep feeding it?

Setlog does let people watch friends’ videos without posting themselves. You do not have to supply a scene every hour.

Maybe that is the rule worth keeping: share when it adds something, and skip when it does not.

The goal is not to prove that every hour matters.

It is to enjoy a small reminder that your friends are out there, living their ordinary Tuesdays too.

Rice balls and all.
