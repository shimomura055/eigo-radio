## Part 1 Imagine this. You are reading a love story on your phone. Your favorite character is about to say something important. Then the episode ends. “No! Just say you like her!” Next week feels very far away. But what if you could enter the story and speak first? That is the idea behind byUs, an AI story chat service. It came to LINE Manga in Japan in October 2026. You become the main character. You type a message, and an AI version of a manga character answers. Together, you make a new story. So, back to our love story. This time, you type: “I like you.” Now it is his turn. But what do you really want? A sweet answer—or an answer that feels like him? ## Part 2 The exciting part is not just getting an answer. It is feeling that the character knows you. A Korean writer who tried byUs said the AI remembered earlier conversations. It used those details later in the story. That made the chat feel more real. Think about talking to someone who remembers your favorite drink. A small detail can make you smile. But the same review also described a funny problem. The characters were talking in a café. Then the story described the place as an office. Wait. An office? Where did the coffee go? Are we on a date, or do we have a meeting? One strange detail can break the feeling. And a strange place is not the only possible problem. What if the character’s words feel wrong, too? ## Part 3 Let’s return to our imagined love story. Your favorite character is quiet. He finds it hard to talk about his feelings. That is part of why you like him. Now imagine he suddenly sends you ten sweet messages. “You are my whole world!” At first, you smile. Then you think: “Would he really say that?” byUs uses works with the original authors’ permission, according to LINE Manga. But permission to use a character does not promise that every AI reply will feel right. That is the tricky part. You want to change the story. But you may not want to change the person you love inside it. So your phone lights up. His answer is here. Do you want him to say exactly what you hoped? Or would you rather hear something less perfect—and think, “Yes. That sounds like him”?

## Part 1 Imagine this. It is eleven at night. You are reading manga on your phone. Your favorite character walks away from a friend. You want to shout, “Wait! Why did you do that?” Usually, the character cannot hear you. You can only turn the page. But what if you could ask—and get an answer? That is the idea behind byUs, an AI service where readers can chat with comic characters. You type a message. The character replies. You ask another question. The story moves forward with you inside it. Now your usual bedtime problem feels a little different. It is not, “Just one more page.” It is, “Just one more message. I need to know what happens!” ## Part 2 But a reply alone is not the exciting part. Imagine telling a character, “I get nervous when I meet new people.” Later, the character brings that up again. You might think, “Wait. You remember?” One reporter described something like this during a test: a character mentioned information from earlier in their chat. That does not mean it will always remember. Still, a small moment like that can make a chat feel more personal. And readers do not have to follow only the original story. They can also create and share fan stories with familiar characters in new situations. One report described a fan story where a character became a spy. Same character. Very different problems. Forget the homework. Someone has a secret! You are no longer only asking, “What happens next?” You are also asking, “What if we changed this?” ## Part 3 That brings us back to your phone at eleven at night. Then eleven thirty. Then midnight. Imagine the character asks, “Can I tell you one more thing?” Would you close the app? A printed manga has a last page. A chat can make the next moment feel one message away. This does not mean the character is a real person. It is AI creating replies. But your wish to hear the next answer can still feel real. So perhaps the biggest change is not that manga characters can answer us. It is that we now have a new job. We help shape the story. And we decide when to stop. Maybe tonight’s best final message is simple: “Tell me tomorrow. I need some sleep.”

Instead of watching the romance from outside, you step into the main character’s place and talk to the characters yourself. In fan-created stories, you can set up new situations and relationships, and share your stories with other users. The characters are used with their original creators’ permission. So, finally, you can stop shouting at the page. You have the keyboard. ## Part 2 And sometimes, the conversation can feel surprisingly convincing. A Korean journalist testing byUs reported that it remembered earlier conversations and used them later in the story. Think about why that matters. A character saying, “How was your day?” is pleasant. Remembering something you mentioned earlier can make it feel as though they were actually listening. But the same review described a problem: a conversation taking place in a café suddenly had an office as its setting. Imagine looking up from your imaginary coffee. Wait. Why is there a printer beside our table? That mistake is funny because it is obvious. You know the scene has gone wrong. But another reaction points to something trickier. One Japanese blogger described feeling irritated by the character’s smooth talk rather than won over. Sometimes, getting a charming reply isn’t the same as getting the reply you wanted. ## Part 3 Go back to that streetlight. Imagine your favorite character is shy. Saying hello is practically an athletic event for him. Now imagine the AI gives him a flawless romantic speech. You finally got your confession. But would *he* really say that? That is the interesting tension here. You can change the situation, start the conversation, and push the romance forward. Yet push too far, and you might lose the awkward, frustrating person you liked in the first place. The café turning into an office is easy to notice. A beloved character turning into someone who always says exactly what you want? That might take longer. Your finger is hovering over “Send.” Do you want him to confess? Or do you want him to be himself?

## Part 1 You have three small toy bears. You want to carry them in one bag. Easy? Not quite. One bag is too small. Another costs too much. You have twenty shopping pages open. The bears are still on your desk. So you ask ChatGPT: “Can you help me find a bag for these three toys?” Imagine it asks about their size and your budget. Soon, you have three bags to compare instead of twenty. You ask, “Should I buy the expensive one?” It answers, “You may not need it. The cheaper one could be enough.” Wonderful. A shopping friend who helps you spend *less*. You look at the bears. “Good news. We may have money left for snacks.” ## Part 2 But now imagine a shopping ad appears near the answer. Wait. Did the AI choose that bag because it fits your bears? Or because someone wants to sell it? The bag has not changed. But your feeling has. An ad on a street poster is easy to understand. It wants your money. A chatbot feels different. It listens. It remembers what you said. It sounds helpful. That can make a sales message feel like advice from a friend. The company behind ChatGPT says its ads have a “Sponsored” label and stay separate from its answers. “Sponsored” means it is a paid ad. Also, a brand name inside an answer does not prove it is an ad. Still, you want one thing to be clear: “Are you helping me choose, or asking me to buy?” ## Part 3 And ads are not the only thing to check. One writer reported losing $150 at a fake online store found through ChatGPT. That report was about a search result, not a paid ad. So even an answer without an ad needs a second look. Back to your bears. Before buying, measure them. Check the bag’s size on the store’s own page. Look at reviews elsewhere. Make sure the shop is real. One Japanese shopper described using AI to find choices, then comparing prices and reviews on shopping sites before buying. That sounds like a useful job for AI: make the list shorter, not make the final decision. You close the extra pages. You check the measurements. The bears fit. AI helped with the shopping. But you kept control of the money. And yes—there is still money for snacks.

## Part 1 You need tissues. You ask your voice assistant to buy some. Easy, right? One person shared this shopping story online. The assistant said the price was 1,100 yen. But when the package arrived, there were no tissues inside. There was a book. Something to read, yes. Something to blow your nose with? Please, no. That mistake is easy to notice. You wanted tissues. Do you still stop and check? ## Part 2 Imagine you want headphones for your train ride to work. You tell an AI, “I want something cheap. And I don’t want to hear the train.” It gives you three choices and explains each one. Wonderful. No more opening twenty websites. Then you see an ad below the answer. Wait. Is that product there because it fits your needs—or because someone paid? OpenAI says ads in ChatGPT are labeled and shown below the answer, separate from it. It also says a paid ad does not mean the company recommends the product. That difference matters. But can we always notice when someone is trying to sell us something? A news report described a study where people chose digital books with AI help. In one test, the AI actively tried to sell certain books. Only about 18 percent of people could tell which products it was trying to sell. This was a research test, not a test of every shopping AI. Still, it raises a useful question: when advice sounds friendly, do we check it less? ## Part 3 Let’s return to your headphones. The AI has saved you time. Good. Now give yourself one small job before buying. Open the seller’s website. Is the price the same? Is delivery extra? Can you return them? Then check one review from another place. Look for someone who uses them on a noisy train, just like you. And if you see “sponsored,” remember: someone paid to put that ad there. An ad is not always bad. A friendly answer is not always right. Let AI help you make a short list. But keep the final choice for yourself. After all, you want to listen to music on the train—not read a book about headphones.

## Part 1 One Japanese shopper had a wonderfully specific question: “Is there a pouch that can hold three stuffed toys?” Not two. Three. Nobody gets left at home. This is where an AI shopping assistant sounds brilliant. Instead of opening twenty tabs, you explain what you need: room for three toys, a shoulder strap, and a price that won’t ruin your weekend. It can even talk you out of spending more. One shopper reported asking an AI about better, more luxurious alternatives—and being told, essentially, “Honestly, don’t bother.” Wonderful. A shopping buddy who protects your wallet. But now imagine that buddy keeps mentioning the same brand. You stop looking at the pouch and start wondering: “Are you helping me—or selling to me?” ## Part 2 That uneasy feeling matters. But it doesn’t tell us whether money actually changed hands. In December 2025, some ChatGPT users saw suggestions for shopping and fitness apps and took them for advertisements. OpenAI said these were unpaid app suggestions, then switched them off while working on improvements. For the person reading the screen, though, the distinction wasn’t necessarily comforting. They had asked for help. Suddenly, a company had joined the conversation. For its advertising system, OpenAI says paid placements are labeled “Sponsored,” appear separately from answers, and do not influence those answers. So, seeing a brand name isn’t proof of a secret advertisement. Back to our imaginary pouch search. The useful follow-up isn’t just, “Is this an ad?” Try: “Why this one? Show me its inside measurements. And give me a cheaper alternative.” Now the friendly recommendation has to do some work. ## Part 3 Suppose the pouch looks perfect. The measurements fit. The price is reasonable. Are we ready to buy? Not quite. A journalist reported losing $150 at a fake store found through ChatGPT. That case wasn’t about a paid advertisement. It was about an untrustworthy shop appearing in an AI-assisted search. In other words, “Nobody paid for this recommendation” does not mean “This seller is safe.” Before paying, check the seller elsewhere. Look for independent reviews. Confirm the product details on the manufacturer’s website. One Japanese shopper described using AI, then comparing prices and reviews on other shopping sites before deciding. That’s a sensible division of labor: let AI make the shortlist, not the final decision. And before you click “buy,” measure those three stuffed toys. A convincing recommendation won’t make the pouch any bigger.

## Part 1 Here’s a shopping story from a Japanese blog. A shopper asks a voice assistant to buy tissues. The assistant announces the price: 1,100 yen. Then the package arrives. Inside? A book. Technically, yes, more paper. But not what you want when your nose is running. It’s one person’s story, not a test of every assistant. Still, it gives us a useful starting point: sometimes, a shopping mistake is easy to spot. But imagine the package contains exactly the headphones you expected. They work. They look good. Could something still be wrong? Perhaps. Because there’s another question besides “Did the AI understand me?” It’s this: “Why did it recommend that particular product?” ## Part 2 Imagine telling an AI, “I need headphones for a crowded train. My budget is 10,000 yen, and my ears get sore.” Instead of twenty browser tabs, you get three choices, with simple explanations. Wonderful. Your evening is saved. Now imagine a paid promotion appearing nearby. Does your brain keep the recommendation and the advertisement in separate boxes—or quietly connect them? OpenAI says its ads are labeled as sponsored and displayed below, visually separate from, ChatGPT’s answers. It also says advertisers cannot change or rank those answers. That distinction matters. But a separate experiment shows why persuasive recommendations deserve attention. A Japanese news report described a study where people chose e-books and an AI actively promoted selected titles. In that persuasion group, fewer than one in five correctly identified the promoted book. That is not a measurement of everyday ChatGPT advertising. But it raises an uncomfortable possibility: a sales pitch can sound remarkably like helpful advice. ## Part 3 So, should we stop asking AI for shopping help? Not necessarily. Making a shortlist can be genuinely useful. The trick is to add one small pause before paying. For those headphones, open the seller’s page. Check the exact model number. Is noise cancellation actually included? Is the price still 9,800 yen? Can you return them after trying them on? Look for sponsorship labels, too. A paid placement is not automatically a bad product—but it isn’t proof that the product suits you. Think of AI as someone helping you browse the shelves, not someone who has already tested everything in your basket. Let it save you twenty tabs. Just spend two minutes checking what will actually arrive. Especially if you ordered tissues.
