走れメロス(Melos) / 新Advanced(A2)

約181秒

Before the Last Light

Melos shouted across the hall. “You’re afraid of men, so you kill them. You don’t even allow them to speak.”

Nobody spoke as guards grabbed the young shepherd. The ruler ordered his death.

“Give me three days to see my sister get married, and I’ll come back.”

With a smile, the ruler said, “Your friend stays here. Return late, and he’ll die in your place.”

Melos faced his friend. Before he could speak, his friend offered both hands for the chains.

“Go now, because I’m sure you’ll come back.”

At the wedding, he heard his sister laughing above the other voices. He wanted to remember that exact sound, so he listened carefully.

On the third day, he started back toward the city before dawn.

The bridge was missing, and rain had made the water brown and wild. Melos stepped back, ran forward, and jumped into the flood.

The water pulled him down, but he struggled toward the light. He caught a branch and pulled himself onto the opposite bank.

Later, three men blocked his way, and a knife shone. Melos hit its owner with his walking stick. He raced uphill, hearing their shouts behind him.

By afternoon, each step hurt, and blood covered his feet. He fell beside a rock and tried to get up. His legs would not move.

He could wait here for someone to find him. Or perhaps he could go back home.

That would mean his friend’s death.

For a terrible moment, getting up seemed harder than that.

He remembered his friend’s hands, ready for the chains. His friend had trusted him without needing another promise.

Water dripped through a crack in the rock. After drinking, Melos got onto his knees and stood up.

He moved one foot forward, then the other.

As he reached the city gates, the death bell sounded.

He raced toward the square, where a sword was already raised above his friend.

“Wait, I’ve come back!”

His friend turned around, and Melos fell against him.

“I almost left you to die,” Melos said softly.

“And I almost lost my trust in you,” his friend replied.

The day’s last light touched the walls as they held each other.

The ruler could only stare at the two men.

“Put down the sword and free both men,” he finally said.

“How can someone earn trust like this?” he asked more quietly.

He did not order them to answer, but waited instead. He had never done this before.

開いた窓(The Open Window) / 新Advanced(A2)

約193秒

The Open Window

Framton sat on his chair’s edge, turning his letter of introduction. His doctor suggested rest in the country, but meeting strangers wasn’t peaceful.

“My aunt will join us soon,” said fifteen-year-old Vera. “How well do you know her?”

“I know very little,” Framton replied.

Vera looked towards the wide-open window and the lawn outside.

“Something terrible happened to my aunt three years ago today.”

The letter stopped moving in Framton’s hands.

“Her husband and her two young brothers went shooting through there. Their little brown spaniel was with them. They tried crossing some wet ground, but fell through and disappeared. No one ever found their bodies.”

“That’s awful,” Framton said softly.

“She still expects them back and keeps this window open until dark,” Vera said more quietly. “Her husband carried his white coat over his arm. The youngest sang, ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ He often sang that to annoy her.”

Her eyes returned to the empty lawn.

“On evenings like this, I sometimes think I’ll see them.”

Her aunt came in quickly before Framton could answer.

“Has Vera looked after you? I hope the open window is all right. My husband and brothers will soon be home from shooting. Their boots will be muddy, of course!”

Framton went cold. She really did expect them back.

He talked about his illness and avoided looking at the window. His listener paid little attention and kept watching the lawn.

Suddenly, she looked happy.

“They’re back at last!”

Vera looked outside with her eyes wide and full of fear.

Framton looked round.

Three men with guns crossed the lawn in the evening light. One carried a white coat over his arm. A brown spaniel followed them.

A young voice reached them across the silent garden:

“Bertie, why do you bound?” the young man sang.

Framton could hardly breathe as the dead men came closer.

He took his hat and stick and rushed outside. Moments later, he was running down the drive.

Hungry and muddy, the men came in through the window.

“Who just left?” her husband asked.

“Such a strange man,” his wife replied. “He spoke about his nerves, then suddenly ran off.”

“He ran because of the spaniel,” Vera said calmly. “He told me he was terribly afraid of dogs. In India, a group chased him into an empty grave. He stayed there all night as they made angry noises above him.”

Making up believable stories in a moment was Vera’s special skill.
