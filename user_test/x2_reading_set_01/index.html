英語本文

Let’s try a little listening test. Imagine it’s four in the afternoon. You want to take a photo of your cat. You pick up your phone and… black screen. The battery is dead. Again.

That night, you ask an AI to help you choose a new phone. It says:

AI: “Look for a phone that you can use all day on one charge.”

Sounds like good advice. Now listen again.

AI: “Look for a phone that you can use all day on one charge.”

Ad voice: “Sponsored. See our new phone plans.”

Wait. Did you start to doubt the first voice when you heard the second one? The advice was exactly the same.

Our listening test was made up. But in July 2026, a shopper in Japan wrote about a moment like this. They asked ChatGPT for advice about getting a new phone. Below the answer was an ad from a mobile phone company.

There was a line between the answer and the ad. The ad also had a label: “Sponsored.” That means a company paid to put the ad there. Did it pay for the answer above the line, too? OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, says no. It says ads come from a separate system. Companies that buy ads cannot change ChatGPT’s answers.

But a line on a screen can’t tell you how to feel. In one study, 282 college students in India took part in a shopping test. When students saw an ad just below the AI’s advice, they trusted the advice less. The ad didn’t have to be *in* the answer. Being next to it was enough.

So, what about your phone? You can still use the AI. But instead of asking, “Which phone should I buy?” try telling it what happened: “My battery dies before dinner. Do I need a new phone?”

Now you have two prices to check. Look up the price of a new phone. Then ask a repair shop how much a new battery would cost. Maybe you’ll buy the phone. Maybe you’ll keep the one in your pocket.

After all, you didn’t really want a new phone at four this afternoon. You wanted a photo of your cat.

英語本文

A shopper in Japan visited many stores to find a bag. But in the end, the shopper chose one that AI had found.

The shopper wrote about feeling a little frustrated. All that walking—and AI found the winner!

You can see why people ask AI for shopping help. There are hundreds of bags, shoes, and headphones online. Who has time to check them all?

You type, “I need a light bag for work. My budget is 10,000 yen.”

AI gives you a short list. No more endless searching. It feels like talking to a helpful friend.

But here is today’s question: when shopping advice comes with ads, does that friend start to feel like a salesperson?

This is already happening.

One user in Japan asked ChatGPT about changing phones. An ad for a mobile phone service appeared below the answer.

The ad was marked “Sponsored.” That means a company paid for that space.

OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, says ads are separate from answers. It says advertisers cannot change those answers.

So an ad below an answer does not mean someone bought the advice.

Still, AI can be very good at selling.

In experiments involving over 2,000 people, researchers tested AI that tried to persuade people to choose a sponsor’s product. About six out of ten chose it. With regular search, about two out of ten did.

These were special experiments, not tests of ChatGPT’s real ads.

But the lesson matters: a helpful conversation can be a powerful way to sell something.

So, should you stop asking AI for shopping help?

Not necessarily. Change the job you give it.

Instead of “Tell me what to buy,” try this:

“Give me three choices under 10,000 yen. Tell me one bad point about each.”

Then leave the chat for a moment. Check the price on the official website. Read what people who bought it say. If possible, try it in a shop.

An ad does not mean a product is bad. And an answer without an ad is not always right.

AI can save you time. But it cannot put that bag on your shoulder and tell you how it feels.

Let AI find the bag.

You decide what goes home.

It is 11 p.m. You want new earphones.

You open a shopping site. There are hundreds of choices. One has great reviews. Another is cheaper. A third is on sale—but only until midnight.

“Please,” you think. “Just show me three good ones.”

So you ask ChatGPT.

A short list appears. Finally, shopping feels easy.

And AI really can help. One Japanese blogger said it suggested earphones from a brand they had not considered. They felt they had made a good purchase.

That is the attraction. AI can turn a long, tiring search into a simple conversation.

But what happens when an ad joins that conversation?

One user in Japan asked ChatGPT for advice about changing phones. Below the answer, a sponsored mobile service ad appeared.

You can almost hear the next question:

“Wait. Is this advice for me—or is someone trying to sell me something?”

There is an important difference here. OpenAI says ads use a separate system. Advertisers cannot change or control ChatGPT’s answers.

So an ad below an answer does not mean the answer itself is a paid message.

Still, the feeling can change.

Think about asking a friend which phone to buy. Your friend gives helpful advice. Then someone puts a phone company’s ad on the table.

Your friend may still be honest.

But now you have another question to think about: “Why am I seeing this?”

That question matters because AI can be very good at changing minds.

In two experiments involving 2,012 people, researchers tested AI persuasion. About 61 percent chose sponsored products, compared with about 22 percent using normal search.

These experiments do not show that everyday ChatGPT answers are secretly ads. They show how powerful advice in a conversation can be.

So, back to those earphones.

Before buying, ask:

“Why do these fit my needs?”

“What is a cheaper choice?”

“What might I dislike about them?”

Then check the price and details on the maker’s website. Read reviews from people who actually use them. If possible, try them yourself.

AI can compare battery life. But it cannot wear the earphones with your ears.

Use it to make shopping easier—not to make your final choice.

And that midnight sale? A countdown is not a reason to buy something you do not need.

Beep. Beep. Beep.

Imagine you are at the supermarket. The food goes into your bag. You look at the total.

“That much? Again?”

Now turn on the news. A political party has won a big election. People are cheering.

But here is the question: Can a win on TV make life easier at the supermarket?

In America’s November 2026 elections, the Democrats won control of both parts of Congress. Congress makes the country’s laws.

For one voter, the vote was a way to “put the brakes on” Trump’s actions.

Think of a car going too fast. You press the brake. You want it to slow down.

But pressing the brake is not the same as choosing where to go next.

The Democrats won Congress. They did not win the president’s job.

Trump’s elected term runs until January 2029. And the new Congress starts work on January 3, 2027—not the morning after the election.

So what changes?

The Democrats can investigate the government. They can ask for documents and call people in to answer questions. They do not need Trump’s permission to start.

But making new laws is harder.

Imagine Congress passes a plan to change a law. Trump says, “No.”

That power has a name: a veto.

Congress can still make the plan law. But it needs support from two-thirds of the members in each part of Congress.

The Democrats cannot reach that number alone.

So neither side gets everything it wants.

The election gave the Democrats stronger brakes. It did not give them every key to the car.

That brings us back to the shopping bag.

One voter wanted the new Congress to focus on living costs, not on trying to remove Trump from office. Another hoped to avoid two more years of political fighting.

These are individual voices. They do not speak for every American. But they show a challenge behind the victory.

Stopping the other side may win cheers. Helping people pay their bills takes more work.

Democrats have named medical bills and other everyday costs as early goals. Turning those goals into laws may require deals with their opponents.

So watch more than the arguments on TV.

Watch what happens to ordinary life.

The brakes are stronger now.

The next question is simple: Can the people holding them help the country move forward?
