# We Thought It Was AI—But There Was a Person Inside Meta’s Muse

Ring, ring. A call came from an AI agent—or so it seemed. As the conversation went on, the voice on the other end turned out not to be AI at all, but a person.

Meta had run a test that created exactly this kind of surprise.

The setting was the phone feature of its AI agent, Muse. In some calls made through Muse, trained contract workers, rather than AI, placed the calls and carried the conversations through to the end.

It was as if someone took off an AI costume and a person stepped out.

Having a person take over is not necessarily bad. A human can handle situations that AI alone finds difficult. As a system, that may even be useful.

But this is where the problem began. When a human staff member handled a call, sensitive information from the user could be shared with a contract worker at a call center.

There is a big difference between speaking because you think you are talking to a machine and speaking after you know a person is listening. When people share their names, plans, or personal circumstances, knowing who is on the other end matters.

### The hidden person behind the AI sign

Here was the reveal. The test began without enough clear notice that contract workers would make the calls. A user might think the exchange was with AI, even though a person was involved. AI had not learned to speak like a human; a human was standing behind the sign that said AI.

It was a future-looking story, but the main question was very basic: Had users been clearly told who was making the call?

As AI becomes able to make calls or reservations for us, this question will become more familiar. The more useful the feature, the more people will want to know whether the other side is AI or human.

### Meta admits a mistake

A Meta executive admitted that starting the test without clearly telling users was a mistake. The human concierge feature has been rolled back for now. In the AI age, we need more than technology that makes a voice sound human: we need to be told, at the start, who is on the other end of the call.

## In one line

Before AI speaks for us, we need to know whether the voice belongs to AI or a person.

# 20% Withdrawn—but the Oil Chart Was Not Finished Yet

This news feels like a short play in three acts. Act One was “20%.” Act Two brought an unexpected turn. Act Three was an unexpected move in oil prices.

The curtain rose on July 13. At 10:16 a.m., Trump posted that the United States would seek a 20% charge on all cargo passing through the Strait of Hormuz. The aim was to recover the cost of US efforts to keep the strait safe.

But “20%” was not a finished system. The post did not say who would pay, how the money would be collected, or what legal basis would support it. For the moment, only a large number stood at center stage.

The number stayed at center stage for about a day. Then the story took a sharp turn.

### The 20% plan changes overnight

About 24 hours and 48 minutes later, at 11:04 a.m. on July 14, Trump said the 20% plan would be replaced by trade and investment projects between Gulf states and the US. He cited “very productive discussions” with Middle Eastern leaders. He also said no one should charge ships in the Strait, and that he disliked fees.

The change was sudden. But to read the oil move, the dates must be kept separate. On July 13, Brent rose 9.59% and settled at $83.30. Reuters linked that rise to concern about a US sea blockade of Iran, planned for the next day, and energy shipments through the Strait of Hormuz.

### The chart refuses to stay down

On July 14, after the withdrawal and replacement announcement, Brent crude futures briefly gave back some of their gains. Soon, they returned close to the high level before the announcement. At the time of reporting, Brent was up about 2.6%, above $85 a barrel. Its final settlement price was $84.73, up 1.7% from the day before.

## In one line

Concerns about US-Iran attacks, the sea blockade, and tanker safety continued on July 14, so the flashy 20% plan left the stage, but the chart only pulled back briefly before recovering: the policy turn and the oil chart’s “not over yet” movement happened on the same day.

# The Oil Market's Main Character: The Missing 20 Percent Bill

This story may look as if Donald Trump is the main character. But the most interesting move came from oil itself: it did not fall.

On July 13, Trump proposed a 20 percent “reimbursement fee” on all cargo passing through the Strait of Hormuz. In simple terms, cargo passing through would help pay for the money the United States spends to keep the strait safe. It was like handing a large bill to every shipment.

The next day, the scene suddenly changed. Trump withdrew the 20 percent plan. Instead, he said it would be replaced by trade and investment deals involving Gulf states and the United States. He said this followed “very productive talks” with Middle Eastern leaders.

A demand for direct payment had become a story about trade and investment. The rough waves seemed a little calmer. What happened next was less reassuring.

### Brent oil returns to its earlier level

After the announcement, the oil market reacted once: Brent crude futures gave back gains. But calm did not last. Prices soon returned close to where they had been before it. At reporting time, they were above $85 a barrel. Still, oil seemed to say, “The bill is gone, but that alone is not enough to feel safe.”

### Danger around the strait has not gone away

Attacks between the United States and Iran continue. Fear of a sea blockade remains, as does worry about whether tankers can pass safely. Trade and investment deals have not changed this. Oil prices affect gasoline prices and transport costs. On the stage, oil showed that changing a plan’s name does not make danger on the route disappear.

## In one line

The 20 percent bill disappeared, but oil showed that the danger around the Strait of Hormuz had not.

# The Fee Plan Disappeared. Oil Prices Did Not Come Back Down

This oil market moved like a play in which the lead actor suddenly changed the script. The first scene seemed easy to follow. Then the performance turned in a different direction.

### A fee plan takes center stage

On July 13, Trump appeared and proposed a 20 percent fee on all cargo passing through the Strait of Hormuz. The idea was that cargo using the strait would help pay the cost of the United States keeping it safe. For ships carrying oil, it is a key route. The possible fee was more than enough to move oil prices.

But the next day, July 14, the stage suddenly turned. Trump withdrew the 20 percent plan and instead pointed to proposals from Gulf countries for trade and investment with the United States. He explained that this followed very productive talks with Middle East leaders.

Since the fee story was gone, oil prices might calm down, or even fall.

For a moment, the market seemed to respond that way. After the announcement, Brent crude futures narrowed their gains for a time. The reaction suggested that the market saw no extra fee, at least for now.

But this was not the end. Prices soon returned to a high close to where they had been before the announcement. At the time of reporting, they were about 2.6 percent higher and above $85 a barrel.

But the performance still had another turn to come.

### The danger at sea remains

Attacks between the United States and Iran were continuing, and fears of a blockade at sea remained. Worries about whether tankers could pass safely had not gone away. The fee proposal had vanished, but the danger at sea had not. That is why prices soon returned to their earlier high.

The sign above the news had changed, but the danger at sea had not. The oil market hit the brakes for only a moment, then stepped on the gas again.

## In one line

In a slightly ironic way, oil prices rose again after the fee story disappeared because there was still no guarantee that tankers could pass safely.
