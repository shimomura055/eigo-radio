Soapy’s Winter Arrangements

A leaf fell onto Soapy’s knees. Winter was sending him a message. Soon his bed on a park bench would be too cold.

He wanted to spend three months in prison. There, he could have meals, a bed and a roof. That was his plan for winter.

First, he entered an expensive restaurant. He planned to eat, say he could not pay, and wait for the police. But a waiter saw his old trousers and pushed him outside. His dinner ended before it began.

Next, Soapy threw a stone through a shop window. He stayed beside the broken glass until a policeman arrived.

“Maybe I broke it,” Soapy said.

The policeman did not believe him. People who broke windows ran away. Another man was running to catch a bus. The policeman ran after that man instead.

At a cheaper restaurant, Soapy got a table. He ate a large meal, then said he had no money.

“Please get a policeman.”

Instead, the waiters threw him out. A policeman nearby watched and laughed.

Outside a theatre, Soapy danced and shouted loudly.

“He’s a student having fun,” a policeman told someone walking past. “He’s not causing any trouble.”

Soapy stopped. Getting into prison was very hard work.

He started walking back towards his bench. On the way, he passed an old church. Warm light came through a window. Someone was playing music inside.

Soapy stopped by the fence and listened. He knew that song. Long ago, he had heard it with his mother. In those days, he had a home. He remembered his friends and the flowers outside the house. He had wanted to do something good with his life.

The music filled the quiet night. Soapy looked at his dirty hands. He could still work. A businessman had once offered him a job as a driver. Tomorrow, he would ask that man for work again.

He did not want to live on the streets any longer. He wanted to change his life. Tomorrow would be a new beginning.

A policeman put a hand on his arm.

“What are you doing?”

“I’m not doing anything,” Soapy answered.

“Then you’re coming with me.”

The policeman took him away.

The next morning, Soapy stood before a judge.

“You will spend three months in prison,” the judge told him.

Soapy’s Winter Arrangements

A dead leaf dropped onto Soapy’s lap in Madison Square. Winter’s first letter brought bad news for the homeless man. His bench had a lovely view, but little cover from snow.

He wanted three months on the Island: meals, walls, a bed. Help came with personal questions, but prison politely closed the door.

First, he would eat at a bright restaurant without paying. He would say he had no money and get arrested.

But the head waiter looked at his trousers first. Two strong hands sent him outside before his stomach could vote.

On Sixth Avenue, he broke a shop window with a stone. He stood by the glass and waited.

“Maybe I did that,” he said when a policeman appeared.

The officer thought criminals always ran away. He chased a man running for a car and ignored Soapy.

A cheaper restaurant let him in, even in those trousers. He ate beef, bread and pie, then said he couldn’t pay.

“Please send for a policeman now.”

Instead, the waiters threw him into the street themselves. A policeman nearby enjoyed the show and laughed.

Outside a theatre, he worked hard at shouting nonsense and dancing.

“Just a college boy having fun,” a policeman told someone passing. “He’s not dangerous.”

Soapy stopped shouting. Getting arrested seemed harder than getting honest work. Of course, he hadn’t tried working recently.

He started back towards his bench.

Light shone from an old church window as he passed. Someone inside was practising a church song on the organ.

He stood still and held the iron fence tightly.

He had heard this song beside his mother long ago. Back then, he had a home, with roses outside. Friends called at the gate, and he dreamed of useful work.

The music filled the quiet night around him. He looked down at the dirt on his hands.

He could still do honest work. The fur importer had once offered him a driver’s job. Tomorrow, he would find the man and ask again. He would start a new life, with no more winters like this.

Just then, a policeman put a hand on his arm.

“Why are you standing here?”

“Not a thing,” Soapy answered.

“Come with me, then.”

“You’ll spend three months on the Island,” the Magistrate told him in the Police Court the following morning.
