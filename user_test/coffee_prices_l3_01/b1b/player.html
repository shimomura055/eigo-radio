Full Story Part 1 (Aoede)
voice=Charon		“Coffee prices are falling!” Great, maybe spring will finally come to my wallet too. But on supermarket shelves, the price tags do not move at all. They look perfectly calm, as if to say, “News? Never heard of it.” If the market price of beans has fallen, why aren’t our costs getting any lower? First, let’s go back to when high prices were causing a stir in the market. Bad weather in Brazil and Vietnam disrupted supply, leaving few beans available for sale and stocks low. Then came strong global demand. It was a tough mix for prices to settle down. Around the same time, ICE’s arabica coffee futures hit a record high up to that point. But futures prices are not the prices of green beans for immediate sale, and they are not store prices, either. They may all wear the same name tag, “coffee price,” but they are not all the same thing.
Comment 2 (Charon)
voice=Charon		A high futures price is not the same as the price on a store shelf. So, when world prices fall but US coffee prices still look higher, what should we pay attention to?
Full Story Part 2 (Aoede)
voice=Charon		After that, an index showing world market prices fell by 25% over seven months. The USDA links this drop to extra supplies becoming easier to get. But in the United States, the coffee part of the Consumer Price Index was still higher than in the same month a year earlier. “Wait a minute, that doesn’t add up!” You might want to say that. But one is a world market index, and the other is the US Consumer Price Index. One shows a drop over seven months, while the other compares prices with a year earlier. They are on different stages and run on different clocks. If you pour the numbers into the same cup, the story gets muddled. And price tags follow their own schedule. Market experts and others interviewed by Reuters estimated that it takes around nine months or longer for green-bean prices to show up in store prices. If market prices are a breaking news alert, price tags are patient letters.
Comment 3 (Charon, Bridge to Part 3)
voice=Charon		So far, we have seen that market prices and store prices are different, and they do not change at the same time. Next, listen for what happens between a change in bean prices and the price on a store shelf.
Full Story Part 3 (Aoede)
voice=Charon		US roasters keep green beans in stock, then roast and package them. It was also reported that price talks with retailers often happen every three months. There are several steps between the beans and the store shelf. Even when market prices fall, not every step responds with, “Got it, cut prices now!” Of course, around nine months is an estimate from market experts and others. It is not a timer that applies to every company, or a promise that “prices will definitely be lower nine months from now.” It is too soon to circle a sale on your calendar. In other words, a fall in market prices is not an update button for price tags. The prices on shelves may reflect purchases and talks from some time ago. The next time you see a headline about falling prices, ask the price tag: “By the way, how many months ago did you get here?”
Comment 4 (Charon)
