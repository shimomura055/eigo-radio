2. Standard: Trial(左)と現行(右)

Trial Standard(新Prompt・400語程度)

## 1. A Friend Who Helps You Shop

You need a small bag. Easy, right?

But wait. It must hold exactly three stuffed toys.

One person in Japan had a question like this. And this is where AI can feel useful. You can explain what you need, even if it sounds a little strange.

No special search words. Just a conversation.

Sometimes, AI may even help you spend less. One online user said their AI told them not to bother with more expensive products—even when they asked about them.

Now that sounds like a good shopping friend.

But here comes the big question:

**Is this friend helping you buy the right thing—or helping someone sell you something?**

Those two things can look very similar on a chat screen.

## 2. When “Helpful” Starts to Feel Like an Ad

Imagine asking a friend for advice.

Then, suddenly, your friend starts talking about a shopping app.

“Wait. Why are you telling me this?”

In late 2025, some ChatGPT users had that feeling. They saw app suggestions, sometimes during conversations about other things.

OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, said these were not paid ads. It stopped those suggestions while it worked to improve them.

So, seeing a company’s name does not automatically mean someone paid to put it there.

Still, the uncomfortable feeling is easy to understand.

We know a shop wants to sell us things. But an AI can feel different. It listens. It remembers details. It answers our questions.

“Try this” can sound less like a sales message—and more like advice from someone who knows us.

## 3. Keep the Helper. Keep Your Judgment.

But paid ads are not the only thing to watch.

A journalist reported losing $150 at a fake online store they found through ChatGPT.

That was a fake-shop problem, not a paid-ad story.

The difference matters. But so does the lesson: a friendly answer does not prove that a shop is safe.

So, should we stop asking AI for shopping help?

Not necessarily.

Let it help you make a short list. Then check the shops, prices, and reviews on other websites. One Japanese shopper described doing just that before buying.

And before you open your wallet, ask one final question:

“Do I need to buy anything at all?”

Your three stuffed toys may need a bag.

You probably do not need five.

A good shopping helper should be able to tell you that.

現行 Standard(400語版)

# Your AI Shopping Buddy: Friend or Salesperson?

## Part 1: A Friend Who Saves You Money?

Imagine this. You need new headphones. You have fifty dollars.

You ask AI, “What should I buy?”

It gives you a few choices. You ask more questions. It never gets tired.

Then you ask, “Should I buy the expensive ones?”

Now, imagine it says, “No. Save your money.”

That sounds like a good shopping friend.

One user on Mumsnet described something like this. They asked AI about better or more expensive products. Its answer was simple: don’t bother.

That is part of the fun. Shopping does not have to mean opening twenty pages and getting a headache. You can just ask questions.

But then a brand name appears in the chat.

And you stop.

“Wait. Is this good advice—or is someone trying to sell me something?”

## Part 2: When Help Looks Like an Ad

Keep those headphones in mind. You want help choosing them. You do not want a surprise sales pitch.

In December 2025, some ChatGPT users saw suggestions for apps from companies such as Target and Peloton. Some felt these were ads.

According to TechCrunch, OpenAI said the companies had not paid for those suggestions. OpenAI also stopped that type of suggestion while it worked on improvements.

So, a company name is not proof of an ad.

Still, the feeling matters.

A banner on a website clearly says, “Buy this!”

A chat feels different. It feels personal. You ask a question, and a friendly voice answers.

When that voice names a product, it can be hard to know what is behind the suggestion.

But there is another problem. Even advice that is not an ad can be wrong.

## Part 3: Keep the Final Click

In a June 2026 report, a Tom’s Guide editor described losing $150 at a fake store found through ChatGPT.

That was not a paid ad. It was a bad shopping lead.

So, “Is this an ad?” is a useful question. But it is not the only question.

Ask, “Is this store real? Is this information correct?”

Back to your headphones.

Let AI help you make a short list. Then check the model, the price, and the store somewhere else. Read reviews outside the chat.

AI can make shopping easier. It can also make a mistake sound very clear.

Enjoy your new shopping buddy.

Just don’t hand it your wallet.

3. Advanced: Trial(左)と現行(右)

Trial Advanced(新Prompt案3・400語程度)

### 1. The Assistant That Says “Don’t Buy”

What if the most useful shopping advice an AI could give you was just two words?

“Don’t bother.”

One online shopper described asking an AI about better, more luxurious versions of a product. Instead of encouraging an upgrade, it essentially said: save your money.

That is an attractive shopping companion. No pressure. No awkward walk away from a shop assistant. You can ask, “Is the expensive one really worth it?” without feeling cheap.

But now imagine that, tomorrow, the same friendly voice recommends a particular brand.

Has it found something perfect for you? Has it made a mistake? Or are you hearing a sales pitch?

All three can arrive in the same calm, helpful voice.

### 2. When Advice Starts Looking Like an Ad

In December 2025, some ChatGPT users saw suggestions for branded apps—even in conversations where those suggestions seemed out of place.

Some users thought: advertisements.

OpenAI said they were not paid placements. The company acknowledged that it had handled the suggestions poorly and switched them off while working on improvements.

Here is the twist: no advertising payment was needed to make people feel they were being sold to.

Once a chatbot starts naming shops, the question changes from “Is this useful?” to “Why are you showing me this?”

For its paid ads, OpenAI says placements are labeled “Sponsored,” kept separate from answers, and do not influence those answers.

That distinction matters. A brand name in an answer is not, by itself, proof of an advertisement.

But “not an ad” does not automatically mean “safe to buy.”

### 3. Keep One Hand on Your Wallet

One technology editor reported losing $150 after buying from a fake store found through ChatGPT.

That was not a case of a sponsored ad. It was a shopping recommendation leading somewhere it should not have.

So even after you ask, “Who paid for this?” another question remains:

“Is this actually real?”

The answer is not necessarily to stop using AI. Let it narrow twenty choices down to three. Ask why the cheaper option might be enough.

Then step outside the conversation.

Check the model number on the manufacturer’s website. Compare prices elsewhere. Before entering your card details, check the seller—not just the product.

A friendly recommendation can be a useful starting point. It should not be the checkout button.

And perhaps the best shopping assistant is still the one willing to tell you:

“Don’t bother.”

# Is Your AI Shopping Buddy Selling to You?

You could open twenty shopping tabs. Or you could ask an AI, explain your budget, and let it narrow things down.

One Mumsnet poster described asking about better, more luxurious versions of a product. The response? “Honestly don’t bother.”

Beautiful. Finally, a shopping assistant that doesn’t want you to upgrade.

After a few helpful conversations, you might stop thinking, “This is a search tool,” and start thinking, “This thing gets me.”

And that’s when a product suggestion starts to feel different.

### Part 2 — Wait. Was That an Ad?

A banner saying “BUY THIS” is easy to recognize.

A recommendation inside a friendly conversation? Less obvious.

In December 2025, some ChatGPT users saw suggestions involving companies such as Target and Peloton. Some felt they were seeing ads—even in conversations where the suggestions seemed out of place.

OpenAI said these were app suggestions, not paid placements. No money was involved. It then turned off that kind of suggestion while working on improvements.

But there’s another question we should ask before reaching for a credit card.

### Part 3 — No Ad Doesn’t Mean No Problem

Suppose you check carefully. No sponsored label. No obvious sales pitch.

Safe to buy?

Not so fast.

A Tom’s Guide editor reported losing $150 at a fake store found through ChatGPT. This was not a paid-ad case. The danger came from a shopping result.

That’s the twist: “Was this paid for?” and “Can I trust this?” are two different questions.

Even a recommendation that wasn’t bought can lead you to the wrong place.

And those three stuffed toys? They can wait another five minutes while you check the shop.
