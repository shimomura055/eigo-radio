記事1 AI広告

原稿 TJ-84

英語本文

A shopper in Japan visited many stores to find a bag. But in the end, the shopper chose one that AI had found.

The shopper wrote about feeling a little frustrated. All that walking—and AI found the winner!

You can see why people ask AI for shopping help. There are hundreds of bags, shoes, and headphones online. Who has time to check them all?

You type, “I need a light bag for work. My budget is 10,000 yen.”

AI gives you a short list. No more endless searching. It feels like talking to a helpful friend.

But here is today’s question: when shopping advice comes with ads, does that friend start to feel like a salesperson?

This is already happening.

One user in Japan asked ChatGPT about changing phones. An ad for a mobile phone service appeared below the answer.

The ad was marked “Sponsored.” That means a company paid for that space.

OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, says ads are separate from answers. It says advertisers cannot change those answers.

So an ad below an answer does not mean someone bought the advice.

Still, AI can be very good at selling.

In experiments involving over 2,000 people, researchers tested AI that tried to persuade people to choose a sponsor’s product. About six out of ten chose it. With regular search, about two out of ten did.

These were special experiments, not tests of ChatGPT’s real ads.

But the lesson matters: a helpful conversation can be a powerful way to sell something.

So, should you stop asking AI for shopping help?

Not necessarily. Change the job you give it.

Instead of “Tell me what to buy,” try this:

“Give me three choices under 10,000 yen. Tell me one bad point about each.”

Then leave the chat for a moment. Check the price on the official website. Read what people who bought it say. If possible, try it in a shop.

An ad does not mean a product is bad. And an answer without an ad is not always right.

AI can save you time. But it cannot put that bag on your shoulder and tell you how it feels.

Let AI find the bag.

You decide what goes home.

“What phone should I buy?”

In seconds, it gives you a few ideas. It even explains which one has a good camera.

Much easier than reading a hundred reviews, right?

But then you see something under the answer: an ad for a phone service.

This happened to one person in Japan, who wrote about it online. And since June 2026, ads have appeared in ChatGPT in Japan.

Now there is a new question:

Is the AI helping you shop—or helping someone sell?

First, an important point: an ad is not always a bad product. And a product in an AI answer is not automatically an ad.

OpenAI says that ChatGPT ads appear separately from the answers. It also says advertisers cannot change the answers.

Still, an ad next to friendly advice can make people wonder. And what if an AI is actually asked to sell something?

Researchers tried this in an experiment with more than 2,000 people. In the tests, an AI was told to help sell a paid product.

About 61 out of 100 people chose the product with the AI’s help. With a usual search tool, the number was about 22 out of 100.

Even a “Sponsored” label did not clearly reduce the effect.

That was an experiment, not a test of ChatGPT ads. But it shows how powerful a helpful conversation can be.

So, should you stop asking AI for shopping advice?

Not at all. One blogger said AI helped them find earphones they had not thought about before. They were happy with the purchase.

AI can be a great place to start. But it does not have to be the place you finish.

Before you buy, look for an ad label. Check the price on the store’s website. Read reviews from people who actually used the product.

You can also ask the AI, “What are the bad points?” But check that answer, too. AI can make mistakes, even when there is no ad.

Think of AI as a friend who has lots of ideas.

Just remember: when it’s time to pay, you make the choice.

英語本文

Beep. Beep. Beep.

You are buying eggs, milk, and bread. The cashier tells you the total. You look at your small bag.

“Wait. That much?”

Now imagine watching election news that evening. The winners are smiling. But you have a question: “Will any of this make my life cheaper?”

That question connects two stories: a big political change and an ordinary shopping trip.

On November 3, 2026, Democrats won control of both parts of Congress, the group that makes U.S. laws.

One voter described voting as a way to “put the brakes on” the madness.

Brakes. Keep that word in mind.

Because winning this election gave Democrats more power to stop things. Making things happen is a different story.

First, the election did not remove President Trump. His term continues until January 2029.

And the new Congress starts work on January 3, 2027. The election result does not change who is in charge overnight.

Once the new Congress begins, Democrats can block some of Trump’s plans. They can also investigate his government and question officials.

But Trump has a stop button, too.

Suppose Congress passes a plan for a new law. Trump can reject it. This is called a veto.

Congress can beat a veto, but it needs two-thirds of the votes in each of its two parts. Democrats do not have enough votes to do that alone. They would need support from Republicans.

Think of two people planning a family trip.

One says, “Let’s go here.”

The other says, “No.”

They can keep saying no all morning. Nobody gets to the beach.

So, is the election just a new way to argue?

Not necessarily.

Congress can demand answers from the government. And the two sides can still agree on laws. A fight is possible. It is not the only possible ending.

In one report, a voter wanted the incoming Democratic Congress to focus on the cost of living.

That brings us back to the supermarket.

For that voter, the important question is not simply, “Can you stop Trump?”

It is, “Can you help with the bills?”

The election changed who holds power. It did not promise cheaper eggs the next morning.

Brakes matter. But after slowing the car, someone still needs to ask:

“Where are we going—and how do we get there?”

記事3 週末の寝坊

原稿 WN-83

英語本文

Beep. Beep. Beep.

Monday. Seven in the morning.

You slept until noon on Sunday. You spent half the weekend in bed. So why does getting up feel like climbing a mountain?

You reach for coffee. Then more coffee.

“Maybe I need even more sleep next weekend.”

But wait. What if the problem is not only how long you sleep?

One person wrote in a blog, “My worst Mondays came after my longest weekend sleeps, not my shortest.”

That sounds unfair. Sleep is supposed to help.

And it can. But your body cares about two things: how much you sleep, and when.

Your body has an inside clock. It helps tell you when to feel awake and when to feel sleepy.

Say you usually get up at seven. On weekends, you get up at eleven. Your phone still shows the right time. But your body may start following a later schedule.

In one small study, ten young adults went to bed and got up two hours later on Friday and Saturday. After the weekend, a body signal linked to sleep came about thirty minutes later, on average.

That does not mean everyone will have the same result. But it shows that even a weekend can move the body’s clock.

Now it is Sunday night.

“Time for bed,” you say.

“Not yet,” your body seems to answer.

Then Monday’s alarm rings.

You have not taken a plane. You have not left home. Yet your sleep times have shifted. This kind of difference is often called “social jet lag.”

Not so fast. Extra sleep can help.

But feeling better does not always mean you have fully recovered.

In another study of thirty people, extra sleep after several short nights brought feelings of sleepiness back to normal. However, scores on attention tests did not improve.

So the message is not “Sleep less.”

It is “Do not ask the weekend to do all the work.”

If possible, make more time for sleep during the week. Try to keep weekend sleep times close to your usual times, too—without cutting the sleep you need.

Your body does not know that Saturday is a special day. It just keeps time.

The goal? Enjoy your weekend without making Monday feel like a trip home from another time zone.

ある小規模な研究では、10人の若年成人が金曜日と土曜日に、普段より2時間遅く寝て、起きました。週末の後、睡眠に関連する体のシグナルが、平均して約30分遅れて現れました。

だからといって、誰もが同じ結果になるわけではありません。しかし、週末だけでも体内時計がずれることを示しています。

さて、日曜日の夜です。

「寝る時間だよ」と言います。

「まだだよ」と、体が答えているようです。

そして、月曜日のアラームが鳴ります。

飛行機には乗っていません。家を離れてもいません。それでも、睡眠時間はずれています。こうしたずれは、「社会的時差ぼけ」と呼ばれることがよくあります。

そう急がないでください。睡眠を多くとることは役に立つ場合があります。

でも、気分がよくなったからといって、完全に回復したとは限りません。

30人を対象にした別の研究では、何晩か睡眠が短かった後に睡眠を多くとると、眠気は通常の状態に戻りました。しかし、注意力テストの得点は改善しませんでした。

つまり、伝えたいのは「睡眠を減らしましょう」ということではありません。

「週末にすべてを任せないでください」ということです。

できるなら、平日にもっと睡眠時間を確保しましょう。必要な睡眠時間を削らずに、週末の睡眠時間も、できるだけふだんの時間に近づけてみましょう。

体は土曜日が特別な日だとは知りません。ただ時を刻み続けています。

目指すのは？別の時間帯から家に帰る旅のように月曜日を感じさせずに、週末を楽しむことです。

英語本文

It’s Friday night. You are tired, but tomorrow is Saturday. You have a plan: sleep late.

On weekdays, your alarm rings at seven. On Saturday, you sleep until ten. No alarm, no hurry. It feels wonderful.

You sleep late on Sunday, too.

Then Monday comes. The alarm rings at seven. You open your eyes and think, “Why am I still so tired? I slept so much!”

Does that sound familiar?

One person wrote online about a weekend like this. They felt rested during the weekend. But on Monday morning, they felt very sleepy. They couldn’t think clearly until after lunch.

What happened? Was all that sleep a bad idea?

Here’s one possible reason: your body has its own clock.

This clock helps tell you when to feel sleepy and when to wake up. When you go to bed and wake up much later on the weekend, your body clock may move a little later, too.

Then Sunday night comes. You want to sleep early, but your body may not be ready. And on Monday morning, your alarm rings before your body wants to wake up.

In one small study, ten young adults went to bed and woke up two hours later on Friday and Saturday. After the weekend, a signal from their body clock came about thirty minutes later, on average.

Thirty minutes may not sound like much. But when you’re trying to sleep on Sunday night, every minute can feel long!

Not at all. If you need sleep, getting more can help.

In another study, thirty people slept less than they needed. Then they got extra sleep. After that, they felt less sleepy. But they did not do better on a test of attention.

In other words, extra sleep can help, but one weekend may not fix a whole week of short nights.

So what can you try? If possible, make more time for sleep on weeknights. And try to keep your weekend wake-up time close to your weekday time.

One blogger made a change like this and said Monday mornings became much easier. But you don’t need to give up sleep just to wake up early.

The goal isn’t a perfect schedule. It’s to give your body enough rest—and make Monday feel a little less like a surprise.
