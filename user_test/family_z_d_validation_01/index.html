In heaven, beside a quiet lotus pond, the Buddha watched the world below. Pink flowers opened on the dark water. Far beneath the clouds was hell, a place of smoke and fire. Datta suffered there with many prisoners.

The Buddha remembered one small good thing Datta had done. Long ago, Datta had seen a little spider on the road. He had almost stepped on it. Then he stopped, moved his foot, and let it crawl away. It was only one small act, but the Buddha had not forgotten.

A small spider walked across a lotus leaf. The Buddha asked it for a thread. The spider spun a thin line that shone in the light. The Buddha lowered it through the clouds, down into hell. It swayed in the hot air, but it did not break.

Datta saw it above him. He reached up. The thread was so thin that he thought it might break, yet he held it tightly and began to climb. Smoke stung his eyes. His hands hurt, but he kept going. The opening above grew brighter, little by little.

Then Datta looked down. Many prisoners had seen him. One by one, they were climbing after him, holding the same thread. It shook under their weight. Datta’s heart beat fast. The bright world was still far away. If the others kept coming, the thread could break.

He looked down at the prisoners.

“Let go!” he shouted. “This thread is mine! I must escape alone!”

He kicked at the hands below him.

At that moment, the thread snapped. Datta fell through the darkness. The prisoners fell too. Their cries rose, then faded into the deep place.

Above, the Buddha watched in silence. His face was full of sadness. A thread had given Datta a way out, but fear had made him close his heart to the others.

The pond stayed calm. A soft wind moved over the water, and the lotus flowers rocked gently in the light. The Buddha looked at them for a long time. Below, Datta was back in hell. The bright thread was gone. Could one kind act be enough, if kindness stopped when it was needed most?

### The Lemon on the Books

I had a little money, a bad cough, and a heavy feeling in my chest. I walked through Kyoto without knowing where to go. The streets were wet from the morning rain. Every few minutes, I stopped to catch my breath.

I liked old things. A faded red sign, a blue cup in a shop window, a worn green coat—these things seemed more alive than new ones. They made me feel less alone.

On Teramachi Street, I found a small fruit shop. Bright oranges and apples stood in wooden boxes. Near the door, a basket held lemons. They were a deep, shining yellow.

I went inside and asked the shopkeeper for one lemon. It cost more than I wanted to pay, but I bought it.

The lemon was cool in my hand. Its fresh smell rose up to my face. For a few moments, I felt awake. I turned it over and watched the yellow skin catch the light.

Then I walked to Maruzen, the big bookshop. I had been there many times. Inside, the rows of books seemed to close around me. I saw familiar covers and remembered bills I could not pay. My chest felt heavy again.

I thought I should leave. But I did not move.

Instead, I began to pile books on a table. One, then another, then another. The pile grew tall and leaned a little to one side. My hands shook as I placed one more book on top.

I looked at the lemon in my hand.

What if I put it on the tower?

Carefully, I set it on the highest book. It sat there, bright and round. For a second, I could not breathe. The lemon seemed to be a tiny bomb. In my mind, it would burst—not with fire, but with a yellow flash. Every book in Maruzen would rise into the air, and all my worries would fly away with them.

Nothing happened.

The books stayed still. The lemon did not move.

I stepped back, then turned and walked out. I left the lemon on the tower.

On the street, I began to laugh. My cough still hurt, and I was still short of money. But the weight in my chest had grown lighter. Behind me, the little yellow bomb waited quietly among the books.
