# 💳 Why Digital Tip Screens Keep Asking for More Than You Planned

At the end of a meal, a ride, or a quick purchase, the payment screen asks one small question:

**“How much would you like to tip?”**

But the screen does not always start with a blank space.

It shows a few ready-made choices. Those choices may be higher than the amount you planned to pay.

A 2014 peer-reviewed study looked at more than **13 million credit-card taxi payments in New York City**. It found a useful natural comparison.

When the taxi fare was just below $15, the screen showed one set of suggested tips. When the fare was just above $15, the menu changed suddenly.

The analysis compared rides with fares just below and just above the threshold. But some passengers saw higher first suggestions.

Those passengers left larger tips.

This suggests that the first number on the screen can change the final amount. It becomes the starting point in the customer’s mind.

There was also a limit.

When the suggested tip was too high, some customers did not choose a smaller amount. They chose **zero** instead.

The taxi study does not cover every restaurant or customer. But it gives a clear clue about why these screens are powerful.

The numbers are not only showing options. They are helping shape the choice.

In the United States, restaurant tipping also changed over time. A policy brief says the usual tip rose from about **10 to 15 percent in the early 1970s** to about **20 percent by the late 1990s**.

Today, some digital payment screens show tips as high as **35 percent**.

### When a suggestion feels like pressure

One researcher says the move from cash jars to digital screens changed the social moment. With a cash jar, the choice could be more private. With a screen, a worker may be standing nearby while the customer chooses. He calls this pressure **“guilt tipping.”** It is his explanation of the situation, not a direct measure of how strong the pressure is.

### The pushback is now visible

A survey released in April 2026 found that **78 percent** of respondents said tipping had become ridiculous. **Forty-four percent** said they had reduced their tips from the year before. It also found that 59 percent felt they had to tip when a digital screen asked, down from 66 percent in September 2025. These are reported opinions, not proof that the screens caused every change.

## In one line…

**The first tip number can pull the final payment upward—but if it feels too high, some customers may stop tipping altogether.**
