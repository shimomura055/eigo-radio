# 💳 Why Tip Screens Often Ask for More Than You Planned

Picture the last moment of a meal or a ride.

You already know how much you want to leave. Then the payment screen shows a few suggested percentages. The first number can quietly become the number you compare yourself with.

That effect was examined in a 2014 peer-reviewed study by Kareem Haggag and Giovanni Paci. The researchers studied more than 13 million credit-card taxi payments in New York City.

The key comparison came around a $15 fare. For rides just below and just above that amount, the suggested tip menu suddenly changed. Because the fares were so close, researchers could compare customers who saw different starting suggestions.

Passengers who happened to see the higher suggested rates left more money. This is strong evidence that the first number on the screen can change the final tip, at least in these taxi transactions.

But the screen can also push too far.

When the suggested amount was too high, some customers did not simply choose a smaller custom tip. Instead, they left no credit-card tip at all.

So the screen is doing two things at once. It can lift the amount some people pay. But a stronger push can make other customers step away completely.

### From a private jar to a public choice

A 2022 policy brief by Michael Lahr of Rutgers University says American restaurant tipping rose from about 10 to 15 percent in the early 1970s to around 20 percent by the late 1990s. Today, digital payment screens can show tips of up to 35 percent. Lahr argues that choosing on a screen in front of a worker creates social pressure—a feeling he calls “guilt tipping.”

### The pushback is becoming visible

A Popmenu survey of U.S. consumers, released in April 2026, found that 78 percent thought tipping had become ridiculous. Forty-four percent said they had reduced their tips, while 42 percent felt more comfortable skipping a tip. The share who felt they had to tip when prompted fell from 66 percent in September 2025 to 59 percent. Also, 36 percent said they chose a custom amount instead of a preset suggestion. These are self-reported answers, so they show changing attitudes, not proof that screens caused them.

## In one line…

The tip screen is not only asking what you want to pay. It is helping set the number you judge yourself against—until that number feels so high that some customers choose zero.
