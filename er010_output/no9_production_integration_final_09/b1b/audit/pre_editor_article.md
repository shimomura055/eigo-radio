# Why Tip Screens Can Start Above Your Plan

You finish your meal, turn the card reader toward yourself, and see a few suggested tip amounts.

The screen looks like it is simply asking a question. But it is also placing a number in your mind. That first number can become the starting point for your decision, even when you planned to give less before the screen appeared.

The strongest evidence comes from New York City taxis. In a 2014 peer-reviewed study, Kareem Haggag and Giovanni Paci examined more than 13 million credit card transactions.

They found a useful natural comparison. When fares were just below or just above $15, the suggested tip menu changed suddenly. This allowed researchers to compare very similar rides that showed different first suggestions.

Passengers who saw the higher suggested rates left more money. In this group of New York taxi credit card users, the displayed default had a clear cause-and-effect link with the final tip.

But the story has a twist.

When the suggested rate was too high, some customers did not simply enter a smaller custom amount. Instead, they chose to leave no credit card tip at all.

So the screen can push the final payment in two directions. A higher starting number may raise the tip. But if it feels too aggressive, it may also turn a customer away from tipping by card.

### When choice becomes a social moment

A Rutgers policy brief says U.S. restaurant tipping rose from about 10 to 15 percent in the early 1970s to about 20 percent by the late 1990s. Today, some digital payment screens show tips as high as 35 percent. Michael Lahr argues that, unlike a more private cash tip jar, the screen makes the employee part of the moment. He calls this pressure “guilt tipping.”

### A backlash is taking shape

Popmenu’s 2026 U.S. survey points to growing fatigue, though it does not prove the screen caused it. Seventy-eight percent called tipping ridiculous, and 44 percent said they had reduced their tips from the year before. The share who felt they had to tip when prompted fell from 66 percent in September 2025 to 59 percent in 2026. Thirty-six percent chose a custom amount.

## In one line…

A suggested tip is more than a suggestion: in the New York taxi study, the first number moved payments upward. But when that number feels too high, the same prompt can make some card users choose zero.
