# The Tip Screen Keeps Asking for More

You finish your meal, turn the card machine toward yourself, and there they are: 20 percent, 25 percent, sometimes even 35 percent.

You were planning to leave less. But now the screen has given you a new starting point.

That small design choice can change the final payment. A peer-reviewed study published in 2014 examined more than 13 million credit-card taxi rides in New York City.

The researchers noticed something useful. For fares just below and just above $15, the screen showed different suggested tip menus. The rides were very similar, but some passengers saw higher starting rates than others.

Passengers who happened to see the higher suggestions left more money. This showed that the first percentage on the screen could strongly influence the final tip.

But there was a twist.

When the suggested rates became too high, some customers did not simply choose a smaller amount. They chose zero and paid no credit-card tip at all.

One policy brief presents this as part of the business logic behind digital tipping. A higher starting point may bring in more money, without much measurable effect on customer satisfaction or return visits.

So the screen is doing more than recording a decision. It is helping set the terms of the decision.

### From a private jar to a public choice

One report says restaurant tipping in the United States rose from about 10 to 15 percent in the early 1970s to about 20 percent by the late 1990s. Today, digital screens can show tips as high as 35 percent.

With a cash tip jar, the choice was relatively private. With a screen, the employee may be standing right in front of the customer. That can create pressure at the exact moment the percentage is chosen—a feeling sometimes called “guilt tipping.”

### Customers are starting to push back

A consumer survey released in 2026 found that 78 percent of respondents thought tipping had become ridiculous. Forty-four percent said they had reduced their tips compared with the year before.

The pressure has not disappeared, but it may be weakening. The share who felt they had to leave a tip when prompted by a digital screen fell from 66 percent in 2025 to 59 percent in 2026. And 36 percent said they chose a custom amount instead of the suggested rates.

These are self-reported survey results, so they do not prove that the screens caused the change. But they show a clear mood: many customers are no longer willing to treat the first number as the right number.

## In one line…

The tip screen sets the starting line—and when that line moves too far, customers may not pay more; they may walk away from tipping altogether.
