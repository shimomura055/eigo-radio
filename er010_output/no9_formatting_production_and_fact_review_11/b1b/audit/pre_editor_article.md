# Why Tip Screens Often Start Higher Than You Planned

You finish a meal, tap your card, and the screen asks for a tip. It may show several suggested percentages before you choose your own amount. That first number is not neutral. It gives the decision a starting point, and that starting point can be higher than the amount you had in mind.

A 2014 peer-reviewed study by Kareem Haggag and Giovanni Paci looked at more than 13 million credit-card payments in New York City taxis. The payment screen showed a small set of suggested tip rates.

When fares were just below or just above 15 dollars, the menu changed suddenly. This let the researchers compare very similar rides with different suggestions. Passengers who saw higher rates left larger tips. In this setting, that is strong evidence that the first number affected the final tip.

But there was a limit.

When the suggested rate was too high, some passengers did not enter a smaller custom amount. They left no credit-card tip at all.

So, a higher request increased payments for some people, but pushed others to zero.

This question sits inside a wider change in U.S. tipping culture. A 2022 policy brief from Rutgers University says restaurant tipping rose from about 10 to 15 percent in the early 1970s to about 20 percent by the late 1990s. It also notes that some digital payment screens show suggestions as high as 35 percent.

By 2026, customer fatigue was visible in a Popmenu survey released on April 8. Seventy-eight percent said tipping had become ridiculous, and 44 percent said they were tipping less than the year before. Thirty-six percent said they chose a custom amount instead of a preset rate. These self-reported answers describe views and behavior; they do not prove that digital screens caused the change.

### A private choice becomes a public moment

With a cash tip jar, the choice was comparatively private, Michael Lahr argues. With a digital screen, the choice can be seen by the employee standing in front of the customer. That may create pressure while the customer selects a percentage. Lahr calls this “guilt tipping.” A 2026 survey found that this feeling may be easing: 59 percent felt they had to leave a tip when prompted, down from 66 percent in September 2025.

### A business choice with a limit

Lahr’s policy brief also presents the issue as a business decision. High suggested rates may increase store income, it says, while having little measurable effect on customer satisfaction or return visits. That does not describe every business. But it shows why a small screen setting can matter beyond the moment of payment.

## In one line…

A tip screen does not merely record generosity. In the New York taxi study, its first suggestion helped shape the payment, while an overly high request led some people to choose zero. The customer is still deciding—but the screen has already started the conversation.
