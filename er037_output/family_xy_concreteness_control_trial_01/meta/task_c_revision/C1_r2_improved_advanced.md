# When an “AI Call” Comes from a Human: Meta’s “Muse” Problem

A phone rings. The screen says an AI agent is calling. You answer expecting that. The voice sounds natural. It asks what you need and moves the conversation along.

“So AI has come this far,” you may think.

Then comes the turn: the voice on the other end is not AI.

This happened in Meta’s “Muse.” In a test of Muse’s feature that lets AI make phone calls, some calls were handled by trained human contract workers. They placed the calls through Muse and carried the conversations through to the end.

On the surface, it looked like a call from AI. In reality, a human was speaking. It was like a movie reveal: you thought there was AI inside, but there was a person instead.

That might sound like a joke. But the story raises a more serious issue.

Human or AI is not just a question of how the voice sounds. It changes the mental barrier people face when giving information. And the more useful AI calls become—handling reservations and questions—the more often people may answer them. That makes one basic fact important.

What happened was not simply that AI acted human. Rather, a human was standing behind the AI sign.

### What callers thought they were sharing

The concern was that trained human staff might receive sensitive information from users. If you think you are talking to AI, you may be ready to share your name, schedule, or personal situation. But if a person is listening, the meaning changes: “Would I have said that if I had known?” The speaker’s identity matters.

### What Meta acknowledged afterward

Human involvement is not automatically wrong. Human judgment and flexible responses can help, and staff taking over a conversation that AI cannot manage is understandable. The problem was that the test began without enough explanation. A Meta executive called this a “mistake,” and the human concierge feature will be rolled back for now.

## In one line

Muse’s lesson is simple: AI help and human help can both be useful, but users must know who is speaking before they decide what to share.
