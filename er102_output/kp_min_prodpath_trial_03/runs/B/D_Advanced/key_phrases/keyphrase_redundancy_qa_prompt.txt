以下は、同じ記事から選ばれた5件のKey Phrase(英語学習ポッドキャストの
リスニング教材)です。5件から作れる全てのペア(10組)について、
それぞれ以下4つの観点で意味重複を判定してください(それぞれPASS=重複なし/
FAIL=重複あり)。

- meaning_overlap: 2つのkey_phraseが表す意味内容が、実質的に同じか(例:
  「注意を引く」と「注意の一部を奪う」は表現は違っても同じ「注意を奪われる」
  という概念を指しており、meaning_overlap=FAILとすべきです)
- usage_context_overlap: 2つの表現が使われる典型的な場面・文脈が、実質的に
  同じか
- grammatical_teaching_value_overlap: 2つの表現が学習者に教える文法・構文上の
  価値(例: 句動詞の型、比喩の型、コロケーションの型)が、実質的に同じか
- conceptual_role_overlap: 記事の中でこの2つの表現が担っている概念・役割が、
  実質的に同じか

is_near_duplicateは、上記4観点のうち2つ以上がFAILの場合、または1つでも
明確にFAILで「単なる言い換え、または同じ内容を別の比喩・表現で表しただけ」
と判断できる場合にtrueにしてください。reasoningには判定理由を1〜2文の
日本語で書いてください。

単に同じ記事・同じ話題について書かれているために表面的な語彙(記事固有の
固有名詞やトピック語)が共通することは、重複とはみなさないでください。
判定対象はあくまで、Key Phraseそのものが教える意味・用法・学習価値です。

pairsには、必ず10組全ての組み合わせを含めてください(rank_a < rank_b)。

【記事本文(参考、文脈確認用)】
# If You Had to Watch a Stone for Five Minutes, Would Your “Bad-Guy Switch” Turn On?

A stone. Still a stone. Surely something will happen soon—no, it’s a stone. Nothing happens on the screen. That goes on for five minutes. But you, the viewer, can still get one thing done: cut someone else’s reward.

This sounds grim all of a sudden, but it’s about an online experiment with participants in the United States. They were randomly assigned to watch either a five-minute video of a stone, where nothing happened, or a video of a magician.

During that time, they had three choices: reduce another participant’s reward, increase it, or do nothing. They could change it by 10 cents at a time, up to a maximum of 50 cents. The stone on the screen stayed silent, while the viewers were handed “a little power.”

So, did boredom become fuel for meanness?

Participants who were strongly inclined to enjoy seeing others suffer were more likely to reduce the other person’s reward after watching the stone video than after watching the magician video. Among those with a weaker tendency, there was no clear difference.

You might want to dismiss this by saying, “So it really is a matter of personality.” But there’s more to the story.

In the next experiment, participants were again randomly assigned to groups. But this time, there was no option to increase the reward. The only choice was to reduce it. This time, regardless of how strongly participants were inclined to enjoy seeing others suffer, those who watched the stone video were more likely to reduce another person’s reward than those in the other conditions.

Well, if we focus only on personality, we may miss something. Along with “what kind of person someone is,” there’s also “what they can do in that situation.” We thought we were reading a profile of the person, but now we start noticing the stage setup, too.

But it’s too soon to jump to “Add a button to increase rewards and bring world peace!” The two were separate experiments. We can’t say that the difference in choices alone caused the difference in results. We don’t know that offering an “increase” option can prevent meanness.

The experiment also dealt only with brief boredom and actions that reduced another person’s reward. We can’t apply its findings directly to everyday meanness or boredom that lasts a long time.

Even so, our view of boring moments shifts a little. We can ask not only “What mustn’t I do?” but also “What else can I do instead?” Before looking into someone’s personality, look at the menu in front of us. The stone may not move for five minutes, but the question we ask shifts a little.

## In one line
Stone-watchers were more likely to cut another person’s reward—only those high in sadistic tendencies in one experiment, but people at every level in another. The findings suggest that the choices available in a situation may matter alongside personality. But these separate, brief experiments do not show that offering an increase option prevents meanness or that the findings apply to everyday life.
