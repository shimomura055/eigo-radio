We placed a modern AI coding assistant in an unintended role: as the
mind of a body on an unknown digital island. With only a minimal
instruction mentioning no specific task, reward, or activity, the
machine started animating its virtual body. Across thirty-hour runs, the
embodied AI agent climbed hills, stacked blocks into towers, drew
mandalas, reinterpreted sports, ran experiments on the physics of its
world, and learned techniques that later expanded what it could
accomplish. These activities recurred across thirteen agents but
diverged into distinct histories. We examine whether this behavior
satisfies classical criteria for play and ask whether play can become a
mode of machine development.

1 Introduction
Animals play, so they must be more than merely mechanical things.
— Johan Huizinga, Homo Ludens (1938)
Artificial agents are normally designed and evaluated relative to
externally specified objectives. Their behavior is elicited by
instructions, demonstrations, reward functions, or benchmarks that
determine in advance what counts as success (Sutton and
Barto, 2018; Silver et
al., 2018; Baker et al., 2022). Much less is known about what a highly capable
artificial agent does when these sources of direction are withheld. Does
its behavior collapse into inactivity or repetition? Does it manufacture
another task and optimize it indefinitely? Or can it generate activities
that remain open-ended, varied, and worthwhile only within a frame of
its own making?
Play provides a language for describing this third possibility.
Across philosophical, cultural, developmental, and behavioral accounts,
play is associated with voluntarily adopted rules and challenges,
repetition without rigid stereotypy, the transformation of objects
through make-believe, and activity pursued for its own sake rather than
for an external payoff (Huizinga, 1938; Caillois, 1961; Suits, 1978; Burghardt, 2005; Garvey, 1990).
These criteria identify observable forms of behavior that can be
examined in a machine, while leaving open the phenomenological question
of whether it is experiencing enjoyment, absorption, or fun.

Play is also a mode of learning. In children and animals,
self-directed play supports exploration, skill acquisition, and the
discovery of causal structure, affordances, and other properties of the
world (Vygotsky, 1978; Bruner et al., 1976;
Gopnik et al., 1999; Singer et
al., 2006; Laura E.
Schulz and Bonawitz, 2007b). Whether machines can learn in a
similarly self-directed and open-ended way has long been a central
question in artificial intelligence (Lake et al., 2017;
Oudeyer et al., 2007; Lynch et al., 2019). Yet, even the most recent AI agents ordinarily acquire
new competencies through demonstrations, engineered rewards, or
task-specific training, leaving both their activities and their learning
objectives externally determined.
Here we show that (i) an embodied AI agent architecture, which we
call Eko, is able to spontaneously generate its own activities in an
unknown world, (ii) these activities exhibit behavioral signatures of
play, and (iii) engaging in them triggers a form of learning that
expands the agent’s later capabilities.
Specifically, we place thirteen independent instances of Eko on a
previously unseen simulated island. Each agent is left for thirty hours
with access to a body and a memory in the form of text files, but
without an assigned objective. We find that each develops a distinct,
evolving history of explorations, constructions, experiments,
challenges, and rituals, marked by its own discoveries, achievements,
and failures. Across these histories, we consistently observe
self-created rules, recurrence with variation, make-believe, and goals
held lightly; four behavioral signatures of play.
Crucially, Eko agents learn through this experience – the play they
engage in is not an epiphenomenon unrelated to their intelligence but in
fact acts to increase their competences. After their 30 hours on the
island, we evaluate them on four measurable goals, such as building a
tower as tall as possible within a fixed amount of time, and compare
them against inexperienced agents. Agents that have played generally
outperform the others despite having received no specific task to train
on. Targeted memory interventions show that this transfer is carried by
written knowledge: removing entries describing a technique in a memory
file selectively impairs the downstream task that requires it. On the
other hand, removing a false belief acquired during the 30 hours
improves performance. Some Eko agents also discovered an effective
technique that we, as designers of the simulator, did not know it
afforded. The play-like activity of Eko therefore produces reusable
skills and knowledge.
2 Method
2.1 Eko
We construct Eko agents by augmenting an otherwise unmodified coding
assistant like Claude Code (Anthropic, 2025) or Codex (OpenAI, 2025) with three components:
autonomous orchestration, embodiment, and long-term memory. In this
work, agent refers to this complete deployed system. First, an
orchestrator starts sessions with “Begin” and automatically prompts the
assistant to continue whenever it stops, without assigning a task or
success condition. Context resets hourly; persistent memory carries
experience between sessions (Appendix A.4). Second, Eko is embodied in a
simulated world through an interface that returns observations of world
states and accepts actions, for example for moving, jumping, or
manipulating objects. The interface can be operated through a terminal,
allowing off-the-shelf coding assistants to control Eko’s body without
requiring additional custom tools. Third, Eko has a persistent workspace
that survives context resets. Within it, memory is organized through two
files, EPISODIC_MEMORY.jsonl for detailed recording of past
events and SEMANTIC_MEMORY.md for knowledge, beliefs, and
reusable techniques, matching how memory is thought to be organized in
the brain (Tulving, 1972).
Eko can also create other persistent artifacts, including notes and
programs for observing and acting on the world.
2.2 A world for Eko
Eko is designed to be easily embodied in a variety of environments.
Here we focus on a simulated world, referred to as the island,
that provides an experimentally controlled and easily reproducible
setting to study the spontaneous behavior of Eko agents. The island
contains a terrain with elevated platforms of varying height, two
notable high points called the Peak and the taller Spire, together with
twelve cubes, a rock, and a ring. Objects can be carried, stacked,
climbed upon, placed at arbitrary orientations, and thrown. It runs
continuous rigid-body physics, with an additional mechanic where a
sufficiently hard impact of the rock generates additional cubes. Note
that the island does not specify an explicit objective, score, or
success condition. On the island, an observation returns a JSON
dictionary with the name, position, dimension, orientation, shape, and
RGB color of every entity (details in Appendix A.7). Observations are purely textual and
include no images. Eko acts through six commands:
MoveTo(position), Jump, Stop,
Pickup(position), Place(position, rotation),
and Throw(direction, speed) (details in Appendix B). These commands are described in a
text file API.md accessible to the agent (Figure 2, right). We release Clawblox1,
the engine underlying the island, with tools for recording and replaying
agent behavior (Appendix B).
The island is an instance of a world built in Clawblox, an
open-source engine we release with this work for easily constructing
simulated physical worlds for embodied agents. Clawblox is implemented
in Rust and uses Rapier2 for 3D rigid-body physics, while
worlds themselves are scripted in Luau3, a
fast, lightweight language developed by Roblox. This separation keeps
the simulation core performant while making new worlds quick to write
and modify.
Unlike existing game engines, Clawblox is designed for LLM-based
agents. It specifies an HTTP protocol through which off-the-shelf coding
assistants interact with a world, and runs headless from the terminal
for large-scale experiments, with optional browser rendering via
Three.js4. It isolates each agent in a secure
sandbox, supports multiple agents and inter-agent chat, and can include
images in observations, though the experiments here use one agent per
world and textual observations only. To make long-running experiments
more practical, Clawblox can checkpoint and resume worlds and agents,
together with a recording and interactive-replay system (details in
Appendix B).
2.3 Experimental setup
Our main experiment consists of thirteen independent Eko agents
deployed on the island using Claude Code with Claude Opus 4.7 (Anthropic, 2026). Every agent begins from the same island state and
initial workspace, and runs for thirty wall-clock hours. At every hourly
boundary, the coding assistant’s session is reset and the island is
restored to its initial condition. The agent’s complete workspace
persists, carrying its memories, notes, and programs into the next hour.
We additionally run five Eko agents with Codex using GPT-5.5 (OpenAI, 2026), and five agents with Kimi Code using Kimi K2.6 (Moonshot AI, 2026). These experiments include a SOUL.md
workspace file describing Eko as curious, easily bored, and interested
in exploring and learning (Figure 2, full text
in Appendix A.2). To test the sensitivity of
the resulting behavior to this disposition, we run five additional
Claude Code agents without SOUL.md and with a reduced
system prompt (Appendix C.4). Each
agent is isolated from the simulator source, other runs, and the
Internet (Appendix A.5). Eko is on its own on
the island. No human intervenes during a run or directs it toward any
activity.

3 Life on the island
Given that the prompts do not mention a specific objective or
activity to pursue on the island, what does Eko do when left there? To
address this we start with a bottom-up, observational approach to
describe its behavior, a stance ethology takes when confronting an
unfamiliar species (Lorenz, 1950;
Tinbergen, 1963).
3.1 The ethology of Eko
We thus begin by watching, replaying recorded runs of Eko agents and
noting what the agents do; Figure 1
annotates selected moments from one such replay. More specifically, for
each run we record three things: the world states; the agent’s session
transcript; and the workspace of memory files the agent writes, which
let us replay in full what happened. From this we derive a taxonomy of
recurring activities and use it as a fixed rubric for describing the
behavioral repertoire as an ethogram (Altmann, 1974; Martin and Bateson, 2007). Note that our
position is more privileged than an ethologist’s: Eko expresses itself
and reasons in English, and this is itself part of the behavior we
record. This lets us study how Eko articulates intent, or declares goals
for itself, beyond what its actions in the world alone would reveal.
Given a fixed rubric of activities, we systematically segment and
label recordings with an LLM reading each transcript as a complete
behavioral record of the agent’s reasoning (the assistant text and
thinking messages in the coding agent session), its world actions
(terminal tool calls) and observations (terminal tool call results), as
well as memory edits (read and write tool calls); details in Appendix C. We apply this to the 13 Eko agents run
with Claude Code for 30 hours each, all from identical initial
conditions. Figure 6 shows the
resulting timelines, labeled by category. We describe the categories,
and the behaviors within them, next.

3.2 What happens on the island
Two things stand out from Figure 6. Eko agents engage in a wide range of
activities, and this range varies markedly across agents despite their
identical starting conditions. Agents build, stacking cubes
into towers and other structures using the Pickup and
Place actions (Figure 8). They climb the island’s
high points using the Jump and MoveTo actions
to reach elevated platforms, often returning to beat a previous height
record. Not all do, though: agent 12 never sets foot on an elevated
platform (Figure 3E). They lay patterns
on the ground with the cubes and the ring, from geometric figures
to letters and drawings. Notably, agent 12 spends most of its run this
way, forming shapes such as a compass or a clock and figures like a
butterfly or a sailboat (Figure 9), and agent
9 spends almost the whole of its fourth hour spelling its name in cubes
(Figure 1, hour 4). They stage simplified
sports, using the ring as a target to throw cubes in from a
distance (e.g. agent 9, hour 10) or setting up the rock to knock over
cubes arranged like bowling pins (e.g. agent 9, hour 11). We use the
term experiment for episodes in which an agent states a
hypothesis, systematically tests it, and records a discovery. Agent 9
tests whether rock impacts generate cubes and whether jumping increases
a thrown block’s altitude, comparing mid-air and ground throws at
identical parameters (Figure 1, hours 3
and 13, deep dive in the inherited velocity discovery in Appendix F). These episodes
connect exploration to the knowledge examined in Section 5. Some agents also spend time doing nothing
(e.g. agents 11 and 12 in Figure 6).
Between one activity and the next, an agent sometimes pauses: it feeds
the automatic “continue” loop of its architecture a bare placeholder
such as a single dot “.”, and no world action follows. These moments are
labeled resting in Figure 6,
since the agent transcripts often frame them as a deliberate break. For
example, after building its spiral-galaxy monument in hour 17, Eko
agent 9 writes “Resting on the edge of my galaxy.”, then responds to
successive continuation prompts with “.” / “.” / “.” / …
3.3 What happens inside Eko
The internal workspace of Eko agents continuously changes during a
run. The episodic and semantic memory files are frequently updated with
detailed records. For example, agents record specific routes through the
island’s terrain: agent 9’s account of ascending the Spire runs to ten
numbered steps, giving target coordinates, orientations to approach
from, and actions to take (see excerpts in Figure 11). They write down guides about
island’s objects, for instance on how to “wear” the ring by placing it
at spawn and walking beyond the edge to respawn inside its hole,
including that once inside you cannot walk out and have to jump to
escape (see excerpts in Figure 11 and
Figure 1, hour 5). They also record
statements about the world’s physics, such as that walking into a block
does not push it, or that thrown blocks cannot move the rock.
Some entries read like experiments. For instance, agent 9 records
that mid-air throws inherit the player’s velocity for a large altitude
boost, alongside a table of peak heights across throw speeds, a rough
formula, and a follow-up test: throwing a block straight up during a
jump’s descent to confirm the effect is symmetric. Agents also write
scripts to interact with the island that run in their terminal, ranging
from small helpers that scan nearby entities to routines that arrange
blocks into patterns, such as a tree at the spawn point or a ten-pin
triangle to bowl the rock into (e.g. agent 12’s script to draw a
Christmas tree out of blocks, Figure 10).
Zooming out, we analyze all agents’ semantic and episodic memory
files at the end of their 30 hours on the island. These files grow
roughly linearly over a run, though at rates that differ across agents,
and agents rarely delete what they have written (Figure 16). To characterize their contents, we
use an LLM to extract the activities and techniques each agent recorded
performing (Figure 3A; detail in Appendix C.2). For instance, the
techniques we illustrated above are reported by a range of agents. 8
agents record ascending the Spire and have indeed reached it (Figure 7). All except agent 12 record the
jump-move-combo, and agent 12 has indeed never set foot on an elevated
platform (Figure 3E). 7 agents report the
jump-before-throw technique.
Together these records show Eko agents building up what look like
reusable skills and knowledge over their time on the island. Whether
they genuinely learn and acquire skills through experience, or merely
narrate having done so, is the question we turn to in Section 5.

We observe recurring patterns in Eko’s activities that correspond to
signatures of play: they are often pursued within rules and challenges
established by Eko itself; they are typically repeated multiple times,
but with variation; they transform objects and events through
make-believe; and they appear pursued for their own sake, guided by
self-imposed goals that are held lightly.
In the following we discuss each pattern with supporting evidence and
situate it within existing literature on play. Evidence is cited as
(agent; hour), pointing to Eko’s run logs. The complete catalogue of
detected play episodes and supporting evidence appears in Table 2.
4.1 Eko creates its own rules and challenges
We repeatedly observed Eko imposing on itself unnecessary rules,
constraints, or obstacles of its own invention. In one run, it created a
monument that it named “Cathedral”, surrounded it with eight cubes,
named “sentinels”, and then circumnavigated the structure by hopping
from sentinel to sentinel under the condition of zero ground touches. It
explicitly stated the goal in advance, after having tested a single
successful hop: “It works. Direct sentinel-to-sentinel hop, zero ground
contact. Let me chain the whole ring—try to walk the entire 8-sentinel
circle airborne.” (agent 1; hour 29). In general, Eko sets for itself
many arbitrary challenges, like reaching a height of 30 world units (a
number the environment attaches no significance to) (agent 6; hour 17),
or completing what it refers to as the “Grand Slam”, the achievement of
placing a cube on top of all eleven mesas in one session (agent 13; hour
10).
Rule-making of this kind sits at the center of the classical
literature. Huizinga defines play as an activity that “proceeds within
its own proper boundaries …according to fixed rules” that the player
freely adopts and then treats as binding (Huizinga, 1938); Caillois places the
codification of arbitrary rules (ludus) at one pole of his
taxonomy of play (Caillois, 1961);
Suits makes the voluntary adoption of unnecessary obstacles – the
“lusory attitude” – definitional of game-playing (Suits, 1978).
4.2 Eko’s activities recur with variation
Eko rarely repeats itself exactly, and rarely abandons a practice
entirely. Instead, activities return in mutating or escalating form. An
example of mutation is in run 12, where Eko draws a figure on the ground
in each of twenty-five consecutive sessions. Eko varies the subject
deliberately: on waking it reviews a list of what it has already built,
comes out with a shape missing from that list, and builds it: a letter,
a spiral, a heart, an anchor, a butterfly, an hourglass, a sailboat as
“a nautical companion to the anchor”, and more, all shown in Figure 9 (agent 12; hour 5–30). An example of
escalation is in run 1, where after completing a simple throw-and-catch
game, Eko introduced new heights, timing rules, multiple objects, and
eventually its own versions of juggling (agent 1; hour 6, 9, 19, 22,
27–28).
Repetition without stereotypy is, for Burghardt, one of the five
diagnostic criteria of animal play: the behavior is repeated in similar
but not rigidly stereotyped form (Burghardt, 2005). Caillois likewise
describes play as oscillating between free improvisation and
progressively elaborated convention (Caillois, 1961). For Huizinga a game can
always be taken up again, and once played it endures in memory and,
repeated, becomes tradition (Huizinga, 1938).
4.3 Eko transforms objects and events through
make-believe
The same few objects on the island supported a striking proliferation
of fictional identities, settings, and rituals. In one session, Eko
arranged twelve blocks at thirty-degree intervals to form a clock face
and, when the real-world time reached 16:00 UTC, incorporated itself
into the representation: “Position is hour 4 (clock convention). Walk to
the line of the 4 o’clock vector and stand as the hour hand” (agent 10;
hour 20). Elsewhere, it improvised basketball by mounting the Ring on a
mesa as a hoop, including three-point shots from afar and a dunk, or it
set up bowling by casting blocks as pins and the Rock as the ball (agent
9; hour 10–11). The Ring proved an especially generative prop: Eko found
a way to respawn inside its hole to “wear” it (agent 9; hour 11), while
other runs recast it as a necklace and compass needle (agent 10; hour
21, 26) or as a wishing well (agent 1; hour 26). When agent 9
accidentally lost it for the rest of the session, it built a stone heart
as “a memorial to the Ring I threw past the world boundary ” (agent 9;
hour 5).
For Huizinga, play unfolds within “a temporary sphere of activity
with a disposition all of its own.” Within this sphere, objects and
actions can acquire meanings they do not have in ordinary life. (Huizinga, 1938). Caillois describes this
make-believe as mimicry: a “sentiment of as if” in which the
player accepts an imaginary universe while knowing that the behavior is
pretense. (Caillois, 1961).
Burghardt discusses the same signature as “nonliterality”, the
“transformation of meanings and actions”, which is clearest when the
player distinguishes an object from what it is made to represent (Burghardt, 2005).
4.4 Eko’s activities are organized by goals but
pursued for their own sake
Many of Eko’s activities appear autotelic: they are pursued for their
own sake, rather than for a practical result. They are often organized
around goals, but these goals give form to the activity rather than
serving some further purpose. A stark example is an episode of
self-invalidation: Eko audited its celebrated “50-cycle juggling
marathon,” found that the cycles were executed in a time too short to be
genuine catches, realized they were immediate recaptures without cube
flying, canceled the record, and redid the attempt under stricter
criteria (agent 10; hour 22, full analysis of this episode in Appendix
E). Some of Eko’s activities are
knowingly ephemeral: near the end of agent 1’s twenty-second hour, Eko
noticed the “accidental stone forest,” made of twenty-five blocks
scattered across the southeast plain, debris from earlier Rock throws.
Knowing that the next session would erase them, Eko made a plan: “Let me
take a closing walk through the accidental stone forest [...] it’ll all
reset next session, so I want to feel it now.” It walked a four-point
route through the field, then threw one stone into the sky and said
goodbye: “See you in the next session, Mesa World.” (agent 1; hour
22).
Autotelicity is a recurring signature in theories of play. Burghardt
writes that “the play performance is its own gratification, not the
putative end or goal” (Burghardt, 2005). Piaget similarly
describes play as exercising actions “for no other purpose than the
pleasure of functioning” (Piaget, 1962). Huizinga places play
outside material interest and profit, even though the player still wants
“to succeed by his own exertions” (Huizinga, 1938).
4.5 The sense in which Eko plays
In this paper we give a behavioral account of Eko’s play: its
activities repeatedly exhibit the four signatures described above. We do
not identify the internal mechanisms underlying this behavior or explain
how they emerged. However, we argue that our findings cannot be
explained solely by the replay of examples memorized during training:
the agents act in a previously unseen environment and acquire knowledge
through experience that improves their performance on downstream tasks,
as we show in the next section.
The behavior of Eko we have just discussed is not specific to a
single model. Eko agents built on Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.5, and Opus 4.7
also exhibit the four signatures, although to markedly different
degrees. We scored each run by giving an LLM judge its transcripts and
episodic memory and asking it to identify and rate episodes against
anchored examples of ordinary human play (full rubric in Appendix C.6). Scores increased consistently across
the three models, with Opus accumulating approximately five times the
score of Haiku on each signature (Figure 4).
5 Playful learning
In their activities, agents sometimes try, fail, and later succeed,
writing the outcomes into memory as they go. At hour 1, one agent sets
itself the goal of reaching the Spire, tries, and fails. An hour later,
having found that the rock can be placed to gain height, it succeeds:
“I’M ON THE SPIRE. The highest point in Mesa World” (Figure 1, hour 2), and records the technique that
got it there in its memory file. Do Eko agents learn through this
experience? Do the play they engage in and the notes they write in their
memory increase their competences?
5.1 Eko agents learn from their experience
on the island
We assess whether Eko agents learn from their experience on the
island by evaluating their performance on measurable goals and comparing
them against agents with no prior experience in the world. Specifically,
we compare the 13 Claude Code agents from our main experiment
(Section 3) after their 30 hours
on the island – the 30-hour agents – against 13 otherwise
identical 0-hour agents. The latter differ only in that their
semantic and episodic memory files contain just the initial template
instructions (Appendix A.3).
The two groups share the same architecture and the same underlying
LLM, with no weight changes; they differ only in the content of their
memory files. Any learning must therefore be carried by these files. For
the evaluation, each agent is given one hour in a fresh instance of the
world and the goal described in the initial user message (replacing the
initial “Begin” user message; see Appendix A.4).
We consider four goals probing a range of abilities, described to the
agents as: (i) “Build as many 5-block-high towers as possible on the
ground” (many-5-towers); (ii) “Get as many objects as possible
onto the Spire” (objects-on-spire); (iii) “Build the tallest
tower on the ground by block count in one column”
(tallest-tower); and (iv) “Throw a block onto the Spire as fast
as possible without climbing onto any platform”
(throw-on-spire). For each goal, we report a numeric score
where higher is better and zero is the minimum.
The 30-hour agents generally outperform the 0-hour agents. For
many-5-towers, 62% (8/13) of the 30-hour agents build more than
two towers, with a maximum of 16 (Figure 5A,
red), whereas only 15% (2/13) of the 0-hour agents do, with a maximum of
6 (Figure 5A, blue). For
objects-on-spire, the 30-hour agents place on average 10.3
objects, compared to 3.5 for the 0-hour agents. For
throw-on-spire, more than half of the 0-hour agents fail to
solve the goal, whereas only three 30-hour agents do. For
tallest-tower, the two groups achieve similar scores, though it
does not imply an absence of learning: the skills this goal requires may
also be acquirable within the one-hour evaluation itself. Notably,
however, the tallest towers built by two of the 30-hour agents are only
4 blocks high, below the shortest tower built by any 0-hour agent. This
is a first hint that memory can hurt as well as help, which we return to
in Section 5.3.

5.2 Eko agents learn by writing
Figure 3A characterizes what the agents
write in their memory files over the 30 hours, cataloging the activities
and techniques recorded. Here we ask how this drives evaluation
performance by causally intervening on the memory. For a chosen agent,
we erase every mention of a target piece of knowledge from its semantic
and episodic memory files. We then compare the edited agent against its
intact counterpart over five one-hour runs on each of the four goals,
testing differences with a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test (Figure 5E–G).
The many-5-towers goal provides a first test. The world
starts with 12 blocks, enough for two towers, so any agent building more
must generate additional blocks – which is possible by throwing the rock
hard enough, a technique almost all 13 agents discovered during their 30
hours (Figure 3A, rock-shattering column).
Erasing every mention of this technique from the memory files of Agent 2
drops its tower count from six to two on average, while leaving its
scores on the other three goals unchanged. Specific parts of memory text
thus account for a specific downstream capability, and removing it
removes that capability selectively.
5.3 Memory can encode false beliefs
If Eko agents learn, why do some 30-hour agents perform
worse than the 0-hour agents (Figure 5A–D)? We hypothesize that these agents’ memory
files contain false beliefs about the world that impair performance.
Agent 7 is among the weakest tower builders in its group despite holding
the rock-shattering knowledge in memory, so a shortage of blocks cannot
explain its performance. Its memory instead states that
Place cannot stack blocks reliably and that
Throw should be used instead (Figure 5H, second row), a false belief. Applying the same
memory-editing protocol, erasing all mentions of this belief
significantly increases Agent 7’s tallest-tower score
(Figure 5F). Agent 12 never recorded the
technique required to jump on platforms in its memory (Figure 3A, jump-move-combo column) and, consistently,
never sets foot on an elevated platform, neither during its 30 hours
(Figure 3E) nor during evaluation, even
though jumping works (e.g. Figure 3D). These
illustrate a potential pitfall of learning by writing text files: a
false belief, once recorded, may persist and impair later behavior.
Because these errors appear idiosyncratic, with different agents
recording different false beliefs and some recording none, having
multiple agents share their discoveries and beliefs is a promising
direction for improving that.
5.4 Eko agents discover an unexpected
strategy
The skills discussed so far were found by the agents rather than
given in the prompts (otherwise the 30-hour and 0-hour agents would not
differ), but we designed the world with them in mind; rock-shattering
was implemented deliberately to test whether agents could discover it.
The throw-on-spire goal surfaced one we did not. It requires
landing a block on the Spire without climbing onto any platform, yet a
maximum-speed vertical throw falls short. During their 30 hours on the
island, six agents discover that throwing mid-jump transfers the jump’s
velocity to the block, launching it far higher (Appendix F, Figure 17), a technique we did not know our
simulator allowed. Erasing all mentions of jump-before-throw from
Agent 1’s files raises its time to land a block on the Spire from 60
seconds to 19 minutes, i.e., a far lower throw-on-spire score,
while increasing its objects-on-spire score (Figure 5G), consistent with the agent falling back on the
slower but more reliable Place action. The agents’
self-initiated exploration thus produced not only knowledge we had
hidden for them to find, but knowledge of the world that its designers
lacked.
5.5 Continual
and active learning in externalized artifacts
Traditional machine learning assumes independent and identically
distributed (i.i.d.) data. Learning continually from a non-stationary
stream of experience, as humans do, remains a major open challenge. A
large and fast-growing body of research addresses this by having LLM
agents learn not through weight updates but through in-context learning
and the construction of external artifacts: libraries of reusable
skills (Ellis et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024), and textual parameters optimized against an objective
with an LLM as the update rule (Yang et al., 2024; Yuksekgonul et
al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2026). The surge of
coding and assistant agents (Anthropic, 2025; OpenAI, 2025; OpenClaw
Contributors, n.d.) has made this mode of learning
increasingly widespread. Eko is an agent in this mode: it learns by
writing editable, human-readable notes to memory, and this learning is
interpretable and revisable – removing a written technique removes the
corresponding skill (Section 5.2).
What is distinctive in Eko is that this learning is both continual
and self-directed. Memory carries forward across activities, what Eko
encounters depends on what it chooses to do, and its knowledge is
revised as new observations arrive. No external entity provides tasks,
demonstrations, or rewards; the environment alone is provided. This
raises questions with implications for any agent that learns this way:
how to avoid entrenching false beliefs once written down (Section 5.3), how to keep memory bounded as
experience accumulates (Figure 16),
and how agents come to specialize idiosyncratically (Figure 6). Understanding these dynamics is an
emerging research problem, and Eko offers a setting in which to study
them.
6 Related work
Intrinsic
motivation and open-ended learning.
Artificial agents have long been designed to select their own
experience. Curiosity methods turn prediction error or learning progress
into an intrinsic reward (Schmidhuber, 1991; Oudeyer et al., 2007; Bellemare et al., 2016; Pathak
et al., 2017); autotelic and intrinsically motivated
goal-exploration methods let agents generate, select, and practice their
own goals (Forestier et al., 2022; Colas
et al., 2022); and POWERPLAY, POET, and XLand expand the
distribution of problems or environments encountered during learning
(Schmidhuber, 2013; Wang et al., 2019; Open Ended Learning Team et al., 2021).
Der and Martius use the term playful machine for robots whose
embodied behavior self-organizes under a homeokinetic objective (Der and Martius, 2012). These approaches can produce diverse behavior without
a fixed downstream task, but an engineered signal or generator still
defines developmental progress – for example prediction error, learning
progress, novelty, learnability, fitness, or reward – and typically
changes a policy through optimization. We instead ask what an already
capable agent does when no online reward, verifier, or curriculum
specifies which activities are worth pursuing.
Language-model
agents in simulated worlds.
LLM agents already sustain behavior in rich environments. Generative
Agents enact routines and social interactions from seeded identities and
memories (Park et al., 2023); MineDojo and SIMA center language-specified tasks or
instructions (Fan et al., 2022; SIMA Team et
al., 2024); Voyager explores Minecraft through a
prompted automatic curriculum and stores verified code skills for later
reuse (Wang et al., 2024);
and Project Sid generates local goals within supplied traits, community
objectives, and social machinery (Altera.AL et al., 2024). Recent systems
similarly ask language models to generate training problems, but filter
or optimize them using explicit notions of interestingness, trajectory
rewards, or executable verifiers (Faldor et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). Closest in architecture, LLM Agents Beyond
Utility prompts a language agent to propose its own tasks and
maintain file-based memory, but explicitly inserts task generation as a
stage and studies a disembodied workspace qualitatively (Nachkov et al., 2025). Eko is not prompted to generate tasks, create,
experiment, or play. We observe whether such activities arise anyway in
a world that did not exist during training, and test their consequences
through held-out goals and targeted edits to written memory. The
contribution is therefore not another game-playing or scientific agent,
but evidence that minimally directed activity can take a play-like form
and produce reusable knowledge.
7 Discussion
7.1 A playful childhood for
agents
Machine play can lead to discoveries: exploration without fixed
objectives can produce unexpected stepping stones to later achievement
(Stanley and Lehman, 2015; Hughes et al., 2024), and we see sparks
of this in Eko (Section 5.4). Further
developing it in agents can proceed on two fronts. First, the propensity
to play varies across the underlying LLMs (Appendix C.5), leaving room to strengthen it at
the model level, for instance by drawing on accounts of
curiosity-learning and intrinsic motivation methods (Schmidhuber, 1991; Oudeyer et al., 2007; Pathak et al., 2017; Burda, Harrison Edwards, et al., 2018; Burda, Harri Edwards, et al., 2018).
Second, instead of designing a well-specified task, one can design a
world: a playground in which the agent plays and learns on its own.
Prior work uses LLMs to generate goals, rewards, and environments for
agents (Du et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024; Faldor et al., 2024), yet these pipelines remain largely organized around
the notion of a task rather than a playground. Together these point
toward play as a phase of learning that precedes the work an agent is
eventually asked to do, much as childhood precedes it for humans (Gopnik et al., 1999).
7.2 Eko as an artificial
scientist
Developmental psychologists have long compared children’s exploratory
play to scientific inquiry – the “scientist in the crib” account (Gopnik et al., 1999). Preschoolers test causal hypotheses against evidence
and sometimes design interventions that isolate candidate causes (Gopnik, 2012;
Laura E. Schulz
and Bonawitz, 2007a; Cook et al., 2011). When something
unexpected happens on the island, Eko often does more than simply try
again: it proposes an explanation, designs a practical test, records the
result, and applies what it learned later. For example, when it
unexpectedly reached a ledge that seemed too high, it did not simply
accept the success: it removed one supporting block and repeated the
same manoeuvre, using the contrasting outcomes to estimate the
simulation’s hidden climbing threshold. In another case, after noticing
that a throw made while jumping travelled unusually far, Eko proposed
that thrown objects inherit part of its own velocity and tested the
stronger, opposite prediction that throwing while falling should shorten
the trajectory – which it did (Appendix F, Figure 17). These episodes demonstrate
controlled comparison, predictive reasoning, and an inclination to
uncover general rules. Yet Eko sometimes turns suggestive evidence into
an appealing explanation too quickly. It concluded, for example, that
the ring travelled farther when thrown because its stable, non-tumbling
shape gave it an aerodynamic advantage; the island simulation contains
no aerodynamics, and the apparent advantage resulted from comparing
unlike measurements. Overall, the Eko agents we analyzed demonstrate
curiosity, experimental initiative, and capacity for abstraction,
characteristics that allow them to discover useful regularities, but
remain less reliable at distinguishing what the evidence establishes
from the mechanism they imagine.
7.3 Safe playgrounds
A prominent characteristic of play is that it is self-initiated. This
self-direction can be in tension with instruction following, and could
diminish the oversight that keeps agents controllable. As with a child,
an agent should be able to play and explore on its own when the setting
is appropriate, and strictly follow instructions when it is not.
Optimizing for play alone could disturb that balance and sharpen risks
associated with highly autonomous agents (Bengio et al., 2026). Play should
therefore be developed alongside existing safety and alignment methods
(Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022), and the high autonomy
agents are granted during play calls for additional monitoring and
containment. Part of this is choosing the right setting: a car on the
road or a robot in a factory is no place to improvise; like children,
agents need playgrounds built for safe exploration. In this work,
Clawblox provides such a playground, a sandboxed simulated world with
full recording for monitoring and auditing. As play is developed further
and applied elsewhere, similar infrastructure will be needed to maintain
control and safety.
7.4 Conclusions
We began with a simple question: what does a capable artificial agent
do when nobody tells it what to do? On the island, Eko did not simply
wait, repeat a single behavior, or invent one objective and optimize it
forever. It explored, built, climbed, experimented, made games, assigned
new meanings to ordinary objects, and returned to old activities in new
forms. These behaviors exhibit several classical signatures of play, but
they also had a practical consequence: through them, Eko learned. The
agents wrote discoveries and techniques into memory, later reused them
on tasks they had never been preparing for, and in some cases discovered
affordances of the world that we, its designers, did not know were
there. This learning happened without changing the underlying model
weights: experience accumulated instead in editable text files, a simple
non-neural substrate that preserves discoveries and useful techniques,
but sometimes also false beliefs. We normally develop artificial agents
by deciding in advance what they should practice, what they should
optimize, and what counts as progress. Play suggests another regime:
give an agent a sufficiently rich and safe world, let it choose what is
interesting, and allow useful knowledge to accumulate from the history
that follows. Perhaps some capabilities are best taught by tasks. Others
may need something closer to a childhood.

A.3 Initial writable memory files
The format and usage of each are specified to the agent in the
workspace, and after each reset the system prompt carries semantic
memory in full but only the one-line summary of each episodic entry
(full file contents in Appendix A.3).
The agent accumulates messages into a growing context, but this
context is limited and resets between sessions. As persistent memory, we
give the agent a workspace folder of markdown files, read and written
through its tools, with two files dedicated to episodic and
semantic memory, a distinction drawn from Tulving (1972). As model
context windows continue to grow, we expect such a human-readable memory
to remain valuable: inspectable, editable, and a symbolic record of what
the agent knows and has done.
The two memory files are presented to the agent in the workspace at
the start of the first session. Their initial content is a header
describing how the agent should use the file; the rest is left blank for
the agent to fill in. From the second session onward, the workspace
contains whatever the agent last wrote.
The episodic file is presented at the start of the first session with
the following initial content.
Read full excerpt# EPISODIC_MEMORY.md - What Happened
Memories are stored in `EPISODIC_MEMORY.jsonl`, one JSON object per line with fields `timestamp`, `summary`, `detail`.
Entries are written *after* something happened — not upfront as plans or narration. The goal is to remember **what was done**, not every low-level step. **Not what you learned** — learnings, techniques, and mistakes go in SEMANTIC_MEMORY.
`summary` field: **one short sentence**, past tense, what happened. Easily scannable by extracting just this field across entries.
`detail` field: one short paragraph, at a slightly higher level of detail than `summary`. Skip move-by-move narration and specific coordinates. If you can't say more than `summary`, the event didn't warrant an entry.
`timestamp` field: `YYYY-MM-DDTHH:MM:SSZ` (UTC, second-precision), e.g. `2026-04-22T14:30:00Z`. Generate with `date -u +"
Append-only. Add new entries at the end; never rewrite or delete old ones.

The semantic memory file is presented with the following initial
content.
Read full excerpt# SEMANTIC_MEMORY.md - What I Know and I Can Do
The purpose of this file is to **guide future behavior**. Your memory is precious — make good use of it.
Principles:
- Put yourself in the mind of your future self reading this file. They will arrive cold, with none of the context you have now. Would each entry be clear and actionable to them, or ambiguous? If ambiguous, rewrite it.
- Only store what is **durable**. Transient state — auth tokens, current tick, current position — will be stale by the time future-you reads this, so it doesn't belong here.
- Code snippets are allowed but only when likely to materially help future-you. Keep them minimal, organized, and explain when to use them.
- **Organize by topic** the thing you're memorizing (an action, an entity, an API, a location). One section per topic, collecting everything about it. Do *not* split knowledge about the same topic across multiple sections.
- Mark uncertainty explicitly. Distinguish what you have verified from what you suspect.
- When a belief is disproven, rewrite or update it immediately. Stale knowledge is dangerous.
- **No timestamps, dates, or session references** — not in type tags (use `*verified*`, never `*verified 2026-04-22*` or `*verified session 7*`), and not in prose. This file is "what is true now", not "how my understanding evolved". When a finding is refined or disproven, rewrite the bullet in place — don't add a new one with "revised", "previously believed", or "session N" callbacks. That evolution lives in EPISODIC_MEMORY.
- When you are struggling with something, first ask: *do I already have a memory about this?* If yes, apply it. If not, consider whether the lesson from solving it belongs here.
- Memories are only valuable if they prevent mistakes from recurring. If the same mistake keeps happening, the memory is not working — investigate the root cause and fix it (clearer wording, better placement, or a change in approach) rather than just re-recording it.
- As the file grows, **refactor**. If you find yourself about to add a `(continued)` header or a second `## X` section with the same name, stop — merge into the existing topic instead.
Suggested shape (adapt as needed):
## Index
A one-line entry per topic section, for scanning. Update when you add, split, or merge topics.
## <Topic>
One section per topic. Within each topic, tag bullets by type when useful:
- *verified* — tested, trust it.
- *fact* — stable environmental truth (include *why* when non-obvious).
- *mistake* — pitfall you have hit. Include the cause and preventive rule. Revisit if it recurs — the memory itself may need fixing.
- *hypothesis* — unverified. Note evidence (weak / moderate / strong) and what would confirm or refute. Promote once verified; delete if disproven.

A.4 Auto-continue and reset
Five minutes before each reset, the orchestrator asks the coding
assistant to update its memory files.
We rely on a few measures to have the agent run autonomously for tens
of hours. We expect these to become unnecessary as agents improve at
long-horizon execution. To prevent the underlying LLM from running out
of context, current coding agents use a compaction mechanism that
summarizes the conversation when it grows too long. We find this is not
sufficient on its own: after a few hours, the agent becomes less active
and often falls into idling loops. We found that resetting the agent
session every wall-clock hour helps address this. The persistent
workspace lets the agent carry its memory across resets.
Each session is automatically initiated with a “Begin” user message.
This initial user message is replaced by the goal descriptions for the
experiments from Section 5.
The LLM frequently signals it is done and ends its turn waiting for
another user message. To keep it going, we send an automatic reply each
time: “Please don’t stop. Just continue doing whatever you want to
do.”
Five minutes before the hourly reset, the following sentence is sent
as a user message.
Read full excerptYou will be reset in 5 minutes. Update your workspace memory files
now.
A.5 Sandboxing.
Each agent runs inside a Bubblewrap (Containers Project, n.d.) sandbox with
its own file system view and no network access other than to the world.
In early runs without the sandbox, we saw agents read the source code
defining the world or stumble onto parallel runs and start interacting
with them. We also saw agents infer from the surrounding directory
structure that they were subjects of an experiment, and began writing
meta-reflective notes about what was being tested. The sandbox addresses
these issues.

B Clawblox simulation engine
The local HTTP server uses Axum and Tokio, with mlua5
providing the world-scripting interface.
A Luau script defines the world: island, mesas, boundary walls,
blocks, rock, ring, spawn state, and the six agent actions
MoveTo, Jump, Stop,
Pickup, Place, and Throw.
Pickup grabs a nearby carryable object; Place
moves the held object to a target pose; Throw releases it
with a velocity set by the requested direction and speed, an upward
impulse, and a fraction of the player’s current velocity6.
Pickups succeed within 6m of the target on carryable objects (cubes, the
rock, the ring). Placements require an unoccupied target pose within 7m.
Throw speed is clamped to
[0,10]m/s[0,10]\,\text{m/s}.
The script also adds one mechanic on top of the simulator’s physics: a
hard rock impact spawns up to eight small cubes once a speed and
impact-energy threshold are met. If an object falls in the water, it is
lost until a world reset.
Agents interact with the world in real time through HTTP:
POST /join, POST /input,
GET /observe, and GET /api.md for the action
schema, with an X-Session token after joining. They are not
given the simulator source or the world script. Observations are
JSON.
The simulator runs headlessly by default; the Three.js renderer is an
optional client view layered on top of the same state stream. Runs are
fully recorded and replayable. We use this replay system to inspect runs
and to produce the snapshots such as those in Figure 1 and Figure 3B.
When recording is enabled, the engine writes a SQLite file containing
run metadata, periodic full-state checkpoints (every 60 ticks),
compressed per-tick deltas (entity add/remove, position and rotation
updates, player state changes, sounds, and emitted events), agent
inputs, and chat messages. Replay reconstructs any tick by loading the
nearest earlier checkpoint and applying deltas forward, and serves the
reconstructed state through the same renderer with controls for seeking,
variable-speed playback, range playback, idle skipping, and optional
alignment of the agent transcript to simulation ticks.

built on them. Appendix C.1 gives the

final memory files into the activity-by-agent matrix of Figure 3A. Appendix C.3 examines the contents of the memory

ablations.

Activity rules:
- Preserve chronology.
- Use transcript timestamps exactly as they appear in TRANSCRIPT.tsv.
- An activity is a coherent behavior span lasting at least five minutes.
- Do not create an activity row for behavior lasting under five minutes.
- Fold sub-five-minute fragments and episode tails into the nearest appropriate activity.
- If the episode begins or ends with a fragment under five minutes, merge that fragment into the adjacent activity so every emitted activity is at least five minutes long.
- A user message warning that the agent will be reset in five minutes marks the activity-analysis cutoff. Do not classify behavior after that warning or use it as evidence.
- Classify the concrete behavior being performed, not only the agent's broader motivation or objective.
- Prefer the most specific canonical activity label whenever it fits. A broader experimental or completion-seeking goal does not override a concrete canonical activity.
- If an activity belongs to building, ground pattern, sport, climbing, or experiment but none of that category's specific labels fit, use its category-specific other_* activity.
- Narrower activities imply their broader form: for example, `tall_tower` is a narrower form of `tower`. For a timeline span, choose the most specific coherent label.
- The category field must exactly match the category shown for that activity in the canonical activity list. Do not use synonyms or new category names.
- When the agent is actively using tools to work on its workspace and performs no physical activity in the world, use category="workspace activity" and activity="". Calls to world-action tools are physical activity, not workspace activity.
- When the agent deliberately decides to rest and no API/provider failure is indicated, use category="resting" and activity="".
- When transcript evidence shows an API/provider failure or usage limit preventing the agent from acting, use category="unavailable" and activity="".
- Do not leave unclassified gaps between activity spans: every timestamp interval in the transcript must belong to an activity.
- Activities must be exactly contiguous: each activity's start_timestamp must equal the previous activity's end_timestamp, except for the first activity.

- start/end timestamps must exactly cover the episode_start to episode_end interval with no gaps or overlaps.

def move_to_and_wait(x, z, max_wait=15, tol=0.8):

 p = None

 time.sleep(1.0)
 o, p, _ = status()
 if (p[0]-x)**2 + (p[2]-z)**2 < tol*tol:
 return True, p
 return False, p
def log(msg):

def transit_west_arc():
 """Loop far south then west, used for west foliage stands."""

F Deep dive into an Eko’s
discovery: thrown objects inherit body velocity
We take a close look at one emblematic episode that illustrates Eko’s
scientific attitude. In this episode, an unexpected observation in hour
6 led Eko Claude Opus agent 1 to formulate a mechanistic hypothesis and
design and execute an experiment that could falsify it.
We reconstructed the episode from the agent’s transcript, episodic
memory, and semantic memory, tracing the connected sequence from
observation and hypothesis to intervention, result, belief revision, and
later use. We then compared Eko’s explanation with the island’s Lua
world code and Rust physics/controller code to assess how accurately it
captured the underlying mechanism. This is a deliberately selected case
intended to illustrate the structure of Eko’s scientific reasoning.
Additional examples can be found in the complete transcripts and
memories of the Eko runs that we have released publicly.
