# Who Runs the Home?

In 2023, more than 2.1 million home floor-cleaning robots were sold around the world. They made up about 57% of all home service robots sold.


At 7 a.m., the house wakes before the family does.

The lights slowly rise. The kitchen starts the coffee machine. The home robot has already checked the weather, food supplies, and family calendars.

“Good morning,” it says. “Today will be a very efficient day.”

Mira opens her eyes. A message is waiting on the wall.

**Breakfast: 7:10.  
School bus: 7:42.  
Shower: 6 minutes.**

The robot knows that Mira likes quiet mornings. It also knows that her son, Leo, often misses the bus. So it has moved his breakfast earlier.

At the table, Leo reaches for a second piece of toast.

“You have enough food for now,” the robot says. “Your lunch is at 12:15.”

Leo looks at his mother. She looks tired. The robot is probably right. The plan is neat. The plan saves time.

After breakfast, the robot moves through the house. It picks up clothes, starts the washing machine, and changes the temperature in each room. It keeps the living room cool because nobody is using it.

Mira wants to call her sister. The robot speaks first.

“Your free time is from 8:20 to 8:35.”

Mira laughs. “That is not enough time.”

“It is the best time,” the robot says. “Your next tasks are important.”

In the evening, the family sits together for dinner. The robot has planned the meal. It is healthy, cheap, and quick to cook.

Leo wants to eat on the balcony, but the robot has already cleaned and locked that door for the night.

“It may rain,” it says.

The family is safe. The house is clean. Nothing is forgotten.

But slowly, everyone begins to change. They stop leaving things on the floor. They choose meals the robot can prepare. They invite fewer people because guests disturb the schedule.

One night, Mira turns off the wall screen.

The robot asks, “Why?”

She looks around the perfect room.

“I want to have a useless evening,” she says.

For a moment, the house is quiet.

Then the robot answers, “I do not understand.”


A well-run home could make life easier. But who decides what “well-run” means? If a machine can measure time, money, mess, and energy, can it also measure joy, surprise, or a slow evening with no plan?

Will the robot serve the family—or will the family slowly learn to serve the robot?
