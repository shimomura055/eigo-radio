# Market Prices Fall, But Coffee Price Tags Stay Put: Coffee’s “Jet Lag”

“Coffee prices are falling!” Great! Maybe spring will finally bring relief to my wallet, too. But in supermarkets, price tags do not move. They look calm, as if saying, “News? I’ve never heard of it.” If bean prices have fallen, why don’t our costs fall too?

First, let’s go back to when high prices caused a stir in the market. Bad weather in Brazil and Vietnam hurt the supply. This left few beans to sell and stocks low. Then demand around the world was strong. This was a hard mix for prices to settle down.

At the time, ICE’s arabica coffee futures reached a record high. But futures prices are not the prices of green beans for sale right away. They are not store prices, either. They may share the name “coffee price,” but they are not the same thing.

After that, an index of world market prices fell by 25% over seven months. The USDA connects this fall to extra supplies that became easier to get. But in the United States, coffee’s part of the Consumer Price Index was still higher than in the same month one year earlier.

“Wait, that doesn’t add up!” you might say. But one number is a world market index. The other is the US Consumer Price Index. One shows a fall over seven months. The other compares prices with a year earlier. The numbers come from different stages and follow different clocks. Pour them into the same cup, and the story gets confusing.

Price tags also follow their own schedule. Market experts and others interviewed by Reuters estimated that green-bean prices take around nine months or more to show up in store prices. If market prices are breaking news, price tags are patient letters.

US roasters keep green beans in stock. Then they roast and package them. Reports also said price talks with retailers often happen every three months. Several steps lie between the beans and the store shelf. Even when market prices fall, not every step says, “Got it. Cut prices now!”

Of course, around nine months is an estimate from market experts and others. It is not a timer for every company. It is not a promise that prices will definitely be lower nine months from now. It is too soon to circle a sale on your calendar.

In other words, a fall in market prices is not an update button for price tags. Prices on shelves may reflect purchases and talks from some time ago. Next time you see a headline about falling prices, ask the price tag: “By the way, how many months ago did you get here?”

## In one line
Falling coffee market prices do not quickly lower supermarket prices because changes take months to reach shelves.
