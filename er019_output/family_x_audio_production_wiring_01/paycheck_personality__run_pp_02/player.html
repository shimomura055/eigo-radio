The participants were working people who reported their personality and income from work. Their personality and income were each surveyed at least three times. So this was not a single snapshot, but a series of photos showing changes over time. The direction of the camera matters here. The researchers separated the differences between people—“That person is more hardworking than me and also earns more”—from changes within the same person: “How did my personality change after my income changed?” They moved from ranking people by yearly income to tracking one person over time. It’s a different contest altogether.

Full Story Part 2 (Aoede)
voice=Charon		The personality trait they focused on was conscientiousness: the tendency to work seriously and steadily. Their analysis found that, within the same person, higher income was linked to higher conscientiousness later on. Wait—we thought this was about personality affecting income, but income got a part too! And the arrow doesn’t point just one way. A rise in conscientiousness was also weakly linked to higher income later on. This was an analysis of data that tracked changes, and it cannot prove that income changed personality or that personality increased income. Something else may have affected both.
