# When AI Sits Between a Job and a Person

## The Question

A job application may first go through software. A computer may screen a résumé, score a personality test, or judge a video. It may study a person's face, voice, and gestures. The applicant sees a job chance. The recruiter sees many applications. The owner sees a company decision. One screen creates three different experiences.

### Voice 1: The Applicant's Story

I speak to a camera. Software scores my voice, face, and gestures. I read about a woman who did well on a skills test. But AI gave her low scores for gestures and facial expressions. She later had no job for a long time. I also know about an applicant who said AI scored his reliability and honesty. He had no way to refuse the score or question it. I want people to see my skills and character. I do not want them reduced to one score.

### Voice 2: The Recruiter's Story

I start with résumés and an AI tool. It sorts résumés, sets interviews, and matches people with jobs. It helps when applications arrive faster than my team can read them. But a score does not finish the work. I may write a simple summary of a fairness check. I may also answer candidates' questions. One company's public notice shows this duty is real. I also judge résumés written by AI. Are they a useful skill, or do they show too little effort? I run the tool. But I do not decide if the company adopts it.

### Voice 3: The Business Owner's Story

Each week, I check the hiring dashboard. I look at time, cost, and the people we still need. If hiring slows, the work does not wait. I have read reports of faster hiring and lower costs after AI. That is why I may keep using it. But news of a tool that rated women lower makes me check my choice again. If people question it, my name is attached. I protect the company's future. But I also carry the risk of being wrong.

## Why They See It Differently

All three want the right person in the right job. But they face different problems. The applicant may lose a fair chance. The recruiter must keep work moving and explain the system. The owner must keep the company running and answer for the choice. The applicant cannot choose the system. The recruiter runs it and may help decide if the company adopts it. The owner may make the final choice. Outside rules limit all three roles. In New York City, an employer needs a recent fairness check. It also needs a public summary and notice before using an automated hiring tool. The recruiter cannot use the tool in secret. The owner cannot depend on speed alone. The applicant still cannot control the score. One company stopped a tool after it rated women lower. These views do not give one simple answer.

## What This Question Really Means

This is not only a question about a machine. It may read a résumé or study a face. It is also about who decides what a fair chance means. Who must explain the system? Who faces the results when it fails? Hiring is not only a choice about tools. It is also about power, proof, and responsibility.
