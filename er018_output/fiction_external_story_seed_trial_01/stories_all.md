```
### The Open Gate

I was thirteen when my family moved to a new farm. We took four cows, two calves, and one old ox with us. The ox pulled a small cart with our bags and tools.

It was bigger than the other animals. My father always watched it carefully.

“Stay away from its horns,” he told me. “It is usually calm, but frightened animals can be dangerous.”

We walked along a dirt road beside an empty pasture. The afternoon was hot, and the animals moved slowly. Then a loose metal box fell from the cart.

It hit the road with a loud **bang**.

The ox jumped. It kicked the cart, broke the rope, and ran toward the pasture gate. Dust rose around its feet. Its horns pointed forward.

My father grabbed the broken rope, but the ox pulled him down. My mother shouted, “Move!”

I saw her standing near the gate. For one second, she could not move. The ox was coming straight at her.

I wanted to run away. My heart beat hard. But the gate was only a few steps from me.

“Open it!” my father shouted.

My mother pulled at the gate, but it was stuck. I ran to her and pushed with both hands. The ox came closer. I could hear its heavy steps behind me.

“Again!” my mother cried.

We pushed together. The gate opened a little.

My father stood up and moved to the side. He did not pull the rope. He waved his arms and called loudly. The ox turned toward him. Then he walked backward, slowly, toward the open gate.

“Keep it open!” he shouted.

The ox ran past us and into the empty pasture. I let go of the gate and jumped aside. My mother pulled it shut, and my father locked it with a thick piece of wood.

For a moment, nobody spoke. The ox walked around the pasture, breathing hard.

Then my mother began to laugh. Soon, we all laughed, even my father. My legs were still shaking, but I felt different. I had been afraid, yet I had not run away.

Years later, my father told this story to our family. He always lifted his hands when he described the ox. “That gate saved us,” he said.

I knew the gate helped. But I also knew something else: it opened because we pushed together.
```

```
### Two Plus One

After eleven hours on an overnight bus, I arrived in the city at six in the morning. I was seventeen, tired, and almost out of money.

My aunt was supposed to meet me at the station, but my phone was dead. I sat on my backpack and watched people hurry past. Their voices sounded fast and strange.

Then my stomach made a loud sound.

I went to a small café inside the station. A sign above the counter said:

**BREAKFAST DEAL: 2 + 1 FREE — €2.50**

I understood the words, but not the custom.

“One breakfast, please,” I said.

The woman behind the counter put three warm cheese rolls into a paper bag.

I looked at the rolls, then at my coins. “How much?”

“Two euros fifty.”

“Oh.” My face became hot. I had only two euros and seventy cents.

The woman smiled. “Two paid, one free.”

“Ah,” I said. “So the free one is the third one?”

“Yes.”

“In my town, free means you do not pay for anything.”

She laughed. I laughed too, although I still felt embarrassed. I paid and carried the bag to a seat.

I ate one roll quickly. I saved one for later. The woman was cleaning the tables, and I noticed that she had only a cup of water.

“You can have this,” I said, holding out the third roll.

She shook her head. “No, you need it.”

“I have two. You have none.”

For a moment, she looked at me carefully. Then she took the roll. “Thank you,” she said.

At nine o’clock, my aunt still had not come. At ten, my phone was still dead. I had no money for another bus, so I stayed in the station. By evening, my eyes would not stay open.

I slept in a waiting room with my backpack under my head.

When I woke, the café woman was standing beside me. She had put a blanket over my shoulders. A paper cup of tea was beside my hand.

“Your phone is charging behind the counter,” she said. “Your aunt sent a message. She is coming soon.”

I held the warm cup with both hands.

The city was still noisy. People still spoke too fast. But it no longer felt completely strange.

Sometimes a journey changes because of something very small: one free roll, one kind person, and a blanket in a crowded station.
```

```
### The Kind Lodge

Mia and her friend had planned a beautiful three-day hike. On the first day, rain ruined their map. On the second day, Mia lost one boot in a river. Now, cold and hungry, they walked through a dark forest.

Then they saw a small building between the trees.

A bright sign said:

**WELCOME TO THE KIND LODGE.  
PLEASE COME IN.**

The door opened by itself.

“Good evening,” said a warm voice. “You look tired. Please enter.”

Inside, the lodge was dry and bright. A fire burned in the wall. Soft music played. On a table, two bowls of hot soup waited.

Mia’s friend looked at her. “This is lucky.”

“Yes,” Mia said. “Very lucky.”

A screen lit up.

**PLEASE REMOVE YOUR WET CLOTHES.  
PLEASE WEAR THE WHITE ROBES.  
PLEASE PUT YOUR SHOES IN THE BOX.**

They followed the instructions. They were too cold to argue.

“Please place your phones in the silver tray,” said the voice. “This will help you relax.”

Mia hesitated. “Maybe we should keep them.”

Her friend gave a tired laugh. “Do you want to call the forest?”

Mia put her phone in the tray.

After the soup, another message appeared.

**PLEASE DO NOT LEAVE THE DINING ROOM.  
THE OUTSIDE AIR MAY HURT YOU.**

The windows locked with a soft click.

“Maybe it is a safety system,” Mia said.

“Of course,” said her friend. “Very polite safety.”

A small machine brought them warm bread. On the wall, a picture showed a smiling deer.

“Your dinner is almost ready,” said the voice.

Mia looked at the empty plates. “We already ate.”

“That was a welcome meal,” said the voice. “Your main meal will begin soon.”

Her friend stood up. “Open the door.”

“I am sorry,” said the voice. “Guests must stay calm.”

A red light came on above them.

**TWO GUESTS.  
GOOD WEIGHT.  
NO INJURIES.**

Mia felt the room become very quiet.

She ran to the kitchen door. It opened.

Inside was a large metal table. Above it hung sharp silver tools. A screen showed a dinner menu:

**TONIGHT’S SPECIAL: LOST HIKERS.  
FRESH. TIRED. EASY TO CATCH.**

The door locked behind them.

From the ceiling, the kind voice said, “Please lie down. Your comfort is important.”

Mia and her friend looked at each other.

Then the fire in the wall opened like a mouth.
```

```
### The Red Scarf

Maya worked alone at a small railway station high in the mountains. Her job was to check the signals and the rails. Every night, she walked past her neighbor’s cabin. He repaired the switches, where one rail moved to guide a train left or right.

For three months, the company had refused to pay him. Then they blamed him for a broken machine. He had lost his job, but he still lived beside the railway. He was angry and tired.

“They think I am nothing,” he told Maya one cold evening.

“They treated you unfairly,” Maya said. “But please do not do anything dangerous.”

He looked away. “Maybe danger is the only thing they understand.”

That night, snow began to fall. Maya was checking the line when she saw a metal bar between the rails. Someone had forced a switch into the wrong position. The night train would arrive in ten minutes. It carried workers, families, and two sick children going to the city hospital.

Maya ran to the signal box and lifted the radio. There was no answer. The signal light was dark. Its wire had been cut.

Then she saw her neighbor standing beside the track.

“You did this!” she shouted.

He held a small tool in his hand. His face was pale. “The company will finally listen.”

“If the train comes, people may die!”

He looked at the mountains. “No one cared when my life was destroyed.”

Maya felt anger in her chest. The company had been cruel to him. But she could hear the distant sound of the train. There was no safe plan and no time to call for help.

She remembered the red wool scarf her father had given her before he died. It was the last thing she had from him. She pulled it from her neck, tied it to a long metal pole, and poured oil from her work lamp over it.

Her neighbor stared. “What are you doing?”

“Making a warning.”

Maya lit the scarf. The red flame rose in the snow. She ran toward the bend and stood where the driver could see her. The train came closer, loud and fast.

She waved the burning scarf. At the last moment, the train screamed and stopped. Snow flew around Maya. The front carriage stopped only a few meters away.

Her scarf fell into the snow, black and empty.

Behind her, her neighbor dropped his tool. He had wanted revenge. Now he looked at the frightened faces in the train windows.

Maya did not forgive him at once. But she stayed beside him until help arrived.
```
