### The Brass Key

Mara was paid to spend one afternoon pretending to be a stranger’s daughter.

The man was waiting outside his apartment. He wore a clean shirt, but his hands shook.

“You came,” he said. “You are late.”

“I’m Mara,” she said. “I’m from the care service.”

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he smiled.

“Yes. My daughter.”

He gave her a small brass key. A piece of blue tape was tied around it.

“We must correct a terrible mistake,” he said. “Before four o’clock.”

“Where are we going?”

“The station. Locker 47.”

On the bus, he held the key tightly.

“I left you there when you were seven,” he said. “I told you to wait. I went home, and you were gone.”

A minute later, he shook his head.

“No. That is wrong. I put your mother on the train. You were with me. She took you away.”

Then he looked frightened.

“No, no. You were never my daughter. You are my daughter today.”

Mara had been told about his early dementia. She knew his memories might be confused. Still, the same fear lived in every story.

At the station, the clock said 3:18.

Locker 47 opened with the brass key.

Inside was a yellow envelope and a child’s red shoe.

The man took the envelope. His fingers moved slowly.

“I wrote this,” he said.

He opened it.

“Dear daughter,” Mara read. “I am sorry I made you wait. I came back after dark. You were gone.”

The man began to cry.

“I came back,” he whispered. “I did come back.”

Then his face changed.

“No. I never came back. I was angry. I wanted you to be afraid.”

Mara looked at the letter. There was no date. No address. Only those words.

“What was the terrible mistake?” she asked.

He stared at the red shoe.

“I gave you the wrong key,” he said. “You opened the wrong door.”

“What door?”

He looked at Mara. For one clear second, he seemed to know exactly who she was.

“I don’t know,” he said. “But you were waiting.”

At 3:55, he pressed the brass key into her hand.

“Take it,” he said. “When you find my daughter, give it to her.”

Mara wanted to tell him she was not his daughter. Instead, she closed her fingers around the key.

On the bus home, she wondered what she had done.

Had she helped him remember the truth?

Or had she become part of the mistake?
