## Story A

### The Door Before Midnight

At a rich banker’s dinner, the guests argued about prison and death. The banker said, “A quick death is kinder than fifteen years in a small room.”

The young lawyer shook his head. “Any life is better than no life.”

“Would you live alone for fifteen years?” the banker asked.

“Yes,” said the lawyer. “And you will pay me two million rubles.”

Their foolish bet was signed that night. A small lodge stood in the banker’s garden. It had one room, a bed, books, and a piano. The lawyer could ask for food and wine by writing a note. But he could not see or speak to another person. If he left even a few hours early, he would lose the money.

On the first day, the lawyer played the piano. Then he drank wine and read light stories. After a year, he began to read serious books. He learned languages. He read history, science, and philosophy. In the last years, he read religious books. His letters became fewer. His life seemed to move far from the world outside.

The banker’s life moved in the opposite direction. He lost money. He lost his house and most of his friends. As the last night came near, he looked at the two million rubles and felt afraid.

“If the lawyer lives until midnight, I am ruined,” he thought.

Near midnight, the banker took a key and walked to the lodge. Cold rain touched his face. He opened the door. The lawyer lay at the table, thin and still. A letter waited beside his hand.

The banker read it.

The lawyer wrote that he had learned the world’s riches were empty. Money, food, books, and even life itself could not give lasting truth. He would give up the two million rubles. To show this, he would leave the lodge five hours early.

The banker stared at the sleeping man. He had come to kill him. Now shame filled his chest.

Before dawn, the lawyer opened the door and walked away into the dark garden. He did not look back.

The banker could not win the bet. But that was not what hurt most. The lawyer had escaped the room. The banker had discovered that he was the prisoner.



## Story B

### The Word in My Hand

I was angry with my teacher.

She often took my hand and made small movements in my palm. She was trying to teach me letters. But the letters had no meaning for me. They felt like a game with no prize.

That morning, she gave me a new rag doll. I held it for a while. Its soft cloth body felt nice in my hands. Then my teacher spelled a word into my palm.

I did not understand.

I pulled my hand away. I threw the doll onto the floor. My teacher picked it up and tried again. I pushed her away. Then I took the doll and pulled its arm hard.

Rip!

The arm came off.

For a moment, I felt strong. I had broken the thing that made me angry. I did not feel sorry. I dropped the doll and turned away.

My teacher took my hand. She did not hurt me, but she did not let go. She led me outside, across the yard, and into the little well-house. I felt the cool air around me. Then she placed one of my hands under the pump.

Cold water ran over my fingers.

With her other hand, she made quick movements in my free hand.

W-A-T-E-R.

The water kept running. Again, her fingers spelled the same letters.

W-A-T-E-R.

Suddenly, something opened inside my mind.

The movements were not a game. They were a name. The name for this cold, running thing was water!

I pulled my teacher toward the pump. I wanted more. She spelled P-U-M-P. Then she touched the door and spelled D-O-O-R. On the way back, she showed me a cup, a book, and my hat. Each thing had a name.

Then my hand touched the broken doll.

D-O-L-L, my teacher spelled.

I understood that name too. I felt the doll’s loose arm in my fingers. Now I felt sorry. It was not only cloth. It was my doll.

I found a piece of thread and tried to fix the arm. My first stitches were not good, but I kept trying.

That day, the world did not become quiet or bright. But it became full of names.



## Story C

### Under the Ruined Gate

The city was dying from hunger. Many houses were empty, and the streets were full of dust. The servant had worked for a rich family, but his master had no food and had dismissed him.

For two days, the servant had eaten nothing. He walked through the city in his torn clothes. He wanted to work honestly, but no one could pay him. At last, he thought, *If I do not steal, I may die.*

That evening, he reached a ruined gate. One tall tower still stood beside it. The servant entered the tower to sleep there. The room was dark and cold. Dead bodies lay on the floor. Some were covered with old cloth, but many were not.

Then he heard a sound.

The servant looked behind a broken wall. An old woman was sitting beside a dead woman. She was pulling long hairs from the dead woman’s head. Near her was a small piece of cloth.

“What are you doing?” the servant asked.

The old woman looked at him but did not stop. “I am making a wig,” she said. “Someone will buy it tomorrow. Then I can get bread.”

“You are taking hair from a dead body,” said the servant.

“I know,” said the old woman. “But what else can I do? I must live.”

She pointed at the dead woman. “And do not think she was better than me. When she was alive, she cheated hungry people. She sold them bad food and took their money. She also lied about the weight of her bread. She lived by taking from others.”

The servant looked at the dead woman. Then he looked at the old woman’s warm coat and strong shoes.

The old woman continued pulling hair. “In a famine, people do what they must. If I do not take this hair, I will die. That is all.”

The servant said nothing. A few moments earlier, he had been afraid to steal. Now he began to think in the old woman’s way.

He moved quietly behind her. Then he struck her, took her coat and shoes, and ran out of the tower.

The night swallowed him. He was still hungry, but his fear was gone.

Until that moment, he had been a poor man afraid of becoming a thief. Now he was a thief—and hunger was his excuse.



## Story D

### The Lion That Rose Again

For three nights, a lion came to our village in East Africa. It killed cattle and disappeared before morning. The villagers were afraid, so I went after it with two local hunters.

We followed its tracks through dry grass. Near sunset, we saw the lion beside a tree. It was about thirty yards away. It looked at us, but it did not move.

I raised my old rifle and fired.

The lion fell. I believed it was dead. While I began to put another bullet into the rifle, the lion suddenly stood up. In one great jump, it reached me.

I fell onto my back. The lion was on top of me. Its teeth closed around my left arm.

Something strange happened then. I was fully awake. I could see the lion’s yellow eyes and hear the hunters shouting. But I felt no pain and no fear. I was calm, almost like a man watching a dream.

The lion threw me aside and ran toward the older hunter. The hunter fired, but he missed. The lion knocked him down and turned to the younger hunter.

The younger hunter tried to run. The lion caught him too. I wanted to help, but my left arm hung uselessly, and I could not lift my rifle.

Then the lion stopped. It took a few slow steps and fell beside the younger hunter. My first bullet had gone deep inside its body. At last, the lion was dead.

The two hunters pulled me away from it. Blood covered my shirt, but only then did I begin to feel pain.

They carried us back to the village. The older hunter and the younger hunter soon recovered. My arm was much worse. The bones were broken, and the muscles were badly torn.

I lived, but my arm never became strong again. Even now, when I remember that evening, I see the lion rising after my shot.

That is why I never again believed that a quiet animal was a dead animal.



## Story E

### The Three-Day Promise

In a small kingdom, the ruler was afraid of enemies. If anyone spoke against him, his guards could take that person to prison. Often, the ruler ordered executions without a fair trial.

One day, a shepherd named Melos stood before him.

“You punish people before you know the truth,” Melos said. “That is not justice.”

The ruler’s face became hard. “Then you will die.”

Melos’s sister was going to be married in three days. He fell to his knees.

“Please let me go to her wedding,” he said. “I will return before my death.”

The ruler laughed. “Why should I believe you?”

Melos’s friend stepped forward. “I will stay here in his place.”

So the ruler agreed. Melos had three days to go and return. If he did not come back, his friend would die.

Melos ran from the city. He reached the river, but heavy rain had made the water wild. The small boat broke against a rock. Melos fell into the cold river and nearly disappeared under the water. At last, he reached the other side, shaking and weak.

He hurried to the wedding. He smiled for his sister, but his eyes stayed on the road. When the wedding ended, he left at once.

On the way back, three attackers jumped from behind some trees. They took his horse and pushed him to the ground. Melos tried to stand, but pain filled his body. He walked through the night, then fell beside the road.

The sky was becoming bright. The time was almost finished.

Melos looked toward the city. “I cannot do this,” he whispered. “Perhaps my friend is already dead.”

For a moment, he wanted to hide in the forest.

Then he remembered his friend’s calm face. His friend had trusted him enough to face death.

“No,” Melos said. “I gave my word.”

He stood and ran.

At the city gate, the guards were leading his friend to the place of execution. Then Melos shouted, “Wait!”

Everyone turned. Melos fell before the ruler.

“I promised to return,” he said. “I am here.”

The ruler looked at the two friends. Slowly, he lowered his hand.

“Free him,” he said.

After that day, the ruler demanded fair trials. He had learned that trust and loyalty could be stronger than fear.
