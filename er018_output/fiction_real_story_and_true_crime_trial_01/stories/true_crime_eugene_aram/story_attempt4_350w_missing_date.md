### The Bone Inquiry

On 8 February 1745, Eugene Aram and Richard Houseman asked Daniel Clarke to walk with them at night. They said they needed to discuss how to dispose of silver and jewels. The three men went to a place near Knaresborough.

Clarke did not return.

Aram and Houseman had murdered him. But people did not know this. They believed that Clarke had disappeared with the goods.

For the next fourteen years, Aram seemed respectable and calm. He worked as a schoolmaster in different English towns. Nothing appeared to connect him with a crime.

Then, in 1758, a labourer found a human skeleton near St. Robert’s Cave. The discovery began an inquiry by the coroner. People wanted to know who the dead person was.

Aram’s estranged wife spoke during the inquiry. She said she suspected Aram and Houseman. This made people look more closely at the two men.

Then a small moment changed the case.

Someone asked Houseman to handle one of the bones. Houseman became frightened and said, “This is no more Daniel Clarke's bone than it is mine!”

His words sounded strange. Why did he know that the bone was not Clarke’s? People wondered if he had seen Daniel Clarke’s real bones.

Houseman then confessed. He gave the exact place where Clarke had been buried. He said that the head was a little farther to the right than the first skeleton.

People dug in that place. They found a second skeleton exactly where Houseman had said.

Aram was arrested at his teaching post. He was taken to York for trial. In a long and eloquent written defence, he said that the bones did not prove that he had killed Clarke. The defence was not accepted.

Aram was convicted and executed. Before his death, he privately confessed his guilt to clergymen who attended him.

For fourteen years, Aram had lived as a respected schoolmaster while the truth lay underground. In the end, it was not a witness who broke the case. It was Houseman’s sudden fear of one bone—and his knowledge of where another body was buried.
