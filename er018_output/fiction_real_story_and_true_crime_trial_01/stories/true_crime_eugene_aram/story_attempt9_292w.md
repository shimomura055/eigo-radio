In eighteenth-century England, Daniel Clarke disappeared. People believed he had simply gone away, but they did not know the truth. Eugene Aram and Richard Houseman had persuaded Clarke to walk out with them at night. They wanted to discuss how to dispose of his silver and jewels. Clarke never returned. Aram and Houseman had murdered him.

For fourteen years, Aram remained apparently respectable. He worked as a teacher, and life continued around the missing man. No one arrested Aram or Houseman for Clarke’s death. The old crime seemed to have vanished with Clarke.

Then a labourer was digging for stone near St. Robert’s Cave. He found a human skeleton. The discovery brought the old question back. A coroner’s inquest began. Whose bones were these? Could they be Daniel Clarke’s? Suspicion soon turned to Aram and Houseman.

At the inquest, Houseman was asked to handle a bone. He exclaimed, “This is no more Daniel Clarke's bone than it is mine!” The words surprised the people who heard them. How could Houseman know that without seeing Clarke’s real bones? This strange answer made people suspect him more strongly.

Houseman later confessed. He identified the true burial place. Clarke’s head was a little farther to the right than the head of the first skeleton. People dug there. They found a second skeleton exactly where Houseman said it was.

Aram was arrested. He wrote an eloquent defence. In it, he gave his side of the case. But he was quickly convicted, despite his defence. Later, in private, he confessed his guilt to the clergymen attending him.

Eugene Aram was executed at York on 16 August 1759. For fourteen years, his respectable life had hidden the truth. Then the ground, and one strange sentence, helped reveal it.
