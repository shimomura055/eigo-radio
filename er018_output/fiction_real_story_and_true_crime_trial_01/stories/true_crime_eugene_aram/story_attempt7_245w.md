### The Second Skeleton

In Knaresborough, England, Daniel Clarke had silver and jewels. He had gathered them on credit. On the night of 8 February 1745, Eugene Aram and Richard Houseman persuaded him to go out with them. They were to discuss how to dispose of the silver and jewels.

Afterward, people assumed that Clarke had left with the goods. The murder stayed hidden. For fourteen years, Aram remained outwardly respectable as a schoolmaster. He was hiding in plain sight.

Then people uncovered a possible burial place. They found a bone and a skeleton there. But the discovery did not show clearly who the skeleton was.

Houseman was questioned about the bone. He said exactly, “This is no more Daniel Clarke’s bone than it is mine!”

This sentence made people suspicious. How could Houseman know that the bone was not Clarke’s? People wondered how he could know this without seeing the real bones.

The first skeleton did not answer the main question. Houseman then confessed that the true burial place was different. Clarke’s head lay a little farther to the right than the first skeleton found.

People dug at the place Houseman identified. They found a second skeleton exactly there.

The second discovery matched Houseman’s words. The physical evidence now connected the case to the murder of Daniel Clarke.

At the trial, Aram offered an eloquent, learned defence. But the physical evidence led to his conviction. He was executed at York on 16 August 1759.
