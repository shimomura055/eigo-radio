### The Second Skeleton

On the night of 8 February 1745, Daniel Clarke went out in Knaresborough with Eugene Aram and Richard Houseman. Clarke had gathered silver and jewels on credit. The three men were to discuss how to dispose of them.

Clarke did not return. Aram and Houseman had murdered him.

Afterward, people assumed that Clarke had left with the silver and jewels. No body was found, so there was no clear proof of a murder. The case seemed to disappear.

For fourteen years, Eugene Aram lived as a respectable schoolmaster. He stayed in plain sight, while the truth about Clarke remained hidden. Then a possible burial place was uncovered. A bone was found there, and Richard Houseman was questioned about it.

Houseman answered, “This is no more Daniel Clarke’s bone than it is mine!”

These words made people suspicious. How could Houseman know that the bone was not Clarke’s? He would need to know what Clarke’s real bones looked like—or know where Clarke was truly buried.

Houseman then confessed that the first skeleton was not Clarke. He said that Clarke’s head lay a little farther to the right.

People dug in the place Houseman identified. There they found a second skeleton. It was exactly where he had said it would be.

The discovery connected the old disappearance to Aram and Houseman. At the trial, Aram gave an eloquent and learned defence. He spoke with knowledge and skill. But the physical evidence was stronger. The court convicted him.

Eugene Aram was executed at York on 16 August 1759.
